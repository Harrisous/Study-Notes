Mamba from scratch · advanced track · lesson 01

The Problem with Sequences

Every model that reads a sequence one token at a time has to answer one question before it answers anything else: what do I keep? This lesson shows why that question has exactly two clean answers, works out the cost of each in arithmetic, in bytes, and in wall-clock time, and then shows what has actually been proved about the things each answer can never do. Nothing about state space models appears until the very end, because the problem has to hurt before the fix means anything.

Who this is for

You are comfortable with linear algebra and calculus and you have trained a neural network, but the sequence modelling vocabulary is not yet second nature. Terms like autoregressive factorization, spectral norm, arithmetic intensity and uniform TC0 get explained where they are first needed, in amber boxes you can skip if they are old news. Nothing is assumed about state space models, RNNs, or Transformer internals.

The one sentence version

A sequence model is a bet about what to forget. Recurrent models bet on a fixed-size summary and pay for it in information; attention bets on keeping everything and pays for it in memory bandwidth. The interesting question, and the subject of this course, is whether a model can make that bet adaptively instead of once at design time.

Section 1

The task, written down properly

Before arguing about architectures it is worth being exact about the job. The job turns out to impose the constraint that the rest of the lesson is about, and it does so before any neural network is mentioned.

1.1 Sequences, tokens, and the map we want

Fix a finite vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\) of size \(V\) - a list of the symbols the model is allowed to see and emit. For text these are subword tokens: " transform", er, ". and so on. A sequence is a finite string \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_L)\) with each \(x_t \in \mathcal{V}\), and \(L\) is its length. The subscript \(t\) is a position, and by convention it also stands for time: position 1 is read first.

The object we want is a probability distribution over sequences, \(p(x_1, \dots, x_L)\). That single object answers everything else - the most likely continuation, the plausibility of a document, the entropy of the language - so getting it is the whole game. The trouble is its size. A distribution over all sequences of length \(L\) has \(V^L - 1\) free parameters. At the modest values \(V = 50{,}000\) and \(L = 1000\) that is more numbers than there are atoms in the observable universe, by an absurd margin. So we do not represent it directly.

Prerequisite: the chain rule of probability skip if you have written \(p(a,b)=p(a)p(b\mid a)\) before

For any two events, \(p(a, b) = p(a)\,p(b \mid a)\): the probability that both happen is the probability that the first happens times the probability that the second happens given the first. It is a rearrangement of the definition of conditional probability, \(p(b \mid a) = p(a,b)/p(a)\), so it is an identity, not an assumption. Applying it repeatedly peels one variable off at a time, and that is the only fact used in the next equation.

Apply the chain rule \(L-1\) times, always peeling the last position off the front:

Autoregressive factorization \[ p(x_1,\dots,x_L) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{L} p\!\left(x_t \mid x_{1},\dots,x_{t-1}\right) \] Exact, for any distribution over sequences, with no modelling assumption whatsoever. The convention \(p(x_1 \mid \varnothing) = p(x_1)\) handles the first factor. (1)

Equation (1) is the reason language models look the way they do. It converts one impossible object into \(L\) merely difficult ones: instead of a distribution over sequences we need a single function that, given any prefix, returns a distribution over the next token. Write that function as

What a sequence model is \[ f_\theta : \mathcal{V}^{\,t-1} \;\longrightarrow\; \Delta^{V-1}, \qquad f_\theta(x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}) \;=\; \hat p(\,\cdot \mid x_{\lt t}) \] \(\Delta^{V-1}\) is the probability simplex in \(\mathbb{R}^V\): vectors with non-negative entries summing to one. \(\theta\) is the parameter vector. \(x_{\lt t}\) abbreviates \(x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}\). (2)

Two properties of (2) are worth naming because they are the source of all the difficulty.

It is causal. The prediction at \(t\) may look at positions \(1\) through \(t-1\) and no further. This is not a stylistic choice; it is forced by (1), since \(p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\) is a conditional on the past. Any mechanism that peeks forward makes the factorization invalid and the resulting number is not a probability of anything.

Its domain grows. The input to \(f_\theta\) at position \(t\) is longer than the input at position \(t-1\). A model with a fixed number of parameters must therefore handle inputs of unbounded size, and the only way to do that is to reuse machinery across positions. How the reuse is arranged is, in the end, the entire architectural question.

1.2 How the model is scored

Given a corpus, we fit \(\theta\) by maximum likelihood: make the observed sequences as probable as possible. Taking a logarithm turns the product in (1) into a sum, which is both numerically safer and easier to differentiate, and negating turns maximization into minimization.

Training objective \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L} \log \hat p_\theta\!\left(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}\right) \] The average negative log-likelihood per token, in nats when the logarithm is natural. This is exactly the cross-entropy between the one-hot empirical distribution at each position and the model's predicted distribution. (3)
Prerequisite: cross-entropy and why (3) is one skip if you can derive \(H(p,q)=-\sum p\log q\)

The cross-entropy of a predicted distribution \(q\) relative to a true distribution \(p\) over the same finite set is \(H(p,q) = -\sum_{v} p(v) \log q(v)\). It measures the average number of nats needed to encode samples from \(p\) using a code designed for \(q\); it is minimized, at the entropy \(H(p)\), exactly when \(q = p\).

At a single position the "true" distribution in a training corpus is the observed token itself: \(p\) puts all its mass on \(x_t\) and none anywhere else. Then \(-\sum_v p(v)\log q(v)\) collapses to the single surviving term \(-\log q(x_t)\). Summing over positions and dividing by \(L\) gives (3). So "negative log-likelihood", "cross-entropy loss", and "average surprisal" name the same number here.

The number people actually quote is perplexity, the exponential of (3):

Perplexity \[ \mathrm{PPL} \;=\; \exp\!\big(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\big) \;=\; \left(\prod_{t=1}^{L}\hat p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\right)^{-1/L} \] The reciprocal geometric mean of the probabilities the model assigned to the tokens that actually occurred. Read it as an effective branching factor: a perplexity of 20 means the model is about as uncertain as someone choosing uniformly among 20 options at every step. (4)

Two consequences that matter later. First, perplexity is multiplicative in probability but additive in loss, so a loss improvement of \(0.05\) nats is a perplexity ratio of \(e^{0.05} \approx 1.05\) regardless of the starting point - which is why small loss deltas are taken seriously. Second, and more important for this lesson, perplexity is an average over positions. A model can be excellent on average while being catastrophically wrong on the rare token that requires reaching back 5000 positions, because that token is one term in a sum of thousands. We will see in §6 that this is not a hypothetical: architectures with clearly different retrieval ability can be separated by benchmark tasks while being nearly tied, or even inverted, on perplexity.

Common error

"Lower perplexity means a better model." Lower perplexity means a better average next-token predictor on that corpus. In Jelassi et al.'s comparison the Mamba models had lower perplexity on the Pile than the Pythia Transformers of the same size, and still lost badly at retrieving a phone number from context.15 Perplexity and long-range retrieval are different measurements and can move in opposite directions.

1.3 The axis that organizes everything

Nothing so far mentions neural networks, and that is the point. Equation (2) already forces the central design decision. At position \(t\) the model must produce a distribution from a prefix of length \(t-1\). It can either

  1. keep the prefix and look at it, in which case whatever it stores grows as the prefix grows; or
  2. keep a summary of bounded size, in which case as \(t\) grows the summary must start throwing information away.

There is no third option, and §2 makes that claim precise. The two branches have names - attention and recurrence - but the branch point is upstream of both, sitting in the shape of the problem itself. Everything else in this lesson is bookkeeping on the consequences: what each branch costs in arithmetic (§3, §4), what it costs in memory traffic on real silicon (§5), and what each branch provably cannot do (§6).

Section 1 in three lines

The chain rule turns modelling a distribution over sequences into modelling one conditional, applied \(L\) times. That conditional is causal and its input grows without bound, so the model must reuse machinery across positions.

The loss is the average per-token cross-entropy and its exponential is perplexity. Because it averages over positions, it is systematically insensitive to failures on the small minority of tokens that need long-range information - the exact failures the rest of this lesson is about.

Section 2

The state, and why there are only two answers

"State" is used loosely in a lot of writing about this subject. Pinning it down turns a vague intuition about memory into a statement about bytes, and makes the dichotomy of §1.3 something you can argue about rather than something you have to accept.

2.1 What a state is

Definition · state

A model processes a sequence with state if there is a set \(\mathcal{S}\), an initial element \(h_0 \in \mathcal{S}\), an update \(\varphi: \mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{S}\), and a readout \(g: \mathcal{S}\to\Delta^{V-1}\), such that

\[ h_t = \varphi(h_{t-1}, x_t), \qquad \hat p(\cdot \mid x_{\le t}) = g(h_t). \]

\(h_t\) is the state at position \(t\) and \(\mathcal{S}\) is the state space. The state size is \(\log_2|\mathcal{S}|\) bits, or for \(\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^N\) stored in \(b\)-bit floating point, \(Nb\) bits.

The definition says something narrow and strong: the state is a bottleneck. Whatever the model will ever do with the prefix \(x_1,\dots,x_t\) has to pass through \(h_t\). If two different prefixes produce the same state, the model's behaviour after them is identical forever after - it has no way to tell them apart, because the prefix itself is gone.

Prerequisite: sufficient statistic skip if the word is familiar

In statistics, a function \(T(X)\) of a sample is a sufficient statistic for a quantity if the sample tells you nothing more about that quantity than \(T(X)\) already does. The sample mean is sufficient for the mean of a Gaussian with known variance: once you know it, the individual observations are irrelevant.

A state is an attempt at a sufficient statistic for the future: an ideal \(h_t\) would preserve everything about \(x_{\le t}\) that is predictive of \(x_{\gt t}\) and discard everything else. The word "ideal" is doing real work. When \(\mathcal{S}\) is small, a sufficient statistic may not exist at all, and then the state is a lossy compression chosen by training rather than by principle.

2.2 The two answers

Now the dichotomy is easy to state. It depends only on how \(|\mathcal{S}|\) relates to \(L\).

Definition · the two families

A fixed-state (recurrent) model has \(|\mathcal{S}|\) independent of \(L\). Cost per token is \(O(1)\) in both time and memory; total cost is \(O(L)\).

A growing-state (attention-style) model has \(|\mathcal{S}|\) scaling with \(L\), in practice \(\Theta(L)\), because it stores a bounded-size record per position. Cost per token is \(O(L)\); total cost is \(O(L^2)\).

Calling attention a state model is not a rhetorical trick. The key-value cache is \(h_t\) in the sense of the definition: \(\varphi\) appends a new key-value pair, and \(g\) computes an attention-weighted readout. The only unusual feature is that \(\varphi\) never overwrites anything, which is precisely why \(\mathcal{S}\) has to grow. Jelassi et al. make exactly this framing explicit, distinguishing input-independent memory (parameters) from input-dependent memory (activations), and noting that it is the second that their lower bound constrains.15

Where the notebook analogy breaks

A notebook suggests deliberate note-taking - you decide what is worth writing down. A vanilla RNN does not decide anything: it applies the same \(\varphi\) to every token regardless of content, so the "notes" are taken by a fixed rule. The analogy is accurate about capacity and wrong about agency. Repairing exactly that gap is what "selection" means in Mamba, and it is the subject of lesson 08. Keep the notebook picture, but remember that in a classical RNN the pen moves the same way no matter what the token says.

Section 2 in three lines

A state is a bottleneck that the prefix must pass through, and its size in bits is the honest measure of how much of the past a model can hold.

Fixed-size state buys \(O(1)\) cost per token and pays in information loss. Growing state buys exact recall and pays in memory that scales with the context. The key-value cache is a state in exactly this sense - one that never overwrites, which is why it grows.

Section 3

The recurrent answer: cheap, lossy, unparallelizable

Three separate problems get bundled together under "RNNs do not work". They have different causes and different fixes, and only one of them was ever really solved. Separating them is the most useful thing this section does, because Mamba fixes a different one than most people assume.

3.1 The Elman network

The simplest instance of Definition 2.1 with \(\mathcal{S} = \mathbb{R}^N\) is the network Elman described in 19902 (Jordan's 1986 variant fed back the output instead of the hidden state). The equation below is the modern formalization, not a quotation: Elman's paper contains no recurrence equation at all. The architecture is given entirely in prose and figure captions - context units that "are also hidden in the sense that they interact exclusively with other nodes internal to the network", copied from the hidden layer "on a one-for-one basis, with fixed weight of 1.0". Worth knowing before attributing notation to him.

Definition · Elman recurrent network \[ h_t \;=\; \sigma\!\left(W_h\,h_{t-1} + W_x\,e(x_t) + b\right), \qquad \hat p(\cdot \mid x_{\le t}) \;=\; \softmax\!\left(W_y h_t + c\right) \]

with \(h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N}\) the hidden state, \(e(x_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) the embedding of token \(x_t\), \(W_h \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\) the recurrent weight matrix, \(W_x \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\), \(W_y \in \mathbb{R}^{V\times N}\), biases \(b\in\mathbb{R}^N\), \(c\in\mathbb{R}^V\), and \(\sigma\) an elementwise nonlinearity, classically \(\tanh\). \(h_0\) is a learned or zero vector.

Everything good about this design is visible immediately. The parameter count does not depend on \(L\). Advancing one step costs one \(N\times N\) matrix-vector product plus one \(N \times d\) one, so \(O(N^2 + Nd)\) arithmetic and \(O(N)\) memory, whatever \(L\) is. Generating token one million costs exactly what generating token three cost. In the vocabulary of §2 this is the fixed-state column of the ledger, and on the two rows that measure cost it wins outright.

Everything bad about it is also already present, just not yet visible. We take the three problems in order of how much they matter, which is roughly the reverse of the order in which the field discovered them.

3.2 Problem one: the gradient. Backpropagation through time, derived

To train the network we need \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta\) for every parameter. The subtlety is that \(W_h\) is used at every step, so it influences the loss at position \(t\) through every intermediate state. The standard treatment - unroll the recurrence into a feedforward network \(L\) layers deep with tied weights, then backpropagate - is backpropagation through time (BPTT), named and analysed by Werbos.6

Prerequisite: Jacobians and the multivariate chain rule skip if \(\partial f/\partial x\) for vector \(f\) is routine

For a map \(f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\), the Jacobian \(\partial f/\partial x\) is the \(m\times n\) matrix whose \((i,j)\) entry is \(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\). It is the best linear approximation of \(f\) near \(x\): \(f(x+\delta) \approx f(x) + (\partial f/\partial x)\delta\).

The chain rule composes Jacobians by matrix multiplication: if \(z = g(y)\) and \(y = f(x)\) then \(\partial z/\partial x = (\partial z/\partial y)(\partial y/\partial x)\). Chaining \(n\) maps gives a product of \(n\) matrices, and that single fact is the whole of §3.3.

One notational convention, borrowed from Pascanu et al.: \(\partial^{+} h_k/\partial\theta\) is the immediate derivative, treating \(h_{k-1}\) as a constant. It isolates "how \(\theta\) enters at step \(k\)" from "how \(\theta\) already changed the state that arrived at step \(k\)".

Derivation · the gradient of a recurrent network
1\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial\theta} = \sum_{1\le t\le T}\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_t}{\partial\theta}\) the loss is a sum over positions, so the gradient is too
2\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_t}{\partial\theta} = \sum_{1\le k\le t}\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_t}{\partial h_t}\,\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k}\,\frac{\partial^{+} h_k}{\partial\theta}\) \(\theta\) acts at every step \(k \le t\); sum those contributions
3\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k} = \prod_{t \ge i \gt k}\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}}\) chain rule across the \(t-k\) intervening steps
4\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} = W_h^{\!\top}\,\diag\!\big(\sigma'(h_{i-1})\big)\) differentiate the definition; \(\diag\) puts a vector on a diagonal
5\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k} = \prod_{t \ge i \gt k} W_h^{\!\top}\,\diag\!\big(\sigma'(h_{i-1})\big)\) substitute 4 into 3; this product is the whole problem

Steps 1 to 5 are equations (3), (4) and (5) of Pascanu et al. 2013.7 Line 2 is worth pausing on. The term with \(k = t\) is the ordinary, local, feedforward-looking gradient: it is healthy. The terms with \(k \ll t\) are the long-term contributions, and they are the only route by which the network can learn that something at position \(k\) mattered for position \(t\). Their size is governed entirely by the matrix product in line 5.

3.3 Why a product of Jacobians is dangerous

A product of \(t-k\) real numbers each slightly below 1 collapses to zero; each slightly above 1 blows up. The same is true of matrices, once you decide how to measure a matrix's size.

Prerequisite: operator norm, singular values, spectral radius skip if \(\lVert A\rVert_2 = \sigma_{\max}(A)\) is familiar

The operator 2-norm \(\lVert A\rVert_2 = \max_{\lVert v\rVert_2 = 1}\lVert Av\rVert_2\) is the largest factor by which \(A\) can stretch any vector. It equals \(A\)'s largest singular value \(\sigma_{\max}(A)\). Two properties are used below and nothing else is:

  • Submultiplicativity: \(\lVert AB\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert\,\lVert B\rVert\). Stretching twice cannot exceed the product of the individual stretches. This is where the exponential comes from.
  • For a diagonal matrix, the norm is the largest absolute entry.

The spectral radius \(\rho(A)\) is the largest \(|\lambda|\) over eigenvalues. It governs the asymptotics of \(A^n\), but it is not a norm and is not submultiplicative, which is exactly why the careful statement below is in terms of singular values rather than eigenvalues.

Derivation · the exponential bound
1\(\big\lVert \diag(\sigma'(h))\big\rVert \le \gamma\) assumption: \(\sigma'\) is bounded. \(\gamma = 1\) for \(\tanh\), \(\gamma = \tfrac14\) for the logistic sigmoid
2\(\displaystyle \Big\lVert \frac{\partial h_{k+1}}{\partial h_k}\Big\rVert \le \big\lVert W_h^{\!\top}\big\rVert\,\big\lVert\diag(\sigma'(h_k))\big\rVert \le \lambda_1\gamma\) submultiplicativity, with \(\lambda_1\) the largest singular value of \(W_h\)
3\(\lambda_1 \lt 1/\gamma \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda_1\gamma =: \eta \lt 1\) definition of the regime we are studying
4\(\displaystyle \Big\lVert \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k}\Big\rVert \le \eta^{\,t-k}\) apply 2 to each of the \(t-k\) factors, by induction
5\(\displaystyle \Big\lVert \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_t}{\partial h_t}\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k}\Big\rVert \le \eta^{\,t-k}\Big\lVert\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_t}{\partial h_t}\Big\rVert \;\xrightarrow[\;t-k\to\infty\;]{}\; 0\) long-term contributions decay geometrically in the gap
The vanishing-gradient condition · Pascanu et al. 2013, §2.1

In the linear case it is sufficient that \(\rho \lt 1\), where \(\rho\) is the spectral radius of \(W_h\), for the long-term components to vanish as \(t\to\infty\), and necessary that \(\rho \gt 1\) for them to explode. Generalizing to nonlinear \(\sigma\) with \(\lVert\diag(\sigma'(h_k))\rVert \le \gamma\), the authors prove that it is sufficient for

\[ \lambda_1 \;\lt\; \frac{1}{\gamma} \]

where \(\lambda_1\) is the largest singular value of \(W_h\), for the vanishing-gradient problem to occur; and necessary that \(\lambda_1 \gt 1/\gamma\) for gradients to explode. For \(\tanh\), \(\gamma=1\); for the sigmoid, \(\gamma = 1/4\), so a sigmoid network tolerates singular values up to 4 before exploding becomes possible.7

Common error

Many secondary accounts, and the arXiv preprint of this very paper, state the nonlinear condition in terms of the largest eigenvalue of \(W_h\). The published ICML version says largest singular value, and that is the correct reading: the proof runs through \(\lVert W_h^{\!\top}\rVert_2 = \sigma_{\max}(W_h)\) and submultiplicativity, which eigenvalues do not supply for a non-symmetric matrix. The revision is not a slip of one word either - the phrase "singular value" occurs zero times in both arXiv versions and four times in the PMLR paper, which changed three separate sentences and renamed the linear-case quantity to \(\rho\) to stop it colliding with the nonlinear \(\lambda_1\). That collision is the likely origin of the whole confusion.

Cite the PMLR version. Note also that this is prose in §2.1 with the proof deferred to supplementary material: the paper contains no numbered theorems at all, so "Theorem 1 of Pascanu et al." refers to nothing.

Three things about this result are routinely overstated, so state them exactly.

  1. It bounds a bound. Line 4 gives an upper bound on a norm. A norm going to zero does force the contribution to zero, so vanishing is genuinely implied; but exploding is only permitted by \(\lambda_1 \gt 1/\gamma\), not caused by it. Hence "necessary" in the theorem, not "sufficient".
  2. It is about learning, not about carrying. Nothing here says the forward pass cannot transport information 100 steps. It says the gradient signal that would teach it to do so is unrepresentably small. The information channel and the credit-assignment channel are different channels, and it is the second that breaks.
  3. The fix is not "just set \(\lambda_1 = 1\)". Pascanu et al. point out that an \(L_1/L_2\) penalty keeping \(\lambda_1 \lt 1\) does prevent explosion, but "limits the model to single point attractor at the origin, where any information inserted in the model dies out exponentially fast". Their proposed remedy for explosion is the blunt and effective one: gradient norm clipping.

Point 3 hints at something deeper that Bengio, Simard and Frasconi had already established in 1994. For a recurrent network to hold information robustly against noise, its state map has to be contractive around the stored configuration - and contraction is exactly the condition that kills the gradient. Their introduction states it without hedging: "either such a system is stable and resistant to noise or, alternatively, it is efficiently trainable by gradient descent, but not both."3

Read the hypothesis, not just the headline

Their conclusion begins "Assuming hyperbolic attractors are used to store state information" - that is, assuming the memory is held at an attractor whose Jacobian has all eigenvalues below 1 in modulus (their Definition 2). The theorem is conditional on that storage mechanism, and the conditional is the whole opening. A recurrence that stores information on a path with no contraction escapes the dilemma entirely. That is precisely what §3.4 is about, and it is a good habit to acquire now: an impossibility result is only as broad as the hypothesis it assumes.

3.4 What gates actually fixed

The LSTM4 attacks the product in line 5 directly by giving the state a path with no matrix and no nonlinearity on it. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber pose the design problem as a one-line requirement, in their §3.2.1: to keep error flowing unchanged through a self-connected unit \(j\), we need \(f_j'(\mathrm{net}_j)\,w_{jj} = 1.0\). Integrating that condition forces \(f_j\) to be linear, and they take \(f_j = \mathrm{id}\) with \(w_{jj} = 1.0\). They name the result the constant error carousel, and §3.2.2 says it "will be LSTM's central feature".

The forget gate is not in the 1997 paper

The 1997 LSTM has the carousel plus an input gate and an output gate; the cell's self-connection is fixed at exactly 1.0, so the cell never decays at all. The forget gate - and with it equation (5) below, the version everyone now draws - arrives three years later in Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins, Learning to Forget.5 The distinction matters for this course, because the object Mamba generalizes is the forget gate, and the whole point of a forget gate is that the retention factor is learned per token rather than pinned at 1.

The additive path, with forgetting \[ c_t \;=\; f_t \odot c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \odot g_t, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{\partial c_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} = \diag(f_t) \] \(f_t, i_t \in (0,1)^N\) are the forget and input gates, each a sigmoid of a learned affine function of \((h_{t-1}, x_t)\); \(g_t\) is the candidate update. The Jacobian along the cell path is diagonal, so the product across steps is a product of scalars per channel, \(\prod_i f_i\), with no \(W_h^{\!\top}\) in sight. (5)

Now the decay of channel \(n\) over \(t-k\) steps is \(\prod_{i} (f_i)_n\). If the network learns \(f \approx 1\) on a channel, that channel's gradient is transported essentially undamped. The decay rate has become a learned, input-dependent, per-channel quantity instead of a global property of one shared matrix - and, looking back at the box in §3.3, it is a storage path with no contraction on it, which is exactly the hypothesis Bengio et al. assumed away.

Mamba's discretized recurrence is \(h_t = \bar{A}_t h_{t-1} + \bar{B}_t x_t\) with \(\bar{A}_t = \exp(\Delta_t A)\), and \(\Delta_t\) is a function of \(x_t\). That is a forget gate written in the language of continuous-time systems: an input-dependent, per-channel decay multiplying the previous state. The lineage from (5) is direct, and lesson 08 makes the equivalence exact for the one-dimensional case.

Gates were enough for LSTMs to dominate sequence modelling for roughly twenty years. But notice what they did not touch. \(f_t \in (0,1)\) still, so retention still decays, only now at a rate the model picked. And \(\mathcal{S}\) is still \(\mathbb{R}^N\) with \(N\) fixed, so capacity is untouched. Two of the three problems remain, and the one that finished RNNs off is the one nobody had been worrying about.

3.5 Problem two: the recurrence cannot be parallelized, and this is what actually killed it

Look again at \(h_t = \varphi(h_{t-1}, x_t)\). Computing \(h_t\) requires \(h_{t-1}\) first. The dependency chain has length \(L\), so no matter how many processors you have, a forward pass takes \(L\) sequential steps. Vaswani et al. tabulate exactly this quantity and call it sequential operations.1

Why the middle column is worth more than the left one

A modern accelerator delivers its throughput only on large, dense, independent work. A recurrent step at \(N = 2048\) is a single matrix-vector product: about \(2N^2 = 8.4\) MFLOP, which an A100 could execute in roughly 27 nanoseconds at peak. It will not: a dependent kernel of that size is dominated by launch and memory latency, on the order of microseconds. Even at an optimistic 2µs per step, a 8192-token sequence costs \(8192 \times 2\,\mu s \approx 16\) ms per layer, and 32 layers is half a second per forward pass. The sequence length multiplies latency and no amount of batching removes it, because batching widens each step without shortening the chain.

Attention replaces those 8192 dependent tiny operations with two large matrix multiplications that saturate the machine. Even at a much worse FLOP count, it finishes far sooner. This is the single most important thing to understand about why the field moved, and it is a statement about hardware, not about modelling - which is why §5 exists.

3.6 Problem three: the state is finite

Suppose gradients were perfect and training were instantaneous. A model whose state is \(N\) numbers in \(b\)-bit floating point holds \(Nb\) bits, full stop. Any task requiring more than \(Nb\) bits of the prefix to be recoverable is impossible for it, at any parameter count, with any training budget. That is not an optimization difficulty; it is counting.

This third problem is the only one of the three that is irreducible. Gates addressed problem one; the parallel scan of lesson 09 addresses problem two; nothing addresses problem three, because it is a property of the design rather than a defect in it. §6 turns it into a theorem and then, more usefully, shows that in practice models fail four orders of magnitude before the theorem says they must.

Section 3 in four lines

Problem one, gradients. BPTT multiplies \(t-k\) Jacobians, so long-range credit decays like \(\eta^{\,t-k}\). Sufficient condition for vanishing: largest singular value of \(W_h\) below \(1/\gamma\). Gates fix this by making the Jacobian diagonal and its entries learned.

Problem two, parallelism. The chain has depth \(L\) and no hardware fixes that. This, not gradients, is why the Transformer replaced the LSTM.

Problem three, capacity. \(Nb\) bits is \(Nb\) bits. Unfixable by construction, and the price of admission for everything else being cheap.

Section 4

The attention answer: exact, parallel, quadratic

Attention solves problems one, two and three of §3 in one stroke, by refusing to compress. This section derives it from the requirement rather than presenting it, works out where the mysterious \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) comes from, and then prices the bill - in FLOPs during training and in bytes during generation, which turn out to be two completely different stories.

4.1 The requirement, and the mechanism it forces

Suppose we insist on exact access to any earlier position. Then position \(t\) must be able to select among \(x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}\) based on content rather than on position, because which earlier token is relevant depends on what the tokens say. So we need a differentiable, content-addressable lookup: a soft dictionary.

A dictionary has keys and values, and a lookup supplies a query. Give every position all three, as learned linear functions of its representation. Then "how much does position \(t\) want position \(s\)" is a similarity between \(q_t\) and \(k_s\), and the answer is a weighted average of the \(v_s\). Choosing the dot product for similarity and the softmax for turning scores into weights gives attention, and nothing else about it is arbitrary.

Prerequisite: dot products and softmax skip if both are old friends

The dot product \(q^\top k = \sum_i q_i k_i = \lVert q\rVert\lVert k\rVert\cos\vartheta\) is large and positive when two vectors point the same way, and it is the cheapest similarity there is: one fused multiply-add per dimension, and a whole matrix of them is one matrix multiplication, which is the operation GPUs are built for.

The softmax turns a score vector \(z\in\mathbb{R}^n\) into a distribution: \(\softmax(z)_i = e^{z_i}\big/\sum_j e^{z_j}\). It is shift-invariant, \(\softmax(z + c\mathbf{1}) = \softmax(z)\), which is what lets implementations subtract \(\max_j z_j\) for numerical safety. Its Jacobian is

\[ \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = p_i\left(\delta_{ij} - p_j\right) \]

and that formula is about to earn its keep: as \(p\) approaches a one-hot vector, every entry of the Jacobian approaches zero, so a saturated softmax passes no gradient.

Definition · scaled dot-product attention, causal

Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d}\) hold the layer input, one row per position, and let \(W_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}\), \(W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_v}\). Put \(Q = XW_Q\), \(K = XW_K\), \(V = XW_V\). Then

\[ \operatorname{Attn}(Q,K,V) \;=\; \softmax\!\left(\frac{QK^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right)V, \qquad M_{ts} = \begin{cases}0 & s \le t\\ -\infty & s \gt t\end{cases} \]

The softmax is row-wise. \(M\) is the causal mask, which enforces the causality requirement of §1.1 by sending forbidden weights to \(e^{-\infty} = 0\). Row \(t\) of the output is \(\sum_{s\le t} \alpha_{ts} v_s\) with \(\alpha_{t\cdot}\) a probability distribution over the prefix: a weighted average of past values, with the weights read off the content.

4.2 Where \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) comes from

This factor is usually presented as a normalization trick. It is a variance calculation, and the paper gives it in a footnote.1

Prerequisite: variance of a sum skip if \(\operatorname{Var}(\sum X_i)=\sum\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\) for independent \(X_i\) is automatic

For independent \(X_1,\dots,X_n\), expectation is linear and variances add: \(\operatorname{Var}(\sum_i X_i) = \sum_i \operatorname{Var}(X_i)\). For independent \(X, Y\) with mean 0 and variance 1, the product \(XY\) has mean \(\E[X]\E[Y] = 0\) and variance \(\E[X^2Y^2] - 0 = \E[X^2]\E[Y^2] = 1\). Both facts are used once each below.

Derivation · the scale factor
1\(q_i, k_i\) independent, \(\E[q_i]=\E[k_i]=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}=1\) the paper's stated assumption; roughly what standard initialization gives
2\(\E[q^\top k] = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k}\E[q_i]\E[k_i] = 0\) linearity and independence
3\(\operatorname{Var}(q^\top k) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k}\operatorname{Var}(q_ik_i) = d_k\) variances add; each product has unit variance
4\(\operatorname{sd}(q^\top k) = \sqrt{d_k}\;\;\big(= 11.31\text{ at } d_k=128\big)\) so logits live on a scale that grows with head width
5\(\operatorname{Var}\!\big(q^\top k/\sqrt{d_k}\big) = 1\) divide by the standard deviation and the scale is fixed for every \(d_k\)
Vaswani et al. 2017, footnote 4, verbatim

"To illustrate why the dot products get large, assume that the components of \(q\) and \(k\) are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1. Then their dot product, \(q\cdot k=\sum_{i=1}^{d_k}q_i k_i\), has mean 0 and variance \(d_k\)." The main text adds the reason it matters: for large \(d_k\) the dot products "grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax function into regions where it has extremely small gradients".

A small piece of history that is easy to miss: this footnote is not in arXiv v1. The first posting said only that the dot products grow "too large in magnitude to result in useful gradients"; the variance argument was added a week later in v2. The scale factor was shipped first and explained afterwards.1

Chain that with the softmax Jacobian from the prerequisite box and the failure mode is fully explained. Logits of standard deviation \(11.31\) put one weight near 1 and the rest near 0; then \(p_i(\delta_{ij}-p_j) \approx 0\) everywhere; then no gradient reaches \(W_Q\) or \(W_K\); then attention never learns where to look. The model is not wrong, it is stuck. Figure 4.1 measures the saturation directly.

4.3 Why it parallelizes

In the definition, \(Q\), \(K\) and \(V\) are computed from \(X\) by three matrix multiplications with no reference to any \(h_{t-1}\). The score matrix is one more matrix multiplication. Nothing in the layer depends on the layer's own output at an earlier position, so all \(L\) positions are computed at once. That is the \(O(1)\) in the sequential-operations column of Table 1, and it is the whole reason the architecture scaled.

The asymmetry that gets forgotten

The \(O(1)\) sequential-operations claim is about training, where the entire target sequence is known and can be pushed through under a causal mask - teacher forcing. At generation time token \(t+1\) cannot be computed before token \(t\) exists, so decoding is inherently \(L\) sequential steps for every architecture. Attention's parallelism advantage is a training-time advantage only. Its inference behaviour, as §4.5 shows, is strictly worse than a recurrent model's. Every hybrid architecture in the field exists because of this asymmetry.

4.4 The training bill, in FLOPs

Count the arithmetic in one causal self-attention layer of a Transformer with \(h\) heads, head width \(d_k = d/h\), and the standard \(4d\) feedforward width. A matrix product of \(m\times n\) by \(n\times p\) costs \(2mnp\) floating-point operations, one multiply and one add per term.

Common error

"Attention is \(O(L^2)\), so it is the bottleneck." At the context lengths where most training happens it is a minority of the FLOPs, and the real inference bottleneck is not FLOPs at all - it is the bytes of §4.5 moved at the bandwidths of §5. Quoting the exponent without the constant and without the hardware gets the engineering conclusion wrong in both directions.

Attention is a differentiable content-addressable lookup. The \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) is a variance normalization without which the softmax saturates and stops passing gradient - measurably so by \(d_k = 128\).

Section 5

The hardware underneath, where the asymptotics lie

Complexity classes assume every operation costs the same. On a GPU they differ by three orders of magnitude depending on where the operand lives. Once that is priced in, several conclusions from §3 and §4 change sign, including the most famous one.

5.1 Two memories, not one

A GPU does not have "memory". It has a hierarchy, and the levels differ far more in bandwidth than in any quantity a complexity analysis tracks. Dao et al. give the A100 figures in one sentence: it has "40-80GB of high bandwidth memory (HBM) with bandwidth 1.5-2.0TB/s and 192KB of on-chip SRAM per each of 108 streaming multiprocessors with bandwidth estimated around 19TB/s".10 That is \(108 \times 192\,\text{KB} = 20.7\) MB of SRAM, about 2000 times smaller than HBM and about 10 times faster.

5.2 Arithmetic intensity and the roofline

Prerequisite: arithmetic intensity, and a naming trap skip if you have used a roofline plot

The quantity is operations divided by bytes moved, and it is a property of an algorithm on a machine, not of either alone: the same matrix multiplication has different intensity depending on how well it is tiled.

The name is where people go wrong, including in secondary accounts of the paper below. Williams, Waterman and Patterson coined operational intensity, "operations per byte of DRAM traffic", and they explicitly declined to call it arithmetic intensity: that older term, which they attribute to the GPU graphics literature, measures traffic between the processor and cache, whereas the roofline needs traffic between cache and DRAM.11 The machine-learning systems literature then adopted "arithmetic intensity" anyway for the cache-to-DRAM quantity: FlashAttention defines it as "the number of arithmetic operations per byte of memory access", citing Roofline.10 This lesson follows the FlashAttention usage, because that is the vocabulary the rest of the field uses.

The roofline follows in one line. If a kernel performs \(F\) FLOPs and moves \(Q\) bytes, its runtime is at least \(\max(F/\pi,\; Q/\beta)\) where \(\pi\) is peak throughput and \(\beta\) is bandwidth. So attainable throughput is \(\min(\pi,\; \beta \cdot I)\) with \(I = F/Q\) the intensity. Plotted against \(I\) it is a rising line that flattens: a slanted bandwidth roof and a flat compute ceiling.

The corner where the two roofs meet is the ridge point, \(I^\star = \pi/\beta\). To the left of it you are memory-bound and adding FLOPs is free; to the right you are compute-bound and saving memory traffic buys nothing. For an A100 80GB at bf16, \(\pi = 312\) TFLOP/s and \(\beta = 2.039\) TB/s, so

Ridge point of one A100 80GB \[ I^\star \;=\; \frac{312 \times 10^{12}\ \text{FLOP/s}}{2.039\times10^{12}\ \text{byte/s}} \;=\; 153\ \text{FLOP per byte} \] An algorithm must do 153 floating-point operations on every byte it reads to keep the machine busy. Reading one bf16 number and doing one multiply-add on it has intensity \(2/2 = 1\), which is 153 times too low. (7)

5.3 FlashAttention: the same FLOPs, a tenth of the traffic

The textbook attention implementation writes the \(L\times L\) score matrix to HBM, reads it back for the softmax, writes the result, and reads it again for the multiplication by \(V\). At \(L = 8192\) that intermediate is \(8192^2 \times 2\) bytes \(= 134\) MB per head per layer, and it is touched several times. The arithmetic was never the problem.

FlashAttention, Theorem 2 and Proposition 3 · Dao et al. 2022

Theorem 2. Let \(N\) be the sequence length, \(d\) the head dimension, and \(M\) the size of SRAM with \(d \le M \le Nd\). Standard attention requires \(\Theta(Nd + N^2)\) HBM accesses, while FlashAttention requires \(\Theta(N^2d^2M^{-1})\).

Proposition 3. With the same quantities, there does not exist an algorithm to compute exact attention with \(o(N^2d^2M^{-1})\) HBM accesses for all \(M\) in the range \([d, Nd]\).

The authors' own reading of the gap: "For typical values of \(d\) (64-128) and \(M\) (around 100KB), \(d^2\) is many times smaller than \(M\), and thus FlashAttention requires many times fewer HBM accesses than standard implementation."10

Common error

"FlashAttention is IO-optimal." Proposition 3 rules out an algorithm that beats \(\Theta(N^2d^2M^{-1})\) simultaneously across the whole range of \(M\); it does not establish optimality at each fixed \(M\). The paper's own phrasing is careful: FlashAttention "is optimal for a range of SRAM sizes". The unqualified claim overstates a real and important result.

Notice what FlashAttention does not do. It does not reduce the FLOP count - the output is bit-identical to standard attention, hence "exact" in the title. It does not change the \(O(L^2)\) arithmetic. It changes the constant on the memory traffic, and that alone bought a 3× speedup on GPT-2 training. If FLOPs were what mattered, that speedup would be impossible. Two successor papers push the same idea further by improving work partitioning12 and by exploiting Hopper's asynchrony and FP8 arithmetic,13 which is worth knowing mostly as evidence that attention's practical cost is an implementation variable rather than a property of the mathematics.

5.4 Why decoding is a bandwidth problem, not a compute problem

Here is the whole of §4.5 restated in the language of §5.2. To emit one token, attention must read every cached key and value for that sequence out of HBM, and it performs a couple of FLOPs per element read. That is intensity \(\approx 1\) against a ridge of 153. Shazeer computed this ratio directly in 2019 and got \(\Theta(n/d + 1/b)\) for the decoding regime versus \(O(1/k + 1/(bn))\) for the batched regime.8

The reframing that matters

Ask "how long does it take to generate one token from a 128k context" and the answer has almost nothing to do with the \(O(L^2)\) that everyone quotes. It is: how many bytes must cross the HBM bus. For Llama-3-70B at 128k that is 42.9 GB of KV cache. At 2.0 TB/s the floor is \(42.9/2000 \approx 21\) ms per token, per sequence, before a single useful multiply. Under 25 tokens per second, from bandwidth alone.

For a recurrent model the same quantity is 13.1 MB, giving a floor of about 7µs. That is a factor of roughly 3000, it is the real reason anyone is interested in state space models, and it is invisible in every complexity table in §2 and §3.

Do not over-rotate

Batching hides much of this in production: at batch \(b\) the weights are amortized, so the \(1/b\) term shrinks, and the effective intensity climbs. What batching cannot amortize is the KV cache, because every sequence has its own. So the KV term is exactly the part of the cost that resists the standard fix, and it is the part that scales with context. Pope et al.'s serving analysis reaches the same conclusion from the deployment side: past a certain batch and context the key-value cache, not the parameters, is what fills the machine.14 That is the shape of the problem.

Section 5 in three lines

Peak FLOPs and memory bandwidth differ by a factor of 153 on an A100 measured in FLOP per byte. Anything below that intensity is limited by transport, and its FLOP count no longer predicts its runtime.

Attention training is compute-bound after tiling, which is what FlashAttention exploits without changing a single FLOP. Attention decoding is bandwidth-bound at intensity near 1, and the term that resists batching is precisely the KV cache. The recurrent alternative wins here by roughly three orders of magnitude, and the whole of the rest of this course is about what that costs in accuracy.

Section 6

What is provably lost, and what is not

There is now a real theory of what these architectures can and cannot represent. It is more interesting than the folklore in both directions: the loss from a fixed state is genuine and provable, the observed failures happen four orders of magnitude earlier than the theory requires, and there are natural tasks that attention provably cannot do and a plain RNN can.

6.1 The counting argument

Start with the cleanest possible statement. Give the model a random string, then a separator, then ask it to reproduce the string. This is the copy task, and it is the purest test of "was the input still available".

Prerequisite: the pigeonhole argument for information skip if counting arguments are familiar

There are \(D^L\) strings of length \(L\) over an alphabet of size \(D\). If a model's state can take only \(|\mathcal{S}|\) distinct values and \(|\mathcal{S}| \lt D^L\), then two different strings must map to the same state. After that point the model cannot behave differently on them, so on at least one of the two it reproduces the wrong string. No assumption about the architecture, the training, or the parameter count is needed: only that the state is a bottleneck, which is Definition 2.1.

Theorem 2.7 and Corollary 2.8 · Jelassi et al. 2024

Theorem 2.7. Fix a generalized state space model \(H\) over state space \(\mathcal{S}\). For all \(L\) and the uniform copy distribution \(\mathcal{D}_L\), the error satisfies

\[ \mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}_L}(H) \;\gt\; 1 - \frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{D^{L}}. \]

Corollary 2.8. Every GSSM whose state memory satisfies \(\mathrm{mem}(\mathcal{S}) \lt L\log D - 1\) has error greater than \(1/2\) on the uniform copy distribution of length \(L\).

Here \(D\) is the alphabet size and \(\mathrm{mem}(\mathcal{S}) = \log_2|\mathcal{S}|\) is the state size in bits. "Generalized state space model" is their name for any architecture with a fixed-size input-dependent state, which includes RNNs, LSTMs, S4 and Mamba.15

The companion result for the other family is stronger than one might guess.

Theorem 2.3 · Jelassi et al. 2024

For all \(n\), there exists a depth-2 transformer \(\mathcal{T}\) of dimension \(O(n\log D)\) such that for all \(2n \le L \le D^{n}\) and any copy distribution \(\mathcal{D}_L\), \(\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}_L}(\mathcal{T}) \lt p_{n\text{-gram}}(\mathcal{D}_L)\), where \(p_{n\text{-gram}}\) is the probability that the input contains a repeated \(n\)-gram. Two layers, width logarithmic in the alphabet, copying strings of length up to \(D^n\): exponential in the model dimension.

Put the two together and the trade of §2 becomes a theorem rather than a table. A Transformer's input-dependent memory grows like \(L\), and by Corollary 2.8 that is optimal for this task up to logarithmic factors - the authors say so explicitly in their Remark 2.9. The fixed-state family is not merely worse at copying; it is provably worse, and the boundary is set by its state size in bits.

The same wall, reached three different ways

A result you meet once is a curiosity; a result three independent proof techniques arrive at is a fact about the problem. Alongside Jelassi et al.'s counting argument:

  • Sanford, Hsu and Telgarsky reduce from set disjointness and get a bound on any bounded-memory online algorithm - their model lets the per-step update be an arbitrary function, so it is not about neural networks at all. Their Theorem 11: approximating the sparse-averaging task requires memory \(m \ge (N-1)/2\) bits.24
  • Arora et al. reduce from INDEX and get, as their Theorem 3.1, that "any recurrent model depending causally on input \(u\) requires \(\Omega(N)\)-bits in state size to solve MQAR", with a Mamba-specific corollary. Their comment on it is the useful one: this "suggests that the tradeoff observed in Figure 2 is fundamental, not an artifact of architectural quirks."17

Counting, set disjointness and INDEX are three different arguments, and they agree: to retrieve an arbitrary item from a length-\(L\) context you need state that grows with \(L\). Note where the credit belongs - Zoology, the paper best known for measuring this gap, proves an \(\Omega(N)\) bound only for RetNet specifically; the general statement is in the Based paper.

Common error, and it is the important one

"The copying lower bound explains why Mamba cannot do retrieval." It does not, by four orders of magnitude. The bound describes a wall the model never reaches. The observed failures are an optimization and inductive-bias phenomenon: gradient descent on next-token prediction does not discover the "store the whole context verbatim" solution, even where it exists inside the state. Teaching the theorem without the gap makes a fixable engineering problem look like a law of nature, and it is the single most common misreading in this literature.

6.3 What the loss looks like in a real corpus

The gap has been measured on natural text, not just synthetics. Arora et al. stratified Pile validation data by whether the next token completes a bigram that already appeared in context - they call these AR hits, for associative recall - and attributed the perplexity gap between attention-free models and attention:

Zoology · Arora et al. 2023, verbatim

"We find that errors on 'AR Hits' account for 82% of the perplexity gap to attention on average, despite only representing 6.4% of all tokens in the Pile dataset."

And, on how badly parameters substitute for recall: "A 70M parameter Transformer can predict AR Hits better than a 1.4Bn parameter Hyena gated convolution model (20× larger)." The gap persists at 7B scale when comparing RWKV with Llama-2.16

Read that against §1.2. Six percent of tokens carry eighty-two percent of the deficit. This is exactly the failure mode a per-token average is built to hide, which is why two architectures can be tied on perplexity and wildly separated on anything that requires looking something up. It also says where to spend effort: the deficit is concentrated, not diffuse, and a mechanism that fixes recall specifically - a few attention layers, say - should recover most of it. That is the empirical case for hybrid architectures, and it is why essentially every shipped state space model interleaves some attention.

Two follow-ups sharpen the picture. Arora et al. show the trade is a dial rather than a binary: recall quality tracks recurrent state size, so a designer can buy back accuracy by spending memory.17 And Wen, Dang and Lyu isolate in-context retrieval as the capability separating the families, showing that chain-of-thought or an explicit retrieval step closes much of the representational gap.23 Both point the same way: the deficit is specific, localized, and addressable, which is a much more useful conclusion than "recurrent models are worse".

One measurement caution before leaving this section. Advertised context windows are not the same thing as usable ones for any architecture: the RULER benchmark varies task length and complexity systematically and finds effective context far below the advertised number across the board.31 When comparing a recurrent model to a Transformer at 128k, check that the Transformer is actually using its 128k before attributing the difference to the state.

6.4 The limits that cut the other way

It would be a poor lesson that only listed the fixed-state family's problems. Both families are, from the standpoint of computational complexity, surprisingly weak, and in overlapping but distinct ways.

Prerequisite: TC0, NC1, and what "circuit class" means skip if you know the ladder

A circuit family solves a problem if for each input length \(n\) there is a circuit \(C_n\) of AND, OR, NOT and (for the "threshold" classes) majority gates that computes the answer. Depth is the longest path from input to output and models parallel time; size models total work.

TC0 is the class solvable by constant-depth, polynomial-size circuits with unbounded-fan-in majority gates. Constant depth means constant parallel time: however long the input, the answer comes out after a fixed number of layers. NC1 allows logarithmic depth. It is widely believed but not proved that TC0 is strictly inside NC1, so a problem that is NC1-complete is considered "inherently sequential": no constant-depth parallel circuit is expected to exist for it.

Uniformity (here, "L-uniform") requires that the circuit for length \(n\) can itself be generated by a small algorithm, which rules out cheating by hiding computation in the choice of circuit.

The Illusion of State · Merrill, Petty and Sabharwal 2024

Their Theorems 4.2 and 4.4 show that log-precision non-gated and diagonal SSMs - the family containing S4 and Mamba - have their convolutional form computable by an L-uniform TC0 circuit family. Consequently they cannot express NC1-complete problems, of which the canonical example is composition of permutations in \(S_5\).

Their summary: "despite their recurrent formulation, the 'state' in common SSMs is an illusion: S4, Mamba, and related models have similar expressiveness limitations to non-recurrent models like transformers". Concrete consequences they list: tracking chess moves in source-target notation (their Corollary 3.4 shows it is NC1-complete), evaluating code, and entity tracking through a long narrative.18

Common error

"The TC0 result explains why Mamba fails at parity." It does not. PARITY is in TC0, and Merrill et al. use it as their example of an easy word problem, the one corresponding to "a world with a single light switch". The TC0 containment rules out NC1-complete problems such as \(S_5\) composition, and nothing weaker. Conflating the two is the most frequent error in secondary accounts of this literature.

Parity does fail for these models, but for an unrelated and much more specific reason, and the reason is a design choice rather than a complexity class.

Theorem 2 · Sarrof, Veitsman and Hahn 2024

"No SSM satisfying nonnegative can recognize PARITY at arbitrary input lengths with finite precision."

The nonnegative condition is \(A(x_t) \ge 0\), and the authors note it holds "across all non-time-invariant SSMs surveyed... due to exponential or sigmoid parameterizations of the gate". In Mamba's case \(\bar A = \exp(\Delta A)\) with \(A \lt 0\), which is positive by construction. The same paper's Theorem 1 shows SSMs handle Flip Flop state tracking, which transformers struggle to represent exactly - so the two families genuinely have different strengths, not merely different costs.19

The follow-up is the satisfying part. Grazzi et al. show that extending the eigenvalue range of linear RNNs from \([0,1]\) to \([-1,1]\) - allowing the recurrence to alternate sign rather than only decay - restores parity and a family of related state-tracking abilities, at no cost to language modelling.20 A limitation that looked fundamental turned out to be one inequality in a parameterization. Keep that in mind whenever you meet an impossibility result about an architecture: read carefully which hypothesis is doing the work.

Separations exist in attention's favour too, and they are provable rather than merely observed. Sanford, Hsu and Telgarsky exhibit a sparse-averaging task on which one attention head is exponentially more efficient than any recurrent or fully-connected alternative of comparable size.24 So the picture is not "attention is better"; it is "each family has tasks the other cannot reach cheaply".

And attention has its own hard limits. Hahn proved in 2020 that transformers cannot recognize certain simple languages - including, for hard attention, parity and Dyck-1 - at unbounded length, and that with soft attention the change in output from a single input symbol decays as \(O(1/n)\), so a fixed-precision model cannot maintain a sharp decision as sequences grow.21 Merrill and Sabharwal established the general form: log-precision transformers are simulable in uniform TC0, which is the same ceiling the SSMs hit.22 The parallelism that made the Transformer trainable is exactly what caps its expressivity, which is why they call it "the parallelism tradeoff".

Proved. A fixed \(S\)-bit state cannot copy strings longer than about \(S/\log_2 D\) tokens (Jelassi Cor. 2.8). A depth-2 transformer can copy strings exponentially long in its width (Thm 2.3), and its linear memory is optimal for the task up to log factors.

Measured. The wall predicted by the bound sits near \(10^7\) tokens; the observed collapse happens near \(10^3\). The gap is optimization, not capacity. On real text, \(6.4\%\) of tokens carry \(82\%\) of the perplexity deficit, and they are the recall-heavy ones.

Both directions. Log-precision SSMs and log-precision transformers both live in uniform TC0, so neither does \(S_5\) composition. Parity fails for SSMs because of a non-negativity choice in the gate, not because of complexity - and allowing negative eigenvalues fixes it.

Section 7

The design space

Sections 3 to 6 established a dichotomy and then complicated it. Two axes are enough to hold every architecture in the field, and once they are drawn, one corner is conspicuously empty. Filling that corner is what Mamba is.

7.1 Two axes

The first axis is the one from §2: how does per-sequence memory grow with \(L\), constant or linear. The second is subtler and is the one this course is really about: does the update depend on the content of the input.

Definition · time-invariant versus selective

A recurrence \(h_t = \varphi_t(h_{t-1}, x_t)\) is time-invariant (equivalently, an LTI system) if \(\varphi_t\) does not depend on \(t\) or on the input beyond the current token entering additively - so the same transition matrix is applied at every step regardless of what has been read.

It is selective, or input-dependent, if the parameters of the transition are themselves functions of \(x_t\). The forget gate of equation (5) is the classical example; Mamba's \(\Delta_t = f(x_t)\) is the modern one.

The second axis carries a hidden cost, and it is worth naming now because it is the engine of the entire S4-to-Mamba story. A time-invariant recurrence can be unrolled into a convolution: because the same matrix applies at every step, the map from inputs to outputs is a single fixed kernel, and a convolution can be evaluated for all positions at once with an FFT. That is how S4 trains in parallel despite being a recurrence, and it is why the bottom-left quadrant below is populated. Make the transition input-dependent and the kernel changes at every position, so the convolution trick dies. Recovering parallel training for a selective recurrence needed a different algorithm - the associative scan of lesson 09 - and that algorithm is the reason Mamba exists as an engineering artefact rather than only as an idea.

What the theory of §6 says about hybrids, and does not

A hybrid inherits the circuit-complexity ceiling of its components: interleaving constant-depth-simulable layers does not escape uniform TC0, so \(S_5\) composition remains out of reach for both pure and hybrid designs at fixed depth. What hybrids buy is the thing §6.1 and §6.3 are about: exact retrieval from context, at a KV-cache cost divided by the number of layers you left as attention. Those are different problems, and it is worth keeping them apart.

What to carry forward

Nine claims that the rest of the course assumes, and eight exercises whose answers you can check against the numbers in this lesson.

8.1 The nine claims

  1. The dichotomy is upstream of neural networks. Equation (1) forces a causal map whose input grows, so either you store the input or you compress it. Attention and recurrence are the two answers, and there is no third.
  2. A state is a bottleneck measured in bits. If two prefixes collide in the state, no downstream machinery can separate them. The KV cache is a state in this sense, one that never overwrites.
  3. RNNs had three problems, not one. Vanishing gradients (fixed by gates), sequential depth (fixed only in 2023, by the scan), and finite capacity (not fixable, and the price of the design).
  4. Sequential depth, not gradients, is why the Transformer won. On FLOPs the recurrent layer was cheaper at the sequence lengths of 2017. It lost on the parallelism column of Table 1.
  5. The \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) is a variance calculation. Logits have standard deviation \(\sqrt{d_k}\); at \(d_k=128\) that is 11.3, the softmax saturates, its Jacobian \(p_i(\delta_{ij}-p_j)\) collapses, and nothing learns.
  6. "Attention is quadratic" is the wrong headline. Attention is \(L/(L+6d)\) of the forward FLOPs, so a minority until \(L \approx 6d\). The inference problem is bytes, not FLOPs: \(320\) KB per token for a 70B-class model, read from HBM at every step.
  7. Below 153 FLOP per byte the FLOP count stops predicting the runtime. That is the A100 ridge point. Attention decoding sits near intensity 1. FlashAttention is a 3× speedup with zero FLOPs removed, which is the proof of the point.
  8. The capacity bound is real and is not what binds. Corollary 2.8 permits \(10^7\) tokens for Mamba-2.8B; retrieval fails near \(10^3\). The gap is what the model learns to store, not what it can store.
  9. Both families sit in uniform TC0. Neither composes permutations in \(S_5\). Parity fails for SSMs for a separate and repairable reason: a non-negative gate. Read the hypotheses of impossibility results before believing their headlines.

8.2 Exercises

Each answer follows from a formula in this lesson. Work them before opening the answer.

1. At what context length does attention consume 90% of the forward FLOPs of a model with \(d = 4096\)?

Set \(L/(L+6d) = 0.9\), so \(L = 9 \cdot 6d = 54d = 221{,}184\) tokens. Note the shape of the answer: the crossover scales linearly with width, so wider models push the quadratic term further out. A frontier-width model at 8k context is spending under a fifth of its arithmetic on attention.

2. A model has 40 layers and grouped-query attention with 8 KV heads of width 128, in bf16. How large is its KV cache at 32k context, and how does that compare to Mamba-2.8B's state?

By equation (6): \(2 \times 40 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 163{,}840\) bytes per token, so \(163{,}840 \times 32{,}768 = 5.37\) GB. Mamba-2.8B holds 13.1 MB, a factor of 410. Also note the per-token figure, 160 KB: at 2.0 TB/s that is 80µs of pure transport per generated token per sequence.

3. With \(\lambda_1\gamma = 0.95\), after how many steps does the gradient bound fall below bf16 machine epsilon, \(7.81\times10^{-3}\)?

Solve \(0.95^{\,n} = 7.81\times10^{-3}\): \(n = \ln(7.81\times10^{-3})/\ln(0.95) = 94.6\), so by step 95. Compare with fp32's \(1.19\times10^{-7}\), which needs \(n = 310\). Training precision therefore changes how far back credit can propagate by a factor of about three, which is a real and underappreciated consequence of the move to bf16.

4. NVIDIA's H100 SXM specification lists BFLOAT16 Tensor Core at 1,979 TFLOP/s with sparsity, and HBM3 bandwidth at 3.35 TB/s. Compute the dense ridge point and compare it with the A100's.

Dense is half the sparse figure, \(1{,}979/2 = 989.5\) TFLOP/s, which is also the number FlashAttention-3 uses as its denominator when it reports 740 TFLOP/s as "75% utilization".13 So the ridge is \(989.5/3.35 = 295\) FLOP per byte, against the A100's 153. It is worse: compute grew faster than bandwidth, so a memory-bound kernel captures an even smaller share of the newer chip. Every generation of accelerator makes the argument of §5 stronger, which is why architectures with low memory traffic keep becoming more attractive rather than less.

The exercise is worded carefully because this is a genuine trap. NVIDIA's H100 datasheet prints only with-sparsity figures for every Tensor Core row, so the dense BF16 number appears nowhere on it. The "989" that is printed there is the TF32 row, also with sparsity, and the near-coincidence has produced a lot of confidently wrong slides. The A100 datasheet, by contrast, prints both dense and sparse.

5. What state size, in bits and in megabytes of fp32, would Corollary 2.8 require to copy a 128k-token context from a 50,280-token vocabulary?

\(131{,}072 \times \log_2 50{,}280 = 131{,}072 \times 15.62 = 2.05\times10^{6}\) bits, so about 256 KB, or 64k fp32 numbers. Mamba-2.8B's state is \(1.678\times10^{8}\) bits, which is 82 times more than the bound requires. Conclusion: capacity is not the binding constraint at any context length anyone runs, which is claim 8 restated.

6. Why does buying a bigger GPU not fix the RNN training problem, when it does fix the attention FLOP problem?

Because the recurrent chain's length is \(L\) regardless of how many arithmetic units exist. Extra parallelism can make each step wider - larger batch, larger \(N\) - but cannot make step \(t\) start before step \(t-1\) finishes. Attention's cost is work, which parallel hardware absorbs; the recurrence's cost is depth, which it cannot. This is why lesson 09's associative scan matters so much: it changes the depth from \(O(L)\) to \(O(\log L)\).

7. Zoology reports that 6.4% of tokens carry 82% of the perplexity gap. What does that predict about hybrid architectures, and what does it predict about scaling a pure recurrent model?

If the deficit is concentrated in recall-heavy tokens, then a mechanism that fixes recall specifically should recover most of the gap at a small fraction of the cost - which is the hybrid design, and Nemotron-H's 8% attention layers are that prediction cashed out. Conversely, adding parameters to a pure recurrent model should not close it, and does not: a 70M Transformer beats a 1.4B gated convolution on AR hits.

8. A colleague says: "Mamba can't do parity, and parity is in TC0, which is where Mamba lives. Case closed." What is wrong with the argument?

Being inside a class you can simulate is no obstacle at all - the containment TC0 rules problems out, so it can only exclude things outside TC0, such as \(S_5\) composition. Parity's difficulty comes from a different hypothesis entirely: Sarrof et al.'s Theorem 2 requires \(A(x_t) \ge 0\), and Mamba's \(\exp(\Delta A)\) satisfies it. Allowing negative eigenvalues repairs parity while leaving the TC0 containment untouched, which is only possible because the two arguments were never about the same thing.

Artefacts cited without a paper

Hardware specifications used in §5 and in exercise 4 come from vendor documentation, not from a publication. The NVIDIA A100 datasheet lists the 80GB SXM part at 312 TFLOP/s BF16 Tensor Core dense (624 with sparsity) and 2,039 GB/s of HBM2e. The H100 product page lists the SXM part at 1,979 TFLOP/s BFLOAT16 Tensor Core with sparsity and 3.35 TB/s of HBM3; NVIDIA does not print a dense figure for H100, so the 989.5 TFLOP/s used in exercise 4 is derived by halving, and is corroborated by FlashAttention-3, which states "the H100 SXM5 GPU has 989 TFLOPS of FP16 matmul". Model shapes used in Table 4.1 come from the published configuration files of Pythia-2.8B, Llama-3-8B and Llama-3-70B. Where this lesson quotes a hardware number it is a specification, not a measurement, and real kernels reach a fraction of it - which is the entire point of §5.
