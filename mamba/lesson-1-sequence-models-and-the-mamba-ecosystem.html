§ 1Formal setup

What a sequence model is required to compute

Before comparing architectures we need a statement of the problem precise enough that "better" means something. This section fixes notation, states the objective, and isolates the single quantity — the input-dependent memory — along which every architecture in this course differs.

1.1 The autoregressive factorization

Let 𝒱 be a finite vocabulary and x1:L = (x1, …, xL) ∈ 𝒱L a sequence. A language model is a parameterized distribution pθ over 𝒱L. The probability chain rule factorizes it exactly — with no modelling assumption whatsoever — into a product of next-token conditionals[1]:

Autoregressive factorization pθ(x1:L) = L∏t=1 pθ(xt | x<t) (1) The equality is exact. What an architecture supplies is not the factorization but a computable approximation of each conditional pθ(xt | x<t), and the entire design problem is how to represent the growing conditioning set x<t.
Prerequisite — the chain rule of probability

For any two events, P(A, B) = P(A) P(B | A). Applying this repeatedly to a sequence gives Eq. (1). The vertical bar means "given": read p(xt | x<t) as "the probability of the token at position t, given every token before it." The notation x<t is shorthand for (x1, …, xt−1).

Nothing has been assumed here — this is an identity, like 6 = 2 × 3. The modelling begins at the next step, when we choose how to compute each factor.

1.2 Objective and metric

Training minimizes the mean negative log-likelihood of the data under Eq. (1):

Cross-entropy loss (nats per token) ℒ(θ) = −1L L∑t=1 ln pθ(xt | x<t)      PPL = exp(ℒ) (2) Perplexity is the exponentiated loss. Its interpretation: a model with PPL = 20 is as uncertain, on average, as if it were choosing uniformly among 20 equally likely tokens.
Prerequisite — nats, bits, and why perplexity is the safer number

Cross-entropy depends on the logarithm's base. Using natural log gives nats; using log₂ gives bits. Since ℒ2 = ℒ / ln 2, the two differ by a constant factor ≈ 1.443, and a "loss of 2.3" is meaningless until you know which one is meant. PyTorch's cross_entropy returns nats, so PPL = exp(loss).

Perplexity is invariant: exp(ℒ) = 2ℒ2 exactly. This is why papers report it. The term dates to Jelinek et al. 1977 in speech recognition[2]. One warning for later sections: perplexity rewards average next-token prediction and is close to blind to rare-but-critical failures such as failing to copy an exact string — a discrepancy that reappears with force in §5.

1.4 The quantity that actually distinguishes architectures

Every architecture computes pθ(xt | x<t) by first compressing x<t into some intermediate representation and then decoding from it. Jelassi et al. give the taxonomy that makes the comparison sharp[48]: separate a model's memory into two kinds.

Definition 1.1 — two kinds of memory

Input-independent memory is the parameters θ. Its size is fixed at design time and does not depend on the input.

Input-dependent memory is the activations that must be carried forward while processing a particular sequence — the RNN's hidden state, the Transformer's KV cache, the SSM's state tensor. Write its size in bits as S(L).

Almost every result in this course is a statement about S(L), not about θ. The two canonical families sit at opposite ends:

familyinput-dependent memory S(L)consequence
Recurrent (RNN, LSTM, SSM)Θ(1) — constant in L Cheap and lossy. Recall is upper-bounded by state size.
Attention (Transformer)Θ(L) — grows with input Lossless and expensive. Recall is free; memory is not.
The axis is not "recurrent vs. attention" — it is whether input-dependent memory grows with the input. Reframing it this way is what makes §5's theorems apply uniformly to both families, and what makes the intermediate designs of §7 (logarithmically-growing state, hybrids) legible as points on a line rather than as ad-hoc engineering.
A correction worth making early

It is common to hear that attention has "unbounded" memory. That is imprecise. For any finite input the KV cache is finite; what is true is that it grows linearly with L and is therefore not bounded independently of the input. The distinction matters because the lower bounds in §5 are information-theoretic statements about S(L), and they are vacuous unless S is being compared against the entropy of the input at a given length.

It is also tempting to say attention is "a special case of" a recurrent model, or vice versa. The state space duality result[24] shows both are instances of structured masked attention, differing in the structure imposed on the mask — neither contains the other. We return to this in Lesson 6.

1.5 The three costs

Architectures are compared on three axes that are routinely conflated. Keeping them apart is the single most useful analytical habit in this literature.

costwhat it measureswho cares
Training timeFLOPs, and critically the number of sequentially dependent steps Anyone with a GPU cluster. Parallelism over L is what killed RNNs.
Inference time per tokenwork required to emit token t Anyone serving a model. Dominated by memory traffic, not arithmetic — see §4.
Inference stateS(L) in bytes Sets maximum batch size and therefore throughput and cost per token.

A model can be excellent on one and catastrophic on another. The Transformer is superb on training parallelism and poor on inference state; the classical RNN is the exact inverse. Everything in the modern literature is an attempt to be good at all three simultaneously, and §5 explains why that is not simply an engineering matter.

§ 2The recurrent family

Constant memory, and the two failures that cost it the field

The recurrent answer to Eq. (1) is to maintain a fixed-size summary and update it once per token. This is optimal on inference cost and it is where Mamba lives. It lost to the Transformer for two distinct reasons — one about gradients, one about parallelism — and only the second turned out to be fundamental.

2.1 Definition

Definition 2.1 — recurrent sequence model

A recurrent model is a triple (s0, u, r) where s0 is an initial state, u : 𝒮 × 𝒱 → 𝒮 is an update rule and r : 𝒮 → 𝒱 a readout, with

ht = u(ht−1, xt),     yt = r(ht)

The defining property is that 𝒮 does not depend on t: S(L) = log|𝒮| is constant.

The classical Elman instantiation takes ht = σ(Wrecht−1 + Winxt + b) with σ a saturating nonlinearity such as tanh. Note for later that Mamba satisfies Definition 2.1 exactly. It is a recurrent model. What differs is the shape of 𝒮, the form of u, and the algorithm used to evaluate the recurrence during training.

2.2 Failure one: gradients through time

Training requires differentiating a loss at position t with respect to parameters that influenced positions long before. Unrolling produces a product of Jacobians, and products of matrices behave badly.

Prerequisite — Jacobians, matrix norms, singular values

The Jacobian ∂a/∂b of a vector function is the matrix of all partial derivatives: entry (i, j) is ∂ai/∂bj. It is the multivariate generalisation of "the derivative", and the chain rule becomes matrix multiplication.

The spectral norm ‖M‖2 is the largest factor by which M can stretch any vector: ‖M‖2 = max‖v‖=1 ‖Mv‖. It equals the largest singular value σ1(M).

This is not the same as the largest eigenvalue modulus (the spectral radius ρ). Always ρ ≤ σ1, and the gap can be arbitrarily large for non-normal matrices. The distinction is the subject of an erratum below, so it is worth holding on to: singular values govern one-step worst-case growth; eigenvalues govern asymptotic growth of repeated application.

Derivation — backpropagation through time [5]
∂ℰ∂θ = ∑1≤t≤T ∂ℰt∂θ total loss is a sum over positions
∂ℰt∂θ = ∑1≤k≤t ∂ℰt∂xt ∂xt∂xk ∂⁺xk∂θ θ enters at every step k ≤ t
∂xt∂xk = ∏t≥i>k ∂xi∂xi−1  =  ∏t≥i>k Wrec⊤ diag(σ′(xi−1)) the product of Jacobians

Everything hinges on the last line: a product of t − k matrices. If each contracts, the product vanishes geometrically; if each expands, it explodes.

Sufficient condition for vanishing gradients [5], §2.1

Let γ bound the derivative of the nonlinearity, i.e. ‖diag(σ′(x))‖ ≤ γ (γ = 1 for tanh, γ = ¼ for the logistic sigmoid). If λ1, the largest singular value of Wrec, satisfies λ1 < 1/γ, then long-term gradient contributions vanish exponentially.

The argument is one line of submultiplicativity: for all k,

‖ ∂xk+1 / ∂xk ‖ ≤ ‖Wrec⊤‖ · ‖diag(σ′(xk))‖ < (1/γ) · γ = 1

so there exists η < 1 with ‖∂xi+1/∂xi‖ ≤ η, giving a bound of ηt−k on the contribution from step k. With η = 0.9 and a gap of 100 steps that factor is 2.7 × 10−5. The converse, λ1 > 1/γ, is necessary for explosion.

Erratum you will trip over — cite the ICML version, not arXiv

The arXiv v2 of Pascanu et al. states this condition using the largest eigenvalue of Wrec. The published ICML 2013 / PMLR version says largest singular value. The published version is the correct one: the proof uses ‖Wrec⊤‖2, which is by definition the largest singular value, not the spectral radius.

The paper does separately use the spectral radius, correctly, for the purely linear recurrence, where ρ < 1 is sufficient for vanishing as t → ∞. Two different claims about two different models; they are conflated constantly. This distinction returns in Lesson 5, where an SSM's stability is stated in terms of eigenvalues precisely because its recurrence is linear.

The historical fix was architectural: gate the update so that the default path through time is multiplication by something close to 1. That is the LSTM[4], anticipated by the analyses of Hochreiter (1991) and Bengio et al.[3]. Hold this thought — Lesson 8 derives Mamba's gate from a completely different starting point and arrives at the same object.

2.3 Failure two: the sequential bottleneck

The gradient problem was solved by 1997. The problem that was not solved is structural, and it is visible in a single column of Vaswani et al.'s Table 1[6]: the number of sequential operations.

layer typecomplexity per layersequential operationsmaximum path length
Self-attentionO(n²·d)O(1)O(1)
RecurrentO(n·d²)O(n)O(n)

Reproduced from [6] Table 1. n is sequence length, d representation dimension, k kernel size, r neighbourhood size in restricted attention.

Read the middle column, not the second. The recurrent layer is cheaper in FLOPs than attention whenever n > d — a caveat the paper states explicitly and that is almost always dropped when the table is reproduced. What sinks it is O(n) sequential operations. On a device with tens of thousands of parallel cores, a computation with 4,096 dependent steps leaves nearly all of them idle regardless of how few FLOPs it needs.

The insight that the entire modern literature is built on

The recurrent family's disqualifying weakness was never its memory model. It was that ht = u(ht−1, xt) with a nonlinear u admits no parallel evaluation over t.

If u is made linear in h — nonlinearity entering only through the input-dependent coefficients — the recurrence becomes an associative scan, computable in O(log L) depth. Every architecture in §7, without exception, is an instance of this move. Linear attention, S4, Mamba, RWKV, RetNet, GLA, DeltaNet and xLSTM differ only in the structure they impose on the linear operator.

§ 3The attention family

Lossless memory, and the price printed on the receipt

Attention takes the opposite position on Definition 1.1: it refuses to compress. Every past token is retained and re-consulted. This buys perfect recall and full training parallelism, and the bill arrives as O(L²) work and Θ(L) state.

3.1 Definition

Scaled dot-product attention — [6] Eq. (1) Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QK⊤√dk )V (3) Q ∈ ℝL×dk, K ∈ ℝL×dk, V ∈ ℝL×dv. In a causal decoder the matrix QK⊤ is masked above the diagonal so position i attends only to j ≤ i.
Prerequisite — softmax, and what a dot product is doing here

Softmax converts a vector of real scores into a probability distribution: softmax(z)i = ezi / ∑jezj. Outputs are positive and sum to 1. It is "soft" because it approximates argmax without the discontinuity — and the approximation gets sharper as the inputs get larger in magnitude, which is exactly the property that causes the problem in §3.2.

The dot product q · k = ∑iqiki is being used as a similarity score. So Eq. (3) reads: score every past position against the current query, normalise the scores into weights, and return the weighted average of the values. The whole mechanism is a soft, learned lookup table.

3.2 Why √dk — the derivation

The scaling factor is not cosmetic. The paper's stated reason[6] is:

"We suspect that for large values of dk, the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax function into regions where it has extremely small gradients. To counteract this effect, we scale the dot products by 1/√dk." Vaswani et al. 2017, §3.2.1

with footnote 4 supplying the variance argument. Here it is in full.

Derivation — the variance of an unscaled logit
assume qi, ki independent, 𝔼[·] = 0, Var[·] = 1 componentwise, not vectorwise
𝔼[q·k]= dk∑i=1 𝔼[qi]𝔼[ki] = 0 independence ⇒ 𝔼[ab] = 𝔼[a]𝔼[b]
Var[qiki]= 𝔼[qi²]𝔼[ki²] − 0 = 1 Var = 𝔼[X²] − 𝔼[X]²
Var[q·k]= dk∑i=1 Var[qiki] = dk variance of a sum of independents adds
so sd[q·k] = √dk  ⟹  dividing by √dk restores unit variance the fix

With dk = 128 the unscaled logits have standard deviation ≈ 11.3, so gaps of 30–40 between the largest and second-largest are routine. Since e−30 ≈ 10−13, softmax saturates to a one-hot vector and its Jacobian — whose entries are of order pi(δij − pj) — collapses to zero. No gradient reaches the attention weights. Dividing by √dk keeps the logit spread O(1) independent of head dimension.

3.3 Cost: time

Forming QK⊤ costs O(L²dk) and multiplying by V costs O(L²dv), giving the O(n²d) of Table 1. At inference under a KV cache, emitting token t requires O(t·d) work, so generating L tokens costs

L∑t=1 O(t·d) = O(L²d) (4) Per-token cost grows with position. A model that is fast at token 100 is measurably slower at token 100,000 — not because of any implementation defect, but by construction.

3.4 Cost: state — the KV cache

Because K and V for past positions never change, they are cached. That cache is the input-dependent memory of Definition 1.1.

KV cache size in bytes SKV = 2 · nlayers · nkv-heads · dhead · L · B · b (5) The leading 2 is for K and V; b is bytes per element (2 for bf16). Under standard multi-head attention with dhead = D/nheads this collapses to 2 nlayers D L B b.
Sanity check against a published figure

No paper states Eq. (5) verbatim — it follows from the definition — so it is worth validating. Pope et al.[10] report that PaLM 540B at batch 512, context 2048 has a KV cache of 3 TB, three times the size of the model's parameters. PaLM 540B: 118 layers, 48 heads, dhead = 128.

2 × 118 × 48 × 128 × 2048 × 512 × 2 B = 3.04 × 10¹² B = 3.04 TB ✓

Equation (5) reproduces the published number exactly. This is the arithmetic that drives every architectural decision in §7 and §8.

Two standard mitigations reduce nkv-heads while leaving nheads for queries untouched: multi-query attention[8] shares a single key/value head across all query heads (nkv-heads = 1, an H-fold reduction), and grouped-query attention[9] interpolates by partitioning query heads into G groups (nkv-heads = G). GQA-1 is MQA; GQA-H is MHA. These change the constant, not the Θ(L) growth — a point that matters when reading Jamba's cache comparison in §8, where part of the advantage over LLaMA-2 comes from GQA and part from having only four attention layers.

What we have established so far

Recurrence gives Θ(1) state and O(L) sequential depth. Attention gives O(1) depth and Θ(L) state. Neither is a bug to be fixed by better engineering — they are consequences of the choice made about Definition 1.1.

The obvious question is whether some third design gets O(1) depth and Θ(1) state. Section 2's closing insight says yes, mechanically. Section 5 explains what such a design must give up in exchange, and proves that it is not nothing.

§ 4The hardware layer

Asymptotic complexity is the wrong model of cost

Sections 2 and 3 counted operations. Modern accelerators do not charge by the operation; they charge by the byte moved. This section makes that precise, because without it two central results — why FlashAttention is fast, and why Mamba's selective scan needed a custom kernel — are simply incomprehensible.

4.1 The memory hierarchy

"The A100 GPU has 40-80GB of high bandwidth memory (HBM) with bandwidth 1.5-2.0TB/s and 192KB of on-chip SRAM per each of 108 streaming multiprocessors with bandwidth estimated around 19TB/s. The on-chip SRAM is an order of magnitude faster than HBM but many orders of magnitude smaller in size." Dao et al. 2022, FlashAttention §2.1[11]

4.2 Arithmetic intensity

Definition 4.1 — arithmetic intensity[7]

The arithmetic intensity of an operation is the number of arithmetic operations performed per byte of memory traffic. An operation is compute-bound if its intensity exceeds the machine's FLOP-per-byte ratio, and memory-bound otherwise.

For the A100 that machine ratio is roughly 312 × 10¹² / 2 × 10¹² ≈ 156 FLOPs per byte. An operation doing less arithmetic than that per byte it touches will leave the arithmetic units idle no matter how it is written.

Shazeer[8] gives the cleanest quantitative statement of what this means for Transformers. He works with the inverse ratio, memory accesses per arithmetic operation, so lower is better. For batched training over a full sequence:

O(1k + 1bn) Small — the model is comfortably compute-bound. "This low ratio is necessary for good performance on modern GPU/TPU hardware, where the computational capacity can be two orders of magnitude higher than the memory bandwidth."

For incremental (autoregressive) decoding, one token at a time, the same analysis gives:

Θ(nd + 1b) (6) "When n ≈ d or b ≈ 1, the ratio is close to 1, causing memory bandwidth to be a major performance bottleneck on modern computing hardware." — Shazeer 2019, §2.4.1
The single most useful fact about inference

The offending term in Eq. (6) is n/d: the KV cache must be re-read from HBM at every decoding step, and it grows with n while the arithmetic done on it does not. Pope et al. put it plainly: "the on-chip memory needs to load this KV cache from off-chip memory once for every token generated, during which the computational core of the chip is essentially idle."[10]

Autoregressive decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound. Training is compute-bound. They are different regimes and an architecture can be excellent in one and poor in the other. MQA reduces that term by a factor of h[8]; Mamba removes it entirely by not having a cache whose size depends on n.

4.3 FlashAttention, and the distinction it teaches

FlashAttention[11] is routinely described as making attention linear. It does not. Keeping this straight is essential, because the same conceptual move reappears as Mamba's selective-scan kernel in Lesson 9.

Theorem 1[11] — FLOPs are unchanged

"Algorithm 1 returns O = softmax(QK⊤)V with O(N²d) FLOPs and requires O(N) additional memory beyond inputs and output."

Same arithmetic as the naive algorithm. What changes is that the N × N attention matrix is never materialized — it is computed tile-by-tile in SRAM using online softmax, so extra memory falls from O(N²) to O(N).

Theorem 2[11] — what actually improves

With M the SRAM size and d ≤ M ≤ Nd: standard attention requires Θ(Nd + N²) HBM accesses, while FlashAttention requires Θ(N²d²M−1).

"For typical values of d (64-128) and M (around 100KB), d² is many times smaller than M" — so the reduction is large in practice while the FLOP count is untouched.

Proposition 3 adds that no exact-attention algorithm achieves o(N²d²M−1) HBM accesses for all M in that range. Note the quantifier: the optimality is over the range of M, not for each fixed M, which is why the abstract hedges to "optimal for a range of SRAM sizes". Calling FlashAttention "IO-optimal" without qualification slightly overstates what is proved.

The transferable lesson, and the one to carry into Lesson 9

FlashAttention's backward pass performs more arithmetic than the naive version, because it recomputes the attention matrix instead of storing it — and it is faster. The paper says so directly: "even with more FLOPs, our recomputation speeds up the backward pass due to reduced HBM accesses."

Mamba's selective scan uses precisely this trade: it never writes the (B, L, D, N) discretized tensor to HBM, and it recomputes intermediate states in the backward pass rather than storing them. Same authors, same insight, different operator. Once you have internalised that memory traffic and not arithmetic is the currency, Mamba's engineering contribution stops looking like an implementation detail and starts looking like the reason the architecture is viable at all.

§ 5What theory says

The compression–recall trade is provable, not empirical

It would be convenient if the choice in Definition 1.1 were a matter of engineering taste. It is not. Between 2024 and 2026 a small set of results established that a fixed-size state imposes hard limits, that those limits are information-theoretic rather than architectural, and — most usefully — exactly where they do and do not bite.

5.1 Copying: an information-theoretic lower bound

Jelassi et al.[48] formalise the recurrent family as a generalized state space model (GSSM) — precisely Definition 2.1, with a finite state set 𝒮 and mem(𝒮) = log|𝒮| bits — and ask how long a string it can copy.

Theorem 2.7[48] — GSSM lower bound

Fix a GSSM H over state space 𝒮. For all L, under the uniform copy distribution 𝒟L over an alphabet of size D,

err𝒟L(H) > 1 − |𝒮| / DL

Corollary 2.8[48] — the statement to remember

Every GSSM whose state satisfies mem(𝒮) < L log D − 1 has error greater than ½ on the uniform copy distribution 𝒟L.

The argument is pigeonhole. A uniformly random length-L string over an alphabet of size D carries L log D bits of entropy. Reproducing it exactly requires that all of that information survive in the state. If the state holds fewer bits, distinct inputs are forced to collide, and colliding inputs must produce identical outputs.

The matching upper bound is equally sharp. Theorem 2.3 constructs a depth-2 Transformer of width O(n log D) that copies sequences of length up to Dn by hashing n-grams: hash the last n tokens, attend to the previous occurrence of that hash, emit its successor. Parameters grow only logarithmically in the length copied.

Four qualifications, all of which get dropped in casual retellings

1. It is a probabilistic statement about a specific distribution — the uniform copy distribution — not a universal impossibility claim. 2. The conclusion is "error > ½", not "cannot"; the −1 in the threshold is what converts inexactness into that bound. 3. The bound constrains activations only. Parameters and per-step compute are entirely unconstrained, which only strengthens the result. 4. It applies to deterministic finite-state update rules per their definition.

Cooper et al.[53] generalise this from one state space to a composition of k layers: any stack of SSM layers computing the task with probability ½ must satisfy k∑i=1 log|𝒮i| ≥ Ω(m log|𝒱| − q log|𝒴|). Jelassi's result is the k = 1 case. Depth does not buy you out of the bound; total state bits is what counts.

5.2 But is the bound what is actually binding?

Here is where careless teaching does damage. Run the numbers on a real model and the bound is nowhere near active. The authors say so themselves:

"A latent state of 1000 32-bit floating point numbers has enough bits to store at least 2000 tokens from a 50K token vocabulary. However, even though a GSSM could fit the context into memory, it may not learn to do so."Jelassi et al. 2024, §3[48]

The correct reading

The information-theoretic bound is real, and at extreme lengths it is the binding constraint. At the lengths where recurrent models are actually observed to fail at retrieval — thousands of tokens, not millions — the state is nowhere near full. The observed gap is therefore predominantly an optimisation and inductive-bias failure, not a capacity failure.

Teaching the theorem without this caveat invites students to attribute an empirical phenomenon to information theory, which is both wrong and demotivating: it suggests the problem is unfixable when in fact the mechanistic evidence in §5.5 suggests it is quite fixable.

5.3 Recall as a function of state size

Arora et al.[41] give the complementary result, and their framing turns the discussion from binary to quantitative.

Theorem 3.1[41]

Any recurrent model depending causally on input u ∈ {0,1}N×d requires Ω(N) bits of state to solve multi-query associative recall (MQAR).

Proved by reduction from the INDEX problem in one-way communication complexity: Alice encodes her bit-string as key–value pairs, runs the model, and transmits only the state; Bob queries it. A sub-linear state would beat the known Ω(N) lower bound for INDEX.

The empirical companion is the more useful artefact: a recall–throughput Pareto frontier that holds both within and across architecture classes. Larger state buys more recall, monotonically; and at a fixed state budget, architectures differ markedly in how efficiently they use it. Among the models they survey, Mamba makes the best use of a limited budget, gated-convolution models such as H3 and Hyena fall well below the frontier, and Based pushes it out further still.

5.4 Expressivity: what a linear recurrence cannot compute

A separate limitation concerns sequential computation rather than memory. Merrill et al.[49] prove that the parallelism which rescued the recurrent family also caps its power.

Prerequisite — circuit complexity in sixty seconds

TC⁰ is the class of problems solvable by boolean circuits of constant depth and polynomial size, using unbounded-fan-in AND, OR and majority gates. "Constant depth" means the computation finishes in a fixed number of parallel rounds regardless of input size — it is the formal notion of "massively parallelisable".

NC¹ allows depth logarithmic in the input size. It is believed strictly larger (TC⁰ ⊊ NC¹), but this is a conjecture, not a theorem — of the same character as P ≠ NP.

A word problem over a group asks: given a sequence of group elements, what is their product? Barrington's theorem[58] states that the word problem of every finite non-solvable group is NC¹-complete. The symmetric group S₅ — the 120 permutations of five objects — is the canonical non-solvable example, so "compose these permutations" is the standard hard sequential task.

Theorem 4.4 and Corollary 4.5[49]

If a generalized linear SSM has diagonal transitions Ai = diag(āi) with āi computable in L-uniform TC⁰ from xi, then its convolutional form is computable in L-uniform TC⁰. In particular this holds for S6, the layer used by Mamba, whose Ai = exp(δiA) is diagonal.

Corollary 4.7[49] — the headline

"Assuming TC⁰ ≠ NC¹, no log-precision SSM with the S4 or S6 architecture can solve the word problem for S₅ or any other NC¹-hard problem."

The title of that paper is The Illusion of State, and the phrase is earned: an S6 layer looks recurrent, carries something called a state, and yet is no more powerful in this sense than a Transformer. The reason is exactly the linearity that makes the parallel scan possible — a product of diagonal matrices collapses to elementwise products, which are computable in constant depth.

A correction that is worth making loudly: parity is a different story

It is extremely common to see Mamba's failure on PARITY attributed to the TC⁰ result. That is wrong. PARITY is in TC⁰ — Merrill et al. give it as their own example of a word problem that is easy. The circuit argument cannot explain a failure on a problem inside the class.

The actual reason is a finite-precision and eigenvalue-range argument from a different paper. Sarrof et al.[50], Theorem 2: "No SSM satisfying nonnegative can recognize PARITY at arbitrary input lengths with finite precision. In particular, this applies to Mamba." On an input of all ones the activations converge, and at finite precision convergence means they become constant, so no alternating output can be read off. Grazzi et al.[51] extend this: transition matrices with only positive eigenvalues cannot do parity, and non-triangular matrices are needed to count modulo 3.

Two genuinely distinct failure modes — one about circuit depth, one about the sign of eigenvalues at finite precision — and only the second is repaired by allowing negative or complex eigenvalues, which is precisely what Mamba-3[25] does.

These limits are architectural, not intrinsic to recurrence. Merrill et al. themselves construct escapes: an SSM with a nonlinearity in the recurrence, or with fully input-dependent non-diagonal transitions, recognises any regular language in a single layer. Shipped instances of the escape now exist — RWKV-7[33] (with Merrill as co-author) claims to recognise all regular languages while remaining parallelisable; DeltaProduct[44] tunes expressivity via products of Householder matrices; Mamba-3 adds complex-valued state updates for exactly this reason.

5.5 What the experiments say, and the hybrid resolution

NVIDIA's controlled 8B-parameter study[54] — same data, up to 3.5T tokens, with Dao and Gu among the authors — is the decisive empirical confirmation:

"While pure SSMs match or exceed Transformers on many tasks, they lag behind Transformers on tasks which require strong copying or in-context learning abilities (e.g., 5-shot MMLU, Phonebook) or long-context reasoning… the 8B Mamba-2-Hybrid exceeds the 8B Transformer on all 12 standard tasks we evaluated (+2.65 points on average) and is predicted to be up to 8× faster when generating tokens." Waleffe et al. 2024[54]

The mechanistic explanation is unusually clean. Bick, Xing & Gu[55] find that both Transformers and SSMs implement retrieval through a Gather-and-Aggregate mechanism concentrated in a very small number of heads — disabling a single such head in a pruned Llama-3.1-8B collapses MMLU from 66% to 25%, which is chance. SSMs' deficit localises to those heads producing smoother attention patterns than the sharp transitions the mechanism needs. The gap "exists in just a few heads, rather than the entire language model", which is precisely why interleaving a small number of attention layers is enough. Wen et al.[52] prove the corresponding statement: adding one Transformer layer lifts an RNN to solving all polynomial-time problems with chain-of-thought.

Theorem 4.3[53] — hybrids are strictly better than both

For selective copying: any pure SSM requires ∑i log|𝒮i| ≥ N log M; any pure Transformer requires working memory Ω(L). But a two-layer hybrid of one Mamba layer and one attention layer solves it for every input with width O(max(log|𝒱|, log L)) and working memory Õ(N) ≪ L.

A caveat on the benchmark literature

Okpekpe & Orvieto[56] report that, unlike Transformers, modern recurrent models are unusually sensitive to learning rate on associative-recall benchmarks — an issue that "can severely affect reported performance in previous works" — and that recurrent and attention models scale differently in width versus depth. Treat specific MQAR numbers from 2024 as provisional. The direction of the results is corroborated from several independent angles; the precise magnitudes are less settled than the citation count suggests.

§ 8Mixture of Experts × Mamba

Two orthogonal scaling axes, and what happens when you use both

Mamba attacks the cost of context length. Mixture of Experts attacks the cost of parameter count. The two are close to independent, which is why the combination appeared within five weeks of the Mamba paper and now underpins several frontier models. This section gives the routing mathematics, the three canonical architectures, and a correction to the argument that is usually offered for why they fit together.

8.1 The MoE layer

Definition 8.1 — sparsely-gated mixture of experts[60]

Replace a dense feed-forward layer by n independent expert networks E1, …, En and a gating network G. The layer computes

y = n∑i=1 G(x)i Ei(x)

Because G is constructed to be sparse — all but k entries are zero — only k experts are ever evaluated. Parameter count scales with n; per-token FLOPs scale with k.

The Switch Transformer[62] simplification, which is what MoE-Mamba adopts, takes k = 1 with a plain softmax router:

Switch routing — [62] Eqs. (1)–(2) pi(x) = eh(x)iN∑jeh(x)j     y = ∑i∈𝒯 pi(x)Ei(x) (7) with h(x) = Wrx and 𝒯 the top-k indices. Keeping the gate value pi as a multiplier — rather than just selecting the expert — is what makes the router differentiable: the gradient reaches Wr through that scalar.

8.2 Load balancing — the problem nobody expects

A naive router collapses. Whichever expert is marginally better early in training receives more tokens, trains faster, becomes better still, and within a few thousand steps the other n − 1 experts are dead weight. The fix is an auxiliary loss that penalises imbalance.

Switch load-balancing loss — [62] Eqs. (4)–(6) ℒaux = α · N · N∑i=1 fi · Pi (8) fi = 1T ∑x∈ℬ 𝟙{argmax p(x) = i} — the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i.   Pi = 1T ∑x∈ℬpi(x) — the fraction of router probability mass on expert i. Coefficient α = 10−2.
Why the loss has that peculiar two-factor form

The obvious thing to penalise is the variance of fi, the actual token counts. But fi comes from an argmax and is piecewise constant — its gradient with respect to the router weights is zero almost everywhere. Optimising it directly is impossible.

So the loss multiplies the non-differentiable quantity fi by the differentiable one Pi. Gradient flows only through Pi, and fi acts as a per-expert weight saying "this expert is currently overloaded, so push its probability mass down." GShard[61] uses the same construction with ce/S · me.

The α·N prefactor makes the loss scale-invariant: at perfect balance fi = Pi = 1/N, so the sum is N · N · (1/N²) = 1 regardless of how many experts there are.

A second mechanism, expert capacity[61], caps how many tokens any expert may accept: capacity = (tokens per batch / number of experts) × capacity factor. Tokens beyond the cap are dropped and pass through the residual connection unmodified. MoE-Mamba uses capacity factor 1.0, i.e. it drops overflow. BlackMamba instead abandons the auxiliary loss entirely in favour of Sinkhorn routing, balancing by optimal transport.

8.3 Why MoE and Mamba fit together — and the argument that does not work

The tempting story is: Mamba's inference is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than FLOP-bound (§4), and MoE buys parameters without FLOPs, so they complement each other. That is not the argument the papers make, and BlackMamba explicitly argues in the opposite direction, noting that "increasing the number of experts increases the parameter count and hence memory cost substantially" — MoE costs you memory and buys you FLOPs, which is exactly backwards for a bandwidth-bound regime.

The argument actually advanced has two parts, and both are better than the bandwidth story.

Part 1 — orthogonal bottlenecks

"Mamba alleviates computation and memory throughput issues stemming from larger context sizes, while MoE alleviates those same issues stemming from increasing number of parameters."[63] Two axes, two tools, near-zero interaction. Jamba states the MoE half identically: it "allows increasing the model capacity (total number of available parameters) without increasing compute requirements (number of active parameters)."[65]

Part 2 — Mamba has an empty slot, and specifically wants a sparse one

A Transformer layer alternates attention with a dense MLP. A vanilla Mamba block has no MLP at all. MoE-Mamba's framing is that this separates "unconditional processing of every token by the Mamba layer" from "conditional processing by an MoE layer" — the block was missing the conditional-computation slot entirely.

The decisive evidence that this is about sparsity and not merely about adding an FFN is their own ablation: a single-expert MoE — which is just a dense feed-forward layer — performs worse than vanilla Mamba (3.75 vs 3.72 log-perplexity), consistent with what the Mamba paper itself reported for Mamba interleaved with MLPs. The gain arrives only once there are enough experts to specialise.

8.5 Two refinements worth knowing

Routing Mamba[69] revisits MoE-Mamba's failed inside-the-block experiment and finds the flaw was independence: expertizing the conv, gate and output projections separately degrades, but sharing a single router matrix across all three — so the projections make the same routing decision — wins, matching dense Mamba models needing up to 2.3× more active parameters.

Swimba[70] supplies the taxonomy that makes the whole subfield legible:

Two kinds of MoE–SSM[70]

(1) MoE over separated SSMs — each expert maintains its own state trajectory, so the recurrence must be evaluated once per expert and compute scales with the number of experts. This is why naive MoE-inside-the-SSM is expensive.

(2) MoE-parameterized SSM — experts produce candidate SSM parameters (B, C), a token-level router mixes them in parameter space before the recurrence, and a single state trajectory is scanned once. The dominant recurrent cost stays fixed.

This is the principled answer to "why does MoE go beside the Mamba block rather than inside it": putting sparsity on the recurrence multiplies the sequential work, while putting it on the projections or on an adjacent FFN does not.

8.6 The counterexample that clarifies the axis

Zamba solves the same problem the opposite way[67][68]

Zamba is not an MoE model — a frequent misattribution. It attaches a single, weight-tied attention + MLP block to a Mamba backbone and invokes it every six Mamba blocks, so that Mamba "can utilize more FLOPs for increased performance at the same memory cost."

Set the two side by side and the design axis becomes explicit:

MoE: more parameters, same FLOPs per token. Costs memory, buys capacity.
Zamba: more FLOPs per token, same parameters. Costs compute, buys capacity.

Which is correct depends entirely on whether you are memory-limited or compute-limited at deployment — which is to say, on §4 rather than on anything about Mamba.

Where MoE × SSM stands in 2026

It is production reality, not research speculation. Granite 4.0[97] (IBM, Apache 2.0): 40 layers at 9:1 Mamba-2:attention, 72 experts top-10 plus a shared expert, 32B total / 9B active. Nemotron 3 Nano[98] (NVIDIA): Mamba-2 + GQA + 128 routable experts and 2 shared, top-6, 31.6B total / 3.2B active, 1M context. Hunyuan-TurboS[79]: 560B/56B active, claimed as the first industry-deployed large-scale Mamba model.

Exercises

mediumFlashAttention performs the same O(N²d) FLOPs as naive attention and its backward pass performs strictly more. Explain, using §4, why it is nonetheless several times faster, and name the quantity it actually optimises.

It optimises HBM accesses, from Θ(Nd + N²) to Θ(N²d²M−1), by never materialising the N × N attention matrix in slow memory — it is computed tile-by-tile in SRAM with online softmax.

Because attention at these shapes is memory-bound (Definition 4.1), the runtime is set by bytes moved, not operations performed. Recomputing the attention matrix in the backward pass costs extra arithmetic but avoids reading an O(N²) tensor from HBM, and arithmetic is roughly two orders of magnitude cheaper than bandwidth. The paper states this directly: "even with more FLOPs, our recomputation speeds up the backward pass due to reduced HBM accesses."

hardA colleague writes: "Mamba can't do copying or parity because the Illusion of State paper proved SSMs are in TC⁰." Identify two distinct errors.

Error 1 — wrong theorem for copying. The copying limitation is information-theoretic, from Jelassi et al.: a state holding fewer than L log D − 1 bits cannot copy reliably. It is a counting argument about state capacity and has nothing to do with circuit depth. The TC⁰ result concerns which functions are computable in constant parallel depth, not how much information is retained.

Error 2 — wrong theorem for parity. PARITY is in TC⁰; Merrill et al. use it as their own example of an easy word problem. So a TC⁰ containment cannot possibly explain failure on it. The real reason is Sarrof et al., Theorem 2: an SSM whose transition coefficients are constrained non-negative cannot recognise PARITY at finite precision, because on a constant input the activations converge and become literally constant. It is an eigenvalue-range and precision argument, and allowing negative or complex eigenvalues repairs it — which is what Mamba-3 does.

What the TC⁰ result does correctly rule out, modulo TC⁰ ≠ NC¹, is composing permutations — the S₅ word problem, NC¹-complete by Barrington's theorem.

mediumTake a Mamba-style model with D = 2560, N = 16, E = 2, 64 layers, fp32 state, and vocabulary 50,280. Compute the longest sequence Corollary 2.8 permits it to copy. Then explain why it fails empirically at roughly one thousandth of that.

State capacity = 64 × 2 × 2560 × 16 × 32 bits = 1.678 × 10⁸ bits. Each token of a 50,280-symbol vocabulary carries log₂(50,280) = 15.62 bits. So the bound permits copying up to ≈ 10.7 million tokens.

Empirical retrieval failures appear at thousands of tokens — three orders of magnitude earlier. The bound is therefore not the binding constraint at any length anyone actually runs. The gap is an optimisation and inductive-bias failure: the model could store the information and does not learn to. Jelassi et al. make exactly this concession, and the mechanistic evidence supports it — Bick, Xing & Gu localise the deficit to a handful of Gather-and-Aggregate heads producing attention patterns that are too smooth, not to exhausted capacity.

The practical implication is optimistic rather than pessimistic: a learning failure is fixable, and interleaving a few attention layers is empirically sufficient to fix it.

mediumWhy is the Switch load-balancing loss a product fi · Pi rather than simply the variance of the token counts fi?

Because fi is defined through an argmax and is piecewise constant in the router parameters — its gradient is zero almost everywhere, so it cannot be optimised directly.

The product pairs it with Pi, the mean router probability, which is differentiable. Gradient flows only through Pi, while fi acts as a constant per-expert weight meaning "this expert is currently overloaded — reduce the probability mass you assign it." GShard uses the identical construction. The α·N prefactor normalises the loss to 1 at perfect balance for any number of experts.

hardWhy does placing MoE inside the SSM recurrence cost far more than placing it beside the block, even at identical active parameter counts?

Swimba's distinction is the clean answer. If each expert is a separate SSM, each maintains its own state trajectory, so the recurrence must be evaluated once per active expert — the sequential scan, which is the expensive and hard-to-parallelise part, multiplies with the number of experts. Active parameter count is unchanged but the dominant recurrent cost is not.

Placing MoE beside the block (MoE-Mamba, Jamba) leaves exactly one scan. Placing it on the block's linear projections (Routing Mamba) also leaves one scan, since the projections are elementwise in time. The principled alternative — Swimba's design — has experts emit candidate SSM parameters which a router mixes in parameter space before the recurrence, so a single trajectory is scanned once.

General principle worth extracting: sparsity is cheap on the parallel parts of a model and expensive on the sequential parts.

mediumThe attention logits are divided by √dk. What would go wrong with 1/dk, and what with no scaling at all?

No scaling: Var[q·k] = dk, so logit spread grows as √dk. At dk = 128 that is a standard deviation of ~11.3, softmax saturates toward one-hot, and its Jacobian entries pi(δij − pj) vanish. No gradient reaches the attention weights.

Scaling by 1/dk: the opposite failure. Variance becomes dk/dk² = 1/dk, so logits shrink toward zero as the head widens and softmax approaches the uniform distribution. Attention becomes an unweighted average and loses its ability to discriminate — the mechanism degenerates to mean pooling.

√dk is the unique power of dk that holds logit variance at O(1) independent of head dimension, which is exactly what keeps softmax in its useful, non-saturated regime.

hardYou must serve a model over 500,000-token legal documents on a fixed GPU budget, and the task requires quoting clauses verbatim. Argue for and against a pure Mamba deployment using this lesson, then state what you would actually build.

For. A 2.8B-class Transformer at 500K tokens carries roughly 328 GB of KV cache (Eq. 5), which does not fit on any single accelerator; batch size collapses to one and cost per token becomes prohibitive. Mamba carries a fixed ~13 MB regardless of length, training is linear rather than quadratic, and decoding is constant-time per token. On a fixed budget this is not an optimisation — it is the difference between feasible and infeasible.

Against. "Quote the clause verbatim" is exact retrieval from a compressed summary, which is precisely the operation §5.1 shows a fixed-size state is worst at, and §5.5 confirms empirically on Phonebook and long-context retrieval. A perplexity-competitive model can fail this task badly, and MoE-Mamba's own perplexity-versus-accuracy discrepancy is the warning sign.

What to build. A hybrid, with the ratio chosen from the evidence: roughly 1:7 attention:Mamba as in Jamba, or ~8% attention layers as in Nemotron-H. Theorem 4.3 of Cooper et al. says a small number of attention layers is provably sufficient for selective copying, and Wen et al. show one attention layer suffices to restore polynomial-time reasoning with CoT. Add GQA on the few attention layers to keep their cache small, and MoE if capacity rather than latency is the binding constraint. If retrieval must be exact and auditable, retrieve the clause into context rather than asking any architecture to hold 500K tokens losslessly — the cheapest correct answer is frequently not an architectural one.
