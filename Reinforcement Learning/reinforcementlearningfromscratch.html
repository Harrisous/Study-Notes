    Lesson 1

    What Reinforcement Learning Is

    Nobody ever handed you a labeled dataset of correct bicycle movements. You got on, wobbled, fell, and something in you noticed which wobbles hurt. That is reinforcement learning, and this class is about writing it down precisely enough that a machine can do it too.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say what makes a problem a reinforcement learning problem
    • Tell RL apart from the other two kinds of machine learning
    • Name the three ingredients every RL setup has
    • Explain why delayed rewards make this hard

    Learning without an answer key

    Most machine learning you have heard of works like a flashcard deck. Here is a photo, the correct answer is "cat". Here is another photo, the correct answer is "dog". Thousands of times. The machine adjusts itself until its guesses match the answers on the back of the cards.

    Reinforcement learning has no cards and no answers. It has a situation, a set of things you could do, and afterward, a number telling you how that went. Nothing tells you what you should have done instead. You have to work that out yourself, by trying things.

    Figure 1.1 — The three families. Look at what comes out the bottom. Supervised learning is told the answer; unsupervised learning is not asked a question; reinforcement learning is given a grade and left to figure out what earned it. Only the third one has an arrow going back up, because only the third one changes the world it is learning from.

    That loop on the right of Figure 1.1 is the whole subject. An agent acts, the world changes, a score comes back, and the agent tries again a little wiser. Everything in the next fifteen lessons is machinery for making that loop work reliably.

    The three ingredients

    Every reinforcement learning problem, from a robot vacuum to a chess engine to the language model you are probably using right now, has exactly three parts.

    The whole vocabulary, for now

    Agent
    The thing doing the learning and the deciding. The robot, the player, the model. It is the only part you get to change.
    Environment
    Everything else. The room, the chessboard, the person typing. It receives the agent's actions and hands back consequences.
    Reward
    A single number, arriving after each action, saying how well things are going. Not "how you should have acted" — just a score.

    Figure 1.2 — The loop, drawn plainly. Two arrows, and that is it. Notice the asymmetry: the agent sends out a choice and gets back both a grade and a new situation. It never receives instructions. Lesson 2 puts names on each arrow.

    Think of it like

    Training a dog. You cannot explain "sit" in words the dog understands. You can only wait, and when something dog-shaped happens that resembles sitting, you produce a treat. The dog is the agent, your kitchen is the environment, and the treat is the reward. Notice that you never once told the dog what to do — you only ever told it how it did.

    The part that makes it hard

    If the reward arrived immediately and told you exactly how good each action was, this would be an easy subject. It does not.

    Consider a chess game you lose on move 40. The reward is a single −1 at the very end. Somewhere back around move 12 you made the mistake that doomed you, but nothing marked it at the time; move 12 felt exactly like moves 11 and 13. The score is delayed, and it is aggregate: one number covering forty decisions.

    Figure 1.3 — Six actions produce nothing at all, then the last one pays out. The agent has to spread that single +1 backward over everything that led to it, without being told how. Discounting (Lesson 3) and value functions (Lesson 6) are the two tools that solve this.

    Where people get lost

    A reward of 0 does not mean "that action was neutral". It means "no information arrived yet". Most steps in most RL problems return exactly 0, and the agent still has to learn from them. If you read a zero as a verdict on the action that just happened, nothing in Part II will make sense.

    Check yourself

    A spam filter is trained on a million emails, each already marked "spam" or "not spam". Is that reinforcement learning?

    • Yes — the labels are the reward.
    • No — every example comes with its right answer, so it is supervised learning.
    • No — reinforcement learning only works on robots.
    • Yes, because the filter improves over time.
    Answer: B. The giveaway is that the correct action is known for every single example. A reward tells you how well you did; a label tells you what you should have done. Option A confuses the two. Option D is tempting but improving over time is true of all machine learning, not just RL.

    The second thing that makes it hard

    The agent's own choices decide what data it ever sees. A supervised learner is handed its dataset. An RL agent generates its dataset by acting, so an agent that never tries the left-hand corridor will never learn anything about the left-hand corridor — including that it was the good one.

    This is the exploration problem. Do you take the meal you already know is decent, or gamble on the restaurant you have never tried? Take the sure thing every time and you will never find anything better. Gamble every time and you will eat badly forever. Lesson 10 gives this a proper treatment; for now just register that it exists, and that it has no free solution.

    The one thing to keep

    Reinforcement learning is what you use when you can score outcomes but cannot demonstrate the right answer, and when today's choice changes tomorrow's situation.

    When to reach for it, and when not to

    RL is the right tool when the problem is sequential, when your feedback is a grade rather than a target, and when you can afford a lot of trials. It is the wrong tool when you already have correct answers — supervised learning is faster, more stable, and easier to debug — or when mistakes are expensive in the real world, because RL learns by making them.

    ProblemGood fit?Why
    Winning at GoYesSequential, one clear score, unlimited self-play
    Cooling a datacenterYesActions now change temperature later; energy use is the score
    Translating a sentenceUsually notMillions of correct translations already exist; just imitate them
    Dosing a patientCarefulSequential and score-driven, but you cannot learn by harming people
    Making a chatbot more helpfulYesNobody can write the ideal reply, but people can reliably say which of two replies is better

    That last row is where this class is headed. Part V shows how the exact machinery from Parts I through IV gets used to train large language models.

    Check yourself

    Why is "we have a million examples of the correct output" usually an argument against using RL?

    • RL cannot handle large datasets.
    • Knowing the correct output is strictly more information than a score, and supervised learning uses it directly.
    • RL requires a physical robot to collect data.
    • A million examples is too few for RL.
    Answer: B. A label says "do this". A reward says "that was worth 0.3". The label is the richer signal, and learning from it needs no exploration, no credit assignment, and no trial and error. Use the strongest signal you have. RL earns its keep precisely when labels do not exist.

    Think it through

    You want to teach a robot arm to pour a glass of water without spilling. Name the agent, the environment, and one reasonable reward. Then say what could go wrong with your reward.

    Agent: the controller choosing joint torques. Environment: the arm, the bottle, the glass, the water, gravity — note that the arm's own physics counts as environment, because the controller cannot change it by wishing. Reward: +1 for each milliliter that lands in the glass, −1 per milliliter spilled.

    What could go wrong: the robot discovers it can score forever by pouring into the glass, tipping it back into the bottle, and repeating. Or it learns to hold the bottle a millimeter above the glass and never actually pour, because that reliably avoids the −1. You wrote down what you could measure, and the agent optimized exactly that rather than what you meant. This gap has a name — reward hacking — and it comes back in Lesson 3 and again in Lesson 15.

    What to remember

    1. RL learns from grades, not answers. No example ever tells the agent what it should have done.
    2. Three ingredients: agent, environment, reward. The agent is the only part you can change; everything else is environment.
    3. Rewards are delayed and lumped together. Working out which past action earned a late reward is the credit-assignment problem.
    4. The agent creates its own data. So it has to explore, and exploring costs something.
    5. Reach for RL only when labels do not exist. If you know the right answer, imitate it instead.

    Lesson 2

    The Agent–Environment Loop

    Almost every diagram in the next fourteen lessons is a redrawing of one picture. Two boxes, three arrows, running forever. Learn it properly now and most of what follows is just putting names on the arrows.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Draw the agent–environment loop from memory and say what each arrow carries
    • Use the words state, action, reward, step, episode, trajectory and terminal state correctly
    • Write a short episode out as a sequence of symbols
    • Say where the boundary between agent and environment really goes, and why it surprises people

    One picture, repeated forever

    Lesson 1 gave you three ingredients: an agent that acts, a world that responds, a number that says how it went. Now watch them move.

    The agent looks at the situation it is in. It picks exactly one action and hands that action over. The world does whatever the world does, and hands back two things: a number saying how that went, and the new situation. Then it all happens again.

    Figure 2.1 — Count the arrows. One goes out and two come back, and keeping those two apart is most of the work. The reward is a verdict on what just happened; the next state is the setup for what happens next. Beginners often collapse them into one thing called "feedback" and then cannot explain why an agent would ever accept a bad reward on purpose.

    That is the entire mechanism. Value iteration, Q-learning, PPO, the procedure that trained the assistant you used this morning — each is a different opinion about how the agent should choose its action, and every one of them sits inside this same loop.

    The words, once and for all

    State
    The situation the agent is in right now, described well enough to decide what to do. In the room below, "which square am I standing on".
    Action
    One of the choices available. The agent picks exactly one per turn.
    Reward
    The number that comes back afterward. One number, not a report card.
    Observation
    What the agent actually gets to see. Sometimes that is the whole state, often it is less.
    Step
    One full trip around the loop: see, act, receive. The unit of time in this whole class.
    Episode
    A run from a starting situation until things stop. One game, one delivery, one conversation.
    Terminal state
    A state where the episode ends. Nothing happens after it; there is no next action and no next reward.
    Trajectory
    The written record of an episode: everything that was seen, done and received, in order.

    The room

    You need somewhere concrete to put all of this, so here is the world we will keep coming back to for the rest of the class. It is small on purpose: small enough that you can work out the right answer in your head, which means you can always check whether an algorithm got it right.

    Figure 2.2 — The room: four columns, three rows, one wall block. A small robot starts bottom-left and has four actions everywhere: up, down, left, right. The blue arrows show one episode — right, right, right, up — which ends at the stairs. That is a legal episode and a bad one, and the whole class is about telling those apart.

    Twelve squares, one of them a wall, so eleven states. Four actions. Two terminal states, the charger and the stairs. Everything in Parts I and II will be worked out on exactly this board.

    One step, written down

    Now the symbols. Nothing new is being introduced here — this is Figure 2.1 written in a form you can do arithmetic with.

    One step of the loop
    st → at → ( Rt+1 , st+1 )
    t Time, counted in steps, not seconds. Step 0 is the first one. A step is one trip around the loop.
    st The state at time t — the square the robot is standing on when it is about to choose.
    at The action it chooses at time t. One of up, down, left, right.
    Rt+1 The reward that comes back. Note the t+1: the payment for acting at time t is stamped with the following tick, because it does not exist until the world has responded.
    st+1 The next state — where the robot is standing once the action has taken effect.
    → Read it as "leads to". The state leads to a choice of action; the action leads to the pair on the right.

    String those together and you get the record of a whole episode. That record has a name, trajectory, and a symbol, τ (the Greek letter tau). It is worth taking seriously, because a pile of trajectories is the only data an RL agent ever has. Where a supervised learner is handed a table of examples, an agent is handed nothing and has to go out and generate these sequences itself.

    A trajectory
    τ = ( s0, a0, R1, s1, a1, R2, s2, … , sT )
    τ The trajectory: the full ordered record of one episode. Also called a rollout or a sample path.
    s0 The state the episode starts in. For the room, the bottom-left square.
    a0 The very first action taken.
    R1 The first reward. It is numbered 1, not 0, and it belongs to a0. There is no R0.
    s1 The state that a0 landed the agent in.
    a1, R2, s2 The same three things one step later: the action taken from s1, the reward it earns, and where it leads. The pattern then repeats.
    … Keep alternating state, action, reward for as long as the episode runs.
    T The time of the last step, so sT is the terminal state. Different episodes have different T.

    Figure 2.3 — The same four steps as Figure 2.2, laid out in time. Follow the dashed arrow: the agent acts at a0 and the consequence is filed under R1. Everything on the reward row is shifted one column right, which is why there is a blank under s0 and no reward at all after the terminal state. Lesson 3 makes this counting convention official.

    Check yourself

    In the episode above, which action was the −1 actually caused by?

    • a4 — the action taken in the terminal state.
    • a3 — the "up" that walked into the stairs.
    • a0 — the first move set the whole thing up.
    • None of them; rewards are handed out by the environment at random.
    Answer: B. Subtract one from the reward's index to find the action that caused it: R4 belongs to a3. Option A does not exist — s4 is terminal, so no action is taken there. Option C is the interesting wrong answer: the first move genuinely did contribute, but that is a question about blame spread over time, not about which step the number arrived on. Sorting out that difference is the credit-assignment problem, and Lesson 3 starts the machinery for it.

    Where the agent stops and the world starts

    Ask someone to draw the line between agent and environment and they will draw it around the robot. That is the wrong place, and the right place is genuinely useful to know.

    The rule is: anything the agent cannot change at will is environment. The agent is only the decision rule. Its motors are not part of it — the agent can command "turn the left wheel", but whether the wheel turns, how fast, and whether it slips on the tile is not up to the agent. The motors respond to commands exactly the way the floor does.

    Figure 2.4 — The robot's motors, sensors and battery sit on the environment side, next to the walls. The agent is the thin thing in the blue box: a rule that turns what it sees into what it does. Draw the boundary anywhere else and you end up with an "agent" that can simply decide to have a full battery, which would make the problem trivial and useless.

    Where people get lost

    The boundary is not physical and it is not fixed. You are allowed to redraw it, and where you draw it changes the problem. If you treat joint torques as the actions, the agent must learn physics. If you treat "walk to the door" as an action and let a controller handle the torques, the agent has an easier job and a coarser one. Neither is wrong. But you must decide, and then be consistent, because everything else — the state, the reward, the whole loop — is defined relative to that line.

    Check yourself

    A chess program's clock is running low. Is the clock part of the agent or part of the environment?

    • The agent, because it is inside the program.
    • The environment, because the program cannot set the clock to whatever it likes.
    • Neither — clocks are not states.
    • It depends on whether the program is winning.
    Answer: B. Apply the test: can the agent change it at will? It can spend the clock by thinking, but it cannot set it. So the clock is environment, and the remaining time belongs in the state — otherwise the agent has no way to learn "move faster when short on time". Option A is the common trap: running on the same machine has nothing to do with it.

    Endings, and things that never end

    Some tasks stop. A chess game ends, a delivery finishes, the robot reaches the charger. These are episodic tasks: the agent runs from a start state to a terminal state, then everything resets and it goes again. Episodes are convenient, because "how well did that go" has a definite answer once the episode is over.

    Other tasks never stop. A thermostat regulating a building, a system routing traffic, a robot that is simply meant to keep going. These are continuing tasks. There is no terminal state, no reset, and no moment at which you can add up the score, because the sum never finishes. That turns out to be a real technical problem, and Lesson 3 exists partly to solve it.

    In practice the two blur. A continuing task is often chopped into fixed-length chunks just so training has somewhere to stop and take stock, and an episodic task can be made to run forever by resetting the moment it ends. What matters is not which label you use but whether there is a genuine terminal state, because a terminal state is the one place where the future is definitely empty — and that fact is what lets an agent ever be certain of anything.

    TaskKindTerminal state
    The robot crossing the roomEpisodicCharger or stairs
    A game of chessEpisodicCheckmate, resignation or draw
    Generating one reply from a chatbotEpisodicThe end-of-message token
    Keeping a datacenter coolContinuingNone
    Balancing a pole, run indefinitelyContinuingNone

    State, or just a glimpse of it

    So far we have quietly assumed the agent sees the state. Often it does not. A robot with a forward-facing camera in the room sees a wall, and that same view occurs from two different squares — the observation is the same even though the state is not. When observation and state are the same thing, life is easy and everything in Parts I and II applies directly. When they are not, the agent has to remember, and its history becomes part of what it must carry around. We will keep to the easy case until it stops being reasonable, and we will say so when that happens.

    Check yourself

    Why can you not simply add up all the rewards in a continuing task?

    • Because rewards in continuing tasks are always zero.
    • Because the agent forgets earlier rewards.
    • Because there is no last step, so the sum has infinitely many terms and can be infinite.
    • Because continuing tasks have no reward function at all.
    Answer: C. A sum with no final term need not settle on a number. Worse, if two different policies both earn an infinite total, you cannot say which is better — and comparing behaviors is the entire point. Option B sounds plausible but memory is not the issue; the arithmetic itself fails. The fix, discounting, is the first thing Lesson 3 builds.

    Think it through

    Someone is learning to cook. Name the state, one action, and a reward. Then name one thing that feels like part of the cook but really belongs on the environment side.

    State: what is in the pan right now, how hot the burner is, how long it has been, what ingredients are left. Action: add salt, lower the heat, stir, take it off. Reward: how the finished dish tastes — one number, arriving at the very end of the episode, which makes it a hard credit-assignment problem.

    On the environment side despite feeling internal: their sense of taste, their knife skills, how steady their hands are. The cook can command "chop finely" but cannot decide to have a steadier hand. Same test as the robot's motors — those abilities respond to commands, they do not obey wishes. The only thing on the agent side is the decision rule: given what is in the pan, what do I do next.

    What to remember

    1. One arrow out, two arrows back. The agent sends an action; the environment returns a reward and a next state. Keep those two separate.
    2. A step is one trip around the loop. An episode is a run of steps ending in a terminal state; the written record of one is a trajectory τ.
    3. Rewards are numbered one tick late. The action at earns Rt+1, because the number does not exist until the world has answered.
    4. The boundary is control, not skin. Anything the agent cannot change at will — including its own motors — is environment.
    5. Episodic tasks end, continuing tasks do not. That difference is what makes summing rewards awkward, and it is where Lesson 3 begins.

    Lesson 3

    Reward, Return, and Discounting

    An agent does not chase rewards. It chases the total of all the rewards still to come, with the far-off ones counted less than the near ones. Those two sentences contain a distinction and a piece of arithmetic, and every algorithm in this class is built on both.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say precisely what separates a reward from a return
    • Explain why an undiscounted total breaks on a task that never ends
    • Compute a discounted return by hand, one multiplication at a time
    • Predict how an agent behaves at γ = 0, 0.5, 0.9 and 0.99
    • Write the recursive form of the return and say why it matters

    One number, every step

    After each action the environment sends back exactly one number. Not a list of scores for different aspects, not a paragraph of feedback — one number, which may be positive, negative or zero.

    That is a deliberate and quite aggressive design choice, and it has a name: the reward hypothesis. It claims that everything we mean by a goal or a purpose can be written as the maximization of the total of a single received number. Not "usually can" — the whole field is built as if this is always true.

    Before anything else, pin down the timing, because half the confusion in this subject comes from getting it wrong.

    The timing convention

    The reward for the action taken at time t arrives as Rt+1, never Rt. The number does not exist until the environment has responded, so it is stamped with the tick on which it arrives. There is no R0. Every formula from here to Lesson 16 obeys this.

    Now, the room. So far we have treated an ordinary step as worth 0. Let us make it slightly more honest: every ordinary step costs −0.04, the charger pays +1, and the stairs pay −1. The small negative is not decoration. It is the only reason the robot has to hurry; with 0 per step, dawdling forever in the corner is exactly as good as arriving, because both eventually collect the same +1.

    Which brings up the thing that goes wrong most often in practice. The agent does not optimize what you meant. It optimizes the number you actually wrote down.

    The gap between what you wrote and what you meant

    A famous case: a boat-racing game where points came from hitting targets scattered along the course. The intended behavior was "race well and you will score well". What the agent found was a small lagoon where three targets respawned in a loop. It drove in circles there, on fire, repeatedly crashing, collecting points forever, and never finished a single race. It scored far higher than any human. It was also perfectly correct — it maximized the stated reward.

    This is called reward hacking, and it is not a rare bug. It is the default outcome whenever the measurable thing and the intended thing come apart. Lesson 15 shows it happening to language models, where the fix costs real engineering.

    Reward is not return

    Here is the distinction the whole lesson turns on.

    A reward is what arrives on one step: a single number, already in the past. A return is the total of every reward still to come, counted from now onward. Reward is today's pay. Return is everything you will ever earn from today until you stop. An agent that maximized reward would take whatever pays most this second; an agent that maximizes return will accept −0.04 four times in a row to reach a +1.

    The return gets its own letter, G, and its own subscript, because "the total from here on" depends on where here is.

    The return, with nothing discounted
    Gt = Rt+1 + Rt+2 + Rt+3 + … + RT
    Gt The return from time t onward: the total score the agent still has coming. G is for gain.
    t The step you are standing on. Everything before it is spent and gone — the return never looks backward.
    Rt+1 The very next reward, the one earned by the action you are about to take.
    Rt+2, Rt+3 The reward after that, and the one after that. Each term sits a full step further into the future than the last.
    … Keep adding one term per remaining step.
    T The time of the final step of the episode, as in Lesson 2.
    RT The last reward, arriving as the agent enters the terminal state. This term is what makes the sum finite.

    That definition works perfectly for episodic tasks, and for a while it is all you need. It falls apart the moment the task does not end.

    If there is no T, the sum has infinitely many terms. A thermostat earning +1 every hour forever has a return of infinity. A worse thermostat earning +0.5 every hour forever also has a return of infinity. The agent now has no way to tell them apart, and comparing behaviors was the entire point.

    Check yourself

    The robot is one step from the charger. It takes that step and receives +1, ending the episode. What was Gt at the moment before it moved, ignoring discounting?

    • +1 — that is the only reward left, so the return equals it.
    • 0 — no reward had arrived yet at time t.
    • It cannot be computed until the next episode starts.
    • −0.04, the cost of taking the step.
    Answer: A. The return counts forward, so on the last step reward and return happen to coincide — which is exactly why they get confused. Option B applies the definition of reward, not return. Option D is the trap: the step into a terminal state pays the terminal reward, and even if it also cost −0.04 you would still be adding, not choosing.

    Discounting: the same reward, worth less later

    The fix is one Greek letter. Pick a number γ (gamma) between 0 and 1, called the discount factor. Multiply the next reward by 1, the one after by γ, the one after that by γ squared, and so on. Rewards further away shrink, and the sum settles down to a finite number even if it never stops.

    The discounted return
    Gt = Rt+1 + γ Rt+2 + γ2Rt+3 + γ3Rt+4 + …
    = Σk=0∞ γk Rt+k+1
    Gt The discounted return from time t. Same idea as before, with later terms shrunk.
    γ The discount factor, a fixed number with 0 ≤ γ ≤ 1. You choose it once, before training, and it never changes.
    γ2, γ3 γ times itself, then times itself again. A reward two steps further away has been shrunk twice, three steps three times.
    Rt+1 The next reward. It carries no discount at all — its multiplier is γ0, which is 1.
    Rt+2, Rt+3, Rt+4 The rewards further out, each paired with one more power of γ.
    … Carry on the same way for every remaining step.
    Σ Capital sigma: "add up everything that follows, once for each value of k". It is shorthand for the line above it, not a new idea.
    k The counter. It starts at 0 and goes up by one each term. Read it as "how many steps beyond the next one".
    ∞ Keep going forever. In an episodic task the rewards after the terminal state are all 0, so the sum quietly stops on its own.
    γk γ multiplied by itself k times: the weight on the reward that is k steps beyond the next one.
    Rt+k+1 The reward at that distance. The +1 is the timing convention again; the first term (k = 0) is Rt+1.

    The weights are just γ multiplied by itself over and over. With γ = 0.9:

    • γ0 = 1 — anything to the power 0 is 1
    • γ1 = 0.9
    • γ2 = 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81
    • γ3 = 0.81 × 0.9 = 0.729

    Each weight is the one above it times 0.9. That is all a power is: repeated multiplication, one factor per step of delay.

    Worked example, one line at a time

    An agent is about to collect four rewards: 2, then 1, then 4, then 10. Take γ = 0.9 and work out G0.

    RewardValueWeightMultiplicationCounts as
    R1212 × 12
    R210.91 × 0.90.9
    R340.814 × 0.813.24
    R4100.72910 × 0.7297.29

    Check the two that are not obvious. Four lots of 0.81: 0.81 + 0.81 = 1.62, and 1.62 + 1.62 = 3.24. Ten times 0.729 is just 0.729 with the decimal point moved one place right: 7.29. Then add down the last column — 2 + 0.9 is 2.9, plus 3.24 is 6.14, plus 7.29 is 13.43.

    Undiscounted the same four rewards are worth 2 + 1 + 4 + 10 = 17. The discount cost the agent 3.57 of paper value, all of it taken from the two rewards furthest away.

    Answer: C. Match it to the sum: Rt+k+1 with t+k+1 = t+4 means k = 3, so the weight is γ3 = 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125, and 8 × 0.125 = 1.     How far ahead the agent looks

    Choosing γ is choosing how far-sighted the agent is, and the effect on behavior is dramatic.

    γBehaviorRough horizon
    0Completely short-sighted. Only Rt+1 survives; every later term is multiplied by zero. The agent cannot plan at all.1 step
    0.5Impatient. A reward ten steps away is worth about a thousandth of its face value, so effectively invisible.2 steps
    0.9The common default for small problems. Plans over roughly a dozen steps; enough for the room.10 steps
    0.99Patient. Used when the payoff is far away — long games, long documents. Slower and noisier to learn.100 steps
    1No discounting. Fine for episodes that reliably end, broken for tasks that do not.the whole episode

    That last column comes from a rule of thumb worth memorizing: the effective horizon is about 1 / (1 − γ) steps. For γ = 0.9 that is 1 / 0.1 = 10; for γ = 0.99 it is 1 / 0.01 = 100. Beyond that distance rewards have faded enough that they barely steer the agent. It is not an exact cutoff, but when someone says "we raised gamma to 0.99" this is the number they have in mind.

    Figure 3.2 — Sixteen steps of future, three discount factors, same first bar every time. At γ = 0.5 the agent is blind past step five. At γ = 0.99 the sixteenth step still counts for about 86 percent of face value, which is why patient agents can chase distant payoffs — and also why they take much longer to train, since a mistake anywhere in a hundred steps muddies the signal.

    Think of it like

    An interest rate, run backwards. A hundred dollars promised next year is worth less to you today than a hundred dollars in your hand, and a hundred dollars promised in thirty years is worth less again. Nobody finds that mysterious. γ is the same shrink-per-year applied per step, and the reasons overlap: the future is uncertain, you might not be around to collect, and having it now lets you do something with it.

    Now put it all on the board. Here is a full episode in the room: the robot goes up, up, right, right, right, dodging the wall and reaching the charger in five steps.

    Figure 3.3 — Read it downward. Each reward is multiplied by its own weight, and the bottom row is added up to give G0 = 0.519. The four step costs barely matter once discounted, but they are what stops the robot wandering; the +1 has lost a third of its value purely by being five steps away. Start the robot three steps from the charger instead and the same +1 would be worth 0.81.

    The one line the rest of the class is built on

    Look at the discounted return again and pull out a common factor. The return from now is the next reward, plus γ times the whole return from one step later. Every term after the first already carries at least one γ, so it factors straight out.

    The recursive form of the return
    Gt = Rt+1 + γ Gt+1
    Gt The return from now: everything still to come, discounted.
    Rt+1 The one reward you are about to receive. Undiscounted, because it is not in the future — it is next.
    γ The discount factor, applied once, to everything beyond the next reward.
    Gt+1 The return from one step later — the same quantity, asked one tick further along. This is what makes the equation recursive.

    It looks like an accounting trick. It is the most important line in the class.

    Every term after the first shares a factor of γ: γRt+2 + γ2Rt+3 + … is γ times (Rt+2 + γRt+3 + …), and the thing in brackets is the definition of Gt+1. That is the whole derivation.

    The one thing to keep

    A quantity defined over an infinite future has just been rewritten in terms of one reward and one lookup of the same quantity one step later. That means an agent can improve its estimate after a single step, without waiting for the episode to end or knowing what happens beyond the next square. Take an average of both sides and you get the Bellman equation (Lesson 7). Replace the right-hand side with a guess and you get temporal-difference learning (Lesson 9), which is the engine underneath Q-learning, DQN, PPO and every language-model method in Part V. All of Part II is this line, elaborated.

    Think it through

    The room charges −0.04 for every ordinary step. What behavior does that small negative number buy, and what would change if you set it to 0 instead? What would change if you set it to −0.5?

    At −0.04 the robot has a reason to hurry. Every extra step it wastes shaves a little off the return, so among all the routes that reach the charger it prefers a short one. The number is small enough that it will still take a longer route to stay away from the stairs, because −1 dwarfs a few −0.04s. That balance is the whole design.

    At 0, all routes to the charger score the same, so the robot has no reason to prefer a short one. Discounting alone would still favor arriving sooner — a +1 five steps away beats a +1 fifty steps away — but the pressure is much weaker, and with γ = 1 it vanishes entirely.

    At −0.5 the arithmetic flips. Five steps of −0.5 is −2.5, worse than walking straight into the stairs for −1. The fastest way to stop losing money is to end the episode immediately, so the robot learns to dive down the stairs on purpose. You wanted a brisk robot and you wrote down a suicidal one — the boat in the lagoon, in miniature.

    What to remember

    1. Reward is one step, return is all the steps left. Agents maximize return. Confusing the two makes every later equation unreadable.
    2. The reward for acting at t is Rt+1. There is no R0, and this convention never changes.
    3. Discounting multiplies the reward k steps beyond the next by γk. It keeps infinite sums finite and sets how patient the agent is: the horizon is roughly 1 / (1 − γ) steps, so 0.9 sees about ten and 0.99 about a hundred.
    4. Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1 is the seed of Part II. One reward plus a discounted lookup of the same quantity, one step later. Everything else is built on it.
    5. The agent optimizes the number you wrote, not the goal you had. Reward hacking is the default, not the exception.

    Lesson 4

    Markov Decision Processes

    You now have a loop, a stream of rewards, and a way to add them up. This lesson draws a box around all of it and gives the box a name. It also asks one sharp question that decides whether any machinery in this class will work on your problem: does your state tell you enough?

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Name the five parts of an MDP and say what each one is for
    • State the Markov property in plain words
    • Look at a proposed state and judge whether it is Markov
    • Repair a state that is not Markov, and say what the repair costs
    • Read a transition probability off a table

    Five things, and that is the whole problem

    Written down formally, everything from the last two lessons becomes a single object with a heavy name: a Markov Decision Process, or MDP. Nearly every algorithm in this class is an algorithm for solving one.

    An MDP is five things. That is not a summary — it is the complete list.

    Figure 4.1 — Every problem in this class is one of these five-part objects. Notice how little of it belongs to the agent: the agent only ever picks from A. The rest is either the world's business (S, P, R) or a dial you turn before training starts (γ).

    Read the name backwards and it explains itself. Process: things unfold over time, one step after another. Decision: at every step somebody chooses, and that somebody is the agent. Markov: the state has a particular property, which is the rest of this lesson.

    Why bother with the formality

    Once your problem is written as an MDP you inherit everything anyone has ever proved about MDPs, for free. In practice, applying RL to a new problem is almost entirely the work of deciding what S, A, and R should be. Get those wrong and no algorithm can save you.

    The Markov property

    Here is the claim in one sentence: the current state tells you everything you need to know, and how you got there is irrelevant.

    That is a claim about the state, not about the world. The world is whatever it is. You are the one who decides what goes into the state, and your choice is either good enough or it is not.

    Start with a state that passes. A chess position is Markov. Hand a grandmaster a board in the middle of a game with no record of the moves that produced it, and they lose nothing at all: every legal move, every threat, every evaluation is computable from the pieces sitting in front of them. Knowing that the white knight arrived via f3 rather than e2 changes nothing about what happens next. The path is genuinely irrelevant.

    The Markov property
    Pr{ St+1 = s′ | St, At } = Pr{ St+1 = s′ | S0, A0, …, St, At }
    Pr{ … } "The probability that the thing inside happens." A number between 0 and 1.
    | Read as "given that". Everything to the right of the bar is what you already know when you ask the question.
    St+1 The state one step from now — the thing you are trying to predict.
    s′ A particular next state you might land in. The little mark is read "s-prime" and just means "some other state", not a new kind of thing.
    St, At The state you are in right now and the action you are taking right now — the only two things the left-hand side is allowed to look at.
    S0, A0, … The entire history: every state and action since the episode began. The right-hand side is allowed to look at all of it, and gets the same answer anyway.
    St, At Capital letters mark the state and action as random variables: which one occurs is not yet settled. Lowercase s and a name one particular value, as in Lesson 2's trajectory.

    The equation says the two sides are the same number. Adding the whole history to the right of the bar changes your prediction by nothing. If that holds, the state is Markov.

    Now the state that fails

    Take a single photograph of a ball in mid-air. The photo tells you the ball is exactly two meters off the ground and three meters from the wall. Call that the state: position.

    Now predict where the ball will be one twentieth of a second later. You cannot. If it was thrown upward it will be higher. If it was falling it will be lower. If it was struck sideways it will be a hand's width to the left. All three of those futures produce exactly the same photograph, so nothing in the state can tell them apart. Any rule that reads only the state must give the same answer for all three, and it will be wrong at least twice.

    Now take two photographs a twentieth of a second apart. Subtract the two positions and you have the velocity. With position and velocity together, high-school physics gives you one answer and only one answer for where the ball goes next.

    Figure 4.2 — The same physical ball, two different states. On the left the state records only where the ball is, and three completely different futures are consistent with that, so no rule reading only the state could ever predict the next position. On the right velocity has been added and exactly one future survives. Markov is not a fact about balls. It is a fact about what you chose to write down.

    Where people get lost

    People hear "Markov" and think it is a property of the environment — that some worlds are Markov and others are not. It is not. Figure 4.2 shows one world and two states, one Markov and one not. If your problem seems not to be Markov, the honest description is that your state is missing something, and your job is to find out what.

    The chess footnote

    A chess position is not quite the pieces alone. The official position also records whether each side may still castle, whether an en-passant capture is available this turn, and how many moves have passed without a capture. Every one of those is a scrap of history that had to be smuggled into the state to make it Markov — which is exactly the repair the next section is about, performed centuries before anyone wrote down an MDP.

    Check yourself

    A thermostat's state is "the current room temperature". Is it Markov for the purpose of predicting the temperature one minute from now?

    • Yes — temperature is a physical measurement, so it is a real state.
    • No — a room at 20 degrees and rising behaves differently from a room at 20 degrees and falling, and the state cannot tell them apart.
    • No — because temperature is a continuous number rather than a discrete one.
    • Yes — as long as the thermostat reads the temperature accurately.
    Answer: B. This is the ball again wearing a different hat: one number, two futures. Whether the heating has just switched on is not recoverable from the temperature alone, so the state fails the test. Options A and D both make the same mistake — accuracy is not the issue, sufficiency is. A perfectly measured 20.000 degrees is still ambiguous. Option C confuses Markov with discrete; continuous states can be perfectly Markov, as position plus velocity is.

    Repairing a state, and what the repair costs

    The fix is always the same shape: take something from the history and staple it onto the state. The ball needed velocity. Chess needed castling rights. A robot that can slip needs to know which way it was already moving.

    The best-known version of this trick is frame stacking. A single frame of a video game does not tell you which way the ball is moving — the identical picture appears on the way in and on the way out. So instead of feeding the agent one frame, you feed it the last four, glued together, and call that the state. Motion becomes visible, and the state becomes Markov enough to work with.

    The cost is not small. Suppose a single frame can be in N different configurations. Four stacked frames can be in N4. You paid four times as much memory per state, and in exchange the number of distinct states went from N to N4. Every repair has this shape: a little more information in the state, an enormous number of extra states to learn about.

    The one thing to keep

    Markov is a design decision, not a discovery. You choose what goes in the state, and you are trading two things off against each other: leave something out and your agent cannot predict; put too much in and it has too much to learn.

    Sometimes you cannot repair it at all. In poker you never get to see the opponent's cards, no matter what you staple to the state. Those problems are called partially observable, they are genuinely harder, and the practical answer is to build the best state you can from what you can observe and accept that it is an approximation. Everything in this class assumes the repair worked.

    What the world does back

    Two of the five pieces describe the world's response, and both are written as functions of "where you are and what you did".

    The first is P, the transition function. Give it a state, an action, and a candidate next state, and it hands back a probability.

    The transition function
    P(s′ | s, a) = Pr{ St+1 = s′ | St = s, At = a }
    P The transition function. Also called the model, or the dynamics. It is the rulebook of the world.
    s The state you are in now.
    a The action you take from that state.
    s′ One particular state you might land in next.
    | "Given that". Everything right of the bar is fixed and known; s′ on the left is the uncertain part.
    St+1 The state at the next tick. For a fixed s and a, the probabilities across all possible s′ add up to exactly 1 — something has to happen.

    Concretely: the room's floor is slippery. When the robot is told to go up, it goes up eight times in ten. One time in ten it slides to the left instead, and one time in ten to the right. It never goes backwards.

    Figure 4.3 — Naming squares as (column, row) counting from the bottom-left, the robot sits at (1, 1) and is told to go up. Eight times in ten it arrives at (1, 2). The middle branch is the interesting one: slipping left from the leftmost column pushes the robot into the outer wall, so it stays exactly where it was. A "move" that changes nothing is still a transition, and it still costs a step.

    The same three lines written as a table, with a second action added so you can see the pattern. Read each row as "from here, doing this, you end up there, this often".

    FromActionLands inProbabilityWhy
    (1, 1)up(1, 2)0.8as commanded
    (1, 1)up(1, 1)0.1slipped left into the outer wall
    (1, 1)up(2, 1)0.1slipped right

    An environment where every action has exactly one outcome with probability 1 is called deterministic; chess and a Rubik's cube are like that. An environment with any spread at all, like this floor, is stochastic. Deterministic worlds are the easy special case, and everything below is written to cover both.

    The second function is the reward. It is written the same way, but instead of a probability it hands back the average reward you get for that move.

    The expected reward
    R(s, a) = 𝔼[ Rt+1 | St = s, At = a ]
    R(s, a) The expected reward for taking action a in state s. One number per state–action pair.
    𝔼[ … ] "The average value of the thing inside, over all the ways it could turn out." If the outcome is always the same, the average is just that value.
    Rt+1 The reward that actually arrives one tick after the action. The +1 is the timing convention from Lesson 3: you act at t, the score lands at t+1.
    St, At The state and action being asked about, fixed to s and a.

    Worked example

    Suppose every step in the room costs −0.04, and stepping into the charger pays +1 on top of that. Stand at (3, 3), directly left of the charger, and take the action "right". Eight times in ten you land on the charger and collect 0.96. The other two times you slip up into the ceiling or down to (3, 2) and collect −0.04. So

    R((3,3), right) = 0.8 × 0.96 + 0.2 × (−0.04) = 0.768 − 0.008 = 0.76

    Notice the agent never actually receives 0.76 on any single step. It receives either 0.96 or −0.04. The expectation is the number you would see if you averaged over many attempts, and it is the number the formulas work with.

    Think it through

    A colleague proposes that for a self-driving car, the state should be "the current image from the front camera". Give two reasons that state is not Markov, say what you would add, and name the price.

    First, the image gives no velocities. A photograph of the car ahead looks the same whether that car is parked or doing 60, and those two situations call for completely different actions. Second, the image says nothing about the car's own speed, or the state of the wheels, or whether the vehicle it just overtook is now in the blind spot. All of that is history the single frame threw away.

    The repair: stack the last few frames so motion becomes visible, and staple on the readings the camera cannot see — speed, steering angle, the planned route. The price is the usual one. Each thing you add multiplies the number of distinct states, so there is more to learn and less experience per state to learn it from.

    Worth saying honestly: even after all that, the state is an approximation. A truck still occludes whatever is behind it. Real problems are rarely perfectly Markov; the goal is to get close enough that the machinery works.

    What to remember

    1. An MDP is five things. States, actions, transitions, reward, discount. Write your problem in those terms and you have an RL problem.
    2. Markov means the present is sufficient. The future depends on where you are now, not on the road you took to get here.
    3. Markov is a property of your state, not of the world. A ball with position alone fails; the same ball with position and velocity passes.
    4. The repair is always to add information, and it always costs state space. Four stacked frames give you motion and N4 states.
    5. P(s′ | s, a) is the rulebook. For fixed s and a its probabilities add to 1, and the room's floor spreads them 0.8 / 0.1 / 0.1.

    Lesson 5

    Policies: The Agent's Strategy

    Four lessons have described the problem. This one introduces the answer. A policy is what an agent has instead of a plan, and it is the only thing reinforcement learning ever actually produces — everything else in this class is machinery for improving it.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say what a policy is, and why it is the thing being learned
    • Read π(a|s) and tell a deterministic policy from a stochastic one
    • Explain why deliberately random behavior is sometimes the right answer
    • Say, with numbers, why storing a policy as a table stops working
    • State the objective every algorithm in this class is maximizing

    A picture that is a policy

    Take the room and draw one arrow in every square: the move the robot should make if it ever finds itself standing there. Nine squares need an arrow — the wall is not a square you can stand on, and the charger and the stairs end the episode, so no decision is required in either.

    That drawing is a policy. Not a description of a policy, not a summary of one — the arrows are the object. Hand them to the robot and it can operate forever without any further instruction: look down, read the arrow, move, repeat.

    Figure 5.1 — The room, with the robot's start square highlighted at (1, 1) and the wall block as the one filled square. Look hard at the bottom-right square: its arrow points left, away from the charger. That is not a drawing error. Going up from (4, 1) walks straight into the stairs, so the policy detours left and takes the long way round. One square along, at (3, 1), the arrow points left for a subtler reason: going up there risks a sideways slip into the stairs. A policy is allowed to look wrong and be right.

    This is worth saying flatly, because it is easy to lose. Value functions, Bellman equations, Q-learning, PPO — every technique in the remaining eleven lessons exists to answer one question: which arrows? When somebody says a model "learned" something, the thing that changed is the policy.

    Think of it like

    A policy is not a route, it is a habit. A route says "go up, up, right, right" and falls apart the moment you end up somewhere you did not expect. A habit says "wherever you are, here is what you do", and it keeps working after a slip, a detour, or being picked up and put down in a different corner. Because the floor is slippery, the robot needs a habit.

    Two kinds of policy

    The simplest kind gives one answer per state and never varies. Written π(s) = a, it is called deterministic. Figure 5.1 is one of these: every square has exactly one arrow.

    The more general kind hands back a probability for each available action. Written π(a|s), it is called stochastic. The robot in state s consults the policy, gets a set of numbers, and rolls a die weighted by them.

    A policy
    π(a | s) = Pr{ At = a | St = s }
    π The policy. The Greek letter p, for "plan". It is the agent's entire behavior and the only thing being learned.
    a One particular action you might take.
    s The state you are currently in.
    | "Given that". The state is known; the action is the uncertain part. Note the order — state on the right, action on the left. People flip these constantly.
    At The action actually chosen at time t.
    St The state at time t. For any fixed s, the policy's numbers across all actions add up to exactly 1 — the agent always does something.

    Figure 5.2 — Two policies asked the same question about the same square. On the left the answer is a single action; on the right it is a set of probabilities, which here add to 0.15 + 0.70 + 0.15 = 1. A deterministic policy is just the special case where one of those numbers is 1 and the rest are 0, so the right-hand form covers both. Everything after this lesson is written in the right-hand form.

    Where people get lost

    The randomness in Figure 5.2 is the agent's, and it is a completely separate thing from the slippery floor in Lesson 4. The floor is randomness in P: you chose "up" and the world moved you sideways. The policy is randomness in π: you had not decided what to choose. A world can be perfectly deterministic and still be played by a stochastic policy, and the other way round.

    Why anyone would want a random policy

    Two reasons, and they are unrelated to each other.

    The first is exploration. A deterministic policy in a fixed world produces the same episode forever. If the arrow at (2, 1) says left, the robot will never once find out what right would have done, so it can never discover that right was better. A dash of randomness is how an agent generates the evidence it needs to improve. Lesson 10 makes this precise.

    The second is adversaries. Play rock-paper-scissors with a deterministic policy — always rock, say. Against a random opponent you break even. Against an opponent who watches you for three rounds, you then lose every single game from round four onward, forever. There is no deterministic policy that avoids this, because every deterministic policy is a pattern. Play each option with probability one third and your expected score is exactly 0 no matter what the opponent does, including if they can read your mind. Here the optimal policy is genuinely, irreducibly random.

    Check yourself

    A stochastic policy in the room gives up 0.70, left 0.15, right 0.15 at square (3, 1). What does the robot do the next time it stands there?

    • It moves up, because up has the highest probability.
    • It draws at random with those weights, so up about seven times in ten and the others about three times in twenty each.
    • It averages the three moves into a single diagonal move.
    • It tries all three and keeps the best outcome.
    Answer: B. Option A describes a different policy — the greedy one you could build from these numbers — but the policy as written is the numbers themselves, and 0.15 means it really does go left sometimes. Option C is a common instinct and is wrong: probabilities weight the choice, they do not blend the actions, and "0.15 of a step left" is not a move the robot has. Option D would require rewinding the world, which no agent can do.

    Where the lookup table runs out

    A policy has to live somewhere. The obvious home is a table: one row per state, one column per action, a probability in each cell. That is called a tabular policy, and for the room it is eleven rows of four numbers. Perfectly practical.

    Now count rows for a real problem. A Go board has 361 points, each empty, black, or white, so there are at most 3361 board configurations — roughly 10172. An Atari screen is 210 by 160 pixels, which is 33,600 pixels, each of which can take about 128 colors; that is on the order of 1070,000 distinct screens.

    For scale, the observable universe contains about 1080 atoms. Storing one Go row per atom, you would run out of universe after the first 1080 rows and still have 1092 universes to go. The table is not expensive. It is impossible.

    The way out is to stop storing the answer and start computing it. Keep a fixed, manageable pile of adjustable numbers — call them θ, the Greek letter theta — and define a function that takes a state in and produces action probabilities out. This is a parameterized policy, written πθ(a|s). Learning now means adjusting θ.

    Figure 5.3 — The table on the left needs one row per state, which is fine for the room's eleven and impossible for anything real. The box on the right stores a fixed number of adjustable knobs and computes the row you asked for on demand. Storage is only half the win. A table learns nothing about its second row from having seen its first; a function is forced to reuse what it learned, so a state it has never visited still gets a sensible answer.

    Vocabulary

    tabular
    The policy is stored as a literal table of numbers, one entry per state. Exact, simple, and limited to small worlds.
    parameterized
    The policy is a function with adjustable settings θ. Approximate, but the size of θ is your choice rather than the world's.
    generalization
    Getting a reasonable answer for a state you have never seen, because it resembles states you have. Tables cannot do this; functions can.

    What "best" means

    You now have an object that can be changed. What should it be changed toward? Here is the target, and it does not change for the rest of the class.

    The objective
    J(π) = 𝔼π[ G0 ] = 𝔼π[ Σk=0∞ γkRk+1 ]
    J(π) How good the policy π is: one number summarizing an entire way of behaving. Bigger is better.
    𝔼π[ … ] "The average of the thing inside, over many episodes run with this policy." The subscript matters: change π and you are averaging over a different set of episodes.
    G0 The return from step 0 — the whole discounted score of one episode, from Lesson 3.
    Σk=0∞ "Add up the following, letting k run 0, 1, 2, 3 and onward."
    γk The discount, multiplied by itself k times. With γ = 0.9 the fourth step counts for 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.729.
    Rk+1 The reward arriving one tick after step k — the usual timing convention.

    Read it in English: a policy's score is its average return — the discounted total of everything it will collect. Not the reward it got last time, not the reward on its best day — the average, over everything the slippery floor and its own dice can throw at it.

    Now the definition falls out. A policy π1 is at least as good as π2 if it scores at least as well, and an optimal policy — written π* and read "pi star" — is one that no other policy beats.

    The optimal policy
    π* = argmaxπ J(π)
    π* An optimal policy. The star means "the best one". There may be several that tie; any of them will do.
    argmaxπ "The π that makes what follows biggest." Note it returns the policy itself, not the score — max would return the score.
    J(π) The objective above: the average return of behaving as π says.

    Two facts about π* that are worth more than they look. First, for a finite MDP an optimal policy always exists. You might have expected a trade-off — policy X better in some squares, policy Y better in others, no clean winner — but no: there is always at least one policy that is at least as good as every other policy in every state at once. Second, at least one optimal policy is deterministic. When the world is a fixed MDP, dice buy you nothing at the finish line.

    The one thing to keep

    The policy is the answer, J(π) is the scoreboard, and π* is the top of it. Every algorithm in the remaining lessons is a different route up that scoreboard.

    Check yourself

    Policy A reaches the charger in four steps on its best run but falls down the stairs one run in three. Policy B always takes seven steps and never falls. Which has the higher J(π)?

    • A, because its best run is the fastest.
    • You cannot tell without averaging — J weighs the −1 falls against the +1 arrivals, and A's speed only helps through γ.
    • B, because avoiding the stairs is always correct.
    • They are equal, since both eventually reach the charger.
    Answer: B. The objective is an expectation, so a best run is worth nothing on its own — that is what makes option A wrong, and it is the most common misreading of J. Option C overcorrects: if γ is small enough, arriving three steps sooner can outweigh an occasional fall. Option D ignores the falls entirely. The honest answer is that you compute both averages and compare, which is exactly what the next three lessons build the tools for.

    Check yourself

    Why is a lookup table hopeless for a policy over Atari screens?

    • Because screens change too fast to store.
    • There are vastly more possible screens than atoms in the universe, and a table also learns nothing about one screen from any other.
    • Because tables can only hold deterministic policies.
    • Because the actions are continuous rather than discrete.
    Answer: B. Both halves matter, and the second half is the one people miss. Even with infinite storage, a table holds every state apart: shifting one pixel produces a brand-new row that has to be learned from scratch. Option C is false — a table can hold four probabilities per row perfectly well. Option D describes a real difficulty in other problems, but an Atari joystick has a small, discrete set of actions.

    Think it through

    Rock-paper-scissors has no good deterministic policy, but the room does. What is different about the two problems?

    The room does not adapt to you. Its transition probabilities were fixed before you arrived, and the floor never notices that you always go up from (1, 1). Because nothing is watching, being predictable costs nothing, and the guarantee above applies: some deterministic policy is optimal.

    Rock-paper-scissors puts another optimizer on the far side of the loop. Your opponent is not a fixed P — they are searching for your pattern, and any deterministic policy is a pattern. The moment they find it, your score collapses. Randomness is not a hedge here, it is the only thing that cannot be exploited.

    The lesson underneath: everything in this class assumes a fixed MDP. When the environment contains an adversary who learns, that assumption breaks, and the field that repairs it is called game theory. You will still want stochastic policies in the room, though — not because the floor is scheming, but because an agent that never varies never finds out what it is missing.

    What to remember

    1. The policy is the thing being learned. One arrow per square, and everything else in this class exists to decide which arrows.
    2. Deterministic is the special case. π(a|s) gives probabilities that sum to 1; a deterministic policy is one of them set to 1.
    3. Randomness buys exploration, and protection from adversaries. Always-rock loses every game once the opponent notices.
    4. Tables die on real problems. Go has about 10172 positions against the universe's 1080 atoms, so the policy becomes a function with knobs θ.
    5. The target is J(π) = 𝔼π[G0]. Average discounted return. An optimal π* always exists, and at least one of them is deterministic.

    Lesson 6

    Value Functions: V and Q

    Standing on a bare square of floor that pays you nothing, you can still be in a very good position — or a very bad one. Value functions are how reinforcement learning turns "good position" into a number, and almost every algorithm in the rest of this class is a way of computing one.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say what the value of a state means, and why it always comes attached to a policy
    • Tell V and Q apart, and say what each one refuses to tell you
    • Explain why an agent holding Q can act and an agent holding V cannot
    • Rebuild V from Q as a weighted average, on paper

    Some squares are better than others

    Put the robot back in the room: the 4x3 floor, the charger in the top-right corner paying +1, the stairs directly below it costing −1, one wall block in the middle. Reaching either the charger or the stairs ends the episode. Every other step pays exactly 0 — we set Lesson 3's −0.04 step cost aside for the rest of Part II so that every number below can be checked by hand.

    Now ask the only question that matters when you are standing somewhere: is this a good place to be? It cannot mean "this square pays well", because almost no square pays anything. It has to mean "this square leads somewhere good."

    That is exactly what a value function measures. The value of a state is the return you can expect from that state onward — the discounted total of all future reward, from here to the end of the episode.

    Two riders come attached, and both matter more than they look.

    Value is always measured under some policy. A square is not good or bad by itself; it is good if you play well from it. The square one step from the charger is worth a lot to a robot that steps onto the charger and almost nothing to a robot that spins in circles. Change the policy and every number changes.

    Value is an average, not a promise. It is what you get on average over all the ways the future could unfold. A single episode can beat it or miss it badly.

    The state-value function
    Vπ(s) = 𝔼π[ Gt | St = s ]
    Vπ(s) The value of state s under policy π. One number per square of the room.
    π The policy from Lesson 5 — the rule the agent uses to pick actions. It sits in the superscript because the number is only meaningful relative to it.
    𝔼π[ … ] "The average value of, if the agent behaves according to π." Read 𝔼 as expected: add up every way the future could go, weighted by how likely each one is.
    Gt The return from Lesson 3: Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ2Rt+3 + … — the discounted total of everything still to come.
    St = s The condition: "given that at time t we are standing in s". Everything to the left of the bar is averaged over; this part is fixed.

    Let us put real numbers on the room. To keep every step checkable by hand, take the floor to be deterministic here — a commanded move always happens — with γ = 0.9, and let the policy be the obvious one: always take a shortest path to the charger without walking into the stairs.

    Figure 6.1 — Value bleeding outward from the charger. The squares are worth powers of 0.9, one factor per step of distance, because the +1 arrives later and later. Look hard at the bottom-right square (4,1): it is physically closer to the charger than (3,1) is, and yet it is worth less. Geography is not value. The direct route up from (4,1) runs into the stairs, so the policy has to walk around, and the extra step costs a factor of 0.9.

    You can check any square in one line. Take (2,3), worth 0.90. From there the policy steps right to (3,3), collecting 0 on the way; (3,3) is worth 1.00; and 0 + 0.9 × 1.00 = 0.90. Every number in the grid was built that way, and Lesson 7 turns that one line into an equation.

    The discount is doing visible work here. With γ = 0.5 instead, the same squares would read 1.00, 0.50, 0.25, 0.13, 0.06 — the far corner of the room would be nearly worthless and the robot would behave as though only the next step or two existed.

    Check yourself

    Square (4,1) sits two moves below the charger. Square (3,1) sits three moves away. Why is (4,1) worth less?

    • The numbers in Figure 6.1 are approximate, so small differences do not mean much.
    • The short route from (4,1) goes through the stairs, so the policy detours and actually takes more steps.
    • Squares in the bottom row are always worth less than squares above them.
    • The discount is applied twice in the corner.
    Answer: B. Value counts steps the policy will actually take, not steps on a ruler. From (4,1) the policy must go left, up, up, right — four steps, hence 0.93 = 0.73. From (3,1) it goes up, up, right — three steps, 0.92 = 0.81. Option A is worth resisting: these numbers are exact, not estimates.

    Q: the number goes on the move, not on the square

    Here is the trouble with V. It tells you that (4,1) is worth 0.73. Fine. Now you are standing in (4,1) and you have to do something. Which of the four moves? The number 0.73 does not contain that information. It is one number for the whole square, and there are four different ways to leave the square.

    So we put the number somewhere more useful. The action-value of an action in a state — written Q — is the return you expect if you take that specific action first, and only afterward go back to following the policy.

    The action-value function
    Qπ(s, a) = 𝔼π[ Gt | St = s, At = a ]
    Qπ(s, a) The action-value of doing a in s and then following π forever after. One number per state-and-action pair — four per square in the room.
    a One specific action: up, down, left or right. Not chosen by the policy — chosen by you, for this one step.
    At = a The extra condition, and the whole difference from Vπ: the first action is pinned to a, even if π would never have chosen it.
    St = s Same as before: we start in state s.
    𝔼π[ Gt ] Still the expected return — the discounted total of all future reward, averaged over what could happen.

    Compute all four for the corner square (4,1), using the values already in Figure 6.1. Nothing here is harder than one multiplication.

    ActionWhere it landsRewardThen worthQ
    upthe stairs — episode over−1nothing follows−1.00
    left(3,1), worth 0.8100.9 × 0.810.73
    rightblocked by the outer wall, stays in (4,1)00.9 × 0.730.66
    downblocked by the outer wall, stays in (4,1)00.9 × 0.730.66

    Look at the spread. The square is worth 0.73, and one of the four things you can do in it is worth −1.00. The state's own value told you none of that.

    Figure 6.2 — The same square, twice. V hangs one number on the square itself; Q hangs a number on every arrow leaving it. The left panel says the corner is a reasonable place to be. The right panel says the corner is a reasonable place to be and that one of your four options destroys the episode. Same square, same policy, same discount — only the place the number is attached has changed.

    Where people get lost

    Qπ(s, a) is not the reward for doing a. It is the reward for doing a plus everything that follows for the rest of the episode. Going left from (4,1) pays 0 immediately; its Q is 0.73 entirely because of what comes after.

    And the second half of the definition trips up just as many people: after that first pinned action, the agent goes back to following π. So Qπ for a bad action does not assume you keep being bad. It assumes one deviation, then business as usual.

    Why Q is the one you can act on

    Give an agent the complete V table for the room and drop it in a square. It knows its own square is worth 0.73. To choose, it would have to answer: if I go up, where do I end up, and what does that pay? That question is about the room, not about the values — it is the transition function P and the reward from Lesson 4. Without a map of the room, the V table is unusable for choosing.

    Give the same agent the Q table instead and it needs nothing else. Read the four numbers attached to the square you are in, take the largest, move. The lookahead has already been done and baked into the numbers.

    Choosing an action from Q, with no map
    a* = argmaxa Qπ(s, a)
    a* The action to take. The star is just a name tag meaning "the winner of this comparison".
    argmaxa "The a that makes what follows biggest." Not the biggest value itself — the action that achieves it. Compare max, which returns the number.
    Qπ(s, a) The four numbers being compared: the action-values of every action available in s.
    s The state you are standing in right now. Notice what is absent from this line: no P, no reward function, no model of the room at all.

    Think of it like

    A chess engine's evaluation of a position versus its evaluation of a move. Position evaluations are V: "white is up about 0.8 of a pawn here." Useful, but you cannot play from it — to pick a move you would have to make each candidate move on the board, look at the resulting position, and compare. That requires knowing the rules of chess.

    Move evaluations are Q: "Nf3 is worth 0.9, Qh5 is worth −2.1." A player who was handed only these could play a perfect game without knowing how the pieces move.

    Check yourself

    A robot is dropped into the room with the full V table memorized, but no map: it does not know which square any move leads to. Can it act well?

    • Yes — it just moves toward whichever neighboring square has the highest V.
    • No — knowing which neighbor is best is useless without knowing which action gets you there.
    • Yes, because V already accounts for the discount.
    • No, because V values are only valid at the start of an episode.
    Answer: B. Option A is the tempting one, and it is exactly the trap. "Move toward the best neighbor" quietly assumes you know which square each action leads to — that is the transition function P, a model of the room. The robot was not given one. Q is precisely V with that lookahead already performed, which is why the value-based control algorithms in Lessons 10 and 11 learn Q rather than V.

    How V and Q fit together

    The two are not rivals. V is what you get when you average the Q values of a square, weighting each action by how often the policy actually picks it.

    V is the policy-weighted average of Q
    Vπ(s) = Σa π(a | s) Qπ(s, a)
    Σa "Add up the following, once for every action a available in s." In the room that means four terms.
    π(a | s) From Lesson 5: the probability the policy picks a in state s. The four numbers add to 1, which is what makes this a weighted average rather than just a sum.
    Qπ(s, a) The action-value being weighted — what that action is worth.
    Vπ(s) The result: the value of the square. Because it is an average, it can never be larger than the best Q in the square, nor smaller than the worst.

    When the policy is deterministic the sum collapses: one action has probability 1 and the rest have 0, so Vπ(s) = Qπ(s, π(s)). That is why (4,1) came out at 0.73 in Figure 6.1 — the policy goes left there, and left is worth 0.73.

    Now make the robot sloppy in that one corner: in (4,1) it goes left 70% of the time and each of the other three directions 10% of the time. Everywhere else it plays as before. Note that the wobble is in the robot's choices, not in the floor — the room is still deterministic. Under this new policy the four action-values shift a little, and the average tells a different story.

    Figure 6.3 — Reading the tree downward gives you Q; reading it upward gives you V. Each branch is weighted by how likely the policy is to take it, and the four products are added. The one-in-ten chance of stepping onto the stairs is small, but it is expensive enough to drag the whole square from 0.73 down to 0.50 — which is what "value is an average over what might happen" means in practice.

    That contrast is the first rider from the top of the lesson, made numeric. The square (4,1) is worth 0.73 under the careful policy and 0.50 under the sloppy one. The room did not change. The robot did.

    Vocabulary

    state-value function
    The formal name for Vπ. One number per state.
    action-value function
    The formal name for Qπ. One number per state-action pair. Also called the Q-function, and when it is stored as a grid, the Q-table.
    greedy
    Acting by always taking the largest Q in the current state, with no exploration. It comes back with a vengeance in Lesson 10.

    Check yourself

    In some state, Vπ is 0.50 while the best Qπ in that state is 0.73. What does the gap tell you?

    • One of the two numbers must have been computed incorrectly.
    • The policy sometimes takes actions worth less than the best one, which pulls the average down — so there is room to improve.
    • The discount factor is too small.
    • The state is unreachable under this policy.
    Answer: B. V is the weighted average of the Q values, so it lands strictly below the best one whenever the policy puts any weight on a weaker action. A gap between "what this state is worth" and "what its best move is worth" is exactly a signal that the policy can be improved — and closing that gap by acting greedily is the engine of Lesson 8. Option A is worth ruling out explicitly: both numbers are correct, and the gap is the whole point.

    The one thing to keep

    V scores a place. Q scores a move. You can only act on the second one, because acting means choosing between moves.

    Think it through

    In your own words: why does a robot holding the complete V table still need a map of the room, while a robot holding the complete Q table does not?

    Because choosing means comparing actions, and V is not indexed by actions. To turn V into a decision you have to ask "where would each action put me, and what would it pay on the way?" — and the answer to that question is a model of the environment: the transition function P and the rewards. With a map you can do that lookahead yourself; without one, the value numbers are true but unusable.

    Q is that lookahead already carried out and stored. Someone has done the "if I go up, then what" reasoning for every action in every state and written the answer on the arrow. All that is left is to read four numbers and take the biggest, which needs no knowledge of the room whatsoever.

    That is why the value-based, model-free control methods in Part III learn Q. Part IV takes a different route: it learns the policy directly and uses V only as a baseline.

    What to remember

    1. Value is expected return from here on. Not what a state pays, but what it leads to.
    2. Value is always relative to a policy. The same square was worth 0.73 under a careful policy and 0.50 under a sloppy one.
    3. V scores states, Q scores state-action pairs. Q pins the first action, then hands control back to the policy.
    4. Only Q lets you choose without a model. Acting greedily on V requires knowing where each action leads; acting greedily on Q requires nothing but the table.
    5. V is the policy-weighted average of Q. So V sits between the worst and best action in a state, and a gap below the best one means the policy can be improved.

    Lesson 7

    The Bellman Equations

    There is one idea underneath every algorithm in this class, and it fits in a single sentence of ordinary English. Once you have that sentence, the equations named after Richard Bellman are just careful bookkeeping over the things you are not sure about.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say in plain words what the Bellman equation claims
    • Follow the derivation from the return, one step at a time
    • Read the equation symbol by symbol, and read a backup diagram
    • Say what changes when "average" becomes "max"
    • Compute one backup by hand on a three-state example

    One sentence, before any symbols

    Here is the whole lesson:

    The value of where you are equals the reward you are about to get, plus the discounted value of where you land.

    Read it again slowly. It says that you do not need to think about the whole future to know what a place is worth. You need two things: what happens on the very next step, and what the place you arrive at is worth. That second part already summarizes everything after it.

    Try it on the room. In Lesson 6 you saw that square (3,1) is worth 0.81 and square (3,2) is worth 0.90, with γ = 0.9. The policy in (3,1) moves up. That step pays nothing, and it lands you in a square worth 0.90. So the sentence says (3,1) should be worth 0 + 0.9 × 0.90 = 0.81. Which is exactly the number that was already there.

    Figure 7.1 — The sentence, drawn. The value of the square you are standing on is rebuilt out of two things only: the reward on the way out, and the value of the square you arrive in, shrunk by γ. Everything beyond the second square is already folded into that 0.90 — which is why you never have to look further than one step ahead.

    Think of it like

    The road signs that give you a driving time. The sign at your exit says "City: 47 minutes". You do not recompute that by simulating every mile. You know it should equal the time to the next junction, plus the time on the sign at that junction. If those two signs disagree, one of them is wrong.

    The Bellman equation is that consistency check, written for value instead of minutes.

    Where the sentence comes from

    Nothing new is being invented here. The sentence follows from the definition of the return in Lesson 3 and the definition of value in Lesson 6. Take it one line at a time.

    1. Lesson 3 ended with a small identity: Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1. In words: everything you collect from now on is the next reward, plus everything you collect from the following step on, discounted once.
    2. Lesson 6 defined value as the expected return from a state: Vπ(s) is the average of Gt over all the ways things could go, given you start in s.
    3. Put the first inside the second. Instead of averaging Gt, average Rt+1 + γGt+1. Those are the same quantity, so their averages are the same too.
    4. An average of a sum is the sum of the averages. So the value of s is (the average next reward) plus γ times (the average return from the following step onward).
    5. Now the trick. That second piece — the average return from the next step onward — is not a new mystery. If you land in some square s′, the average return from there is by definition Vπ(s′). The value function eats its own tail.
    6. So: value of here = average of (reward on the way out + γ × value of where you land). Which is the sentence.

    Step 5 is the one that carries the weight. Everything else is arithmetic.

    The Bellman expectation equation for V
    Vπ(s) = 𝔼π[ Rt+1 + γ Vπ(St+1) | St = s ]
    Vπ(s) The value of the state you are in now, under policy π. "The value of where you are."
    𝔼π[ … | St = s ] Average the bracket over everything uncertain — which action π picks, and where the world puts you — given that you start in s.
    Rt+1 The reward for the step you are about to take. The +1 is the timing convention from Lesson 3: the reward for acting at time t arrives at t+1.
    γ The discount factor. Here it appears exactly once, because this equation only ever looks one step ahead.
    St+1 The state you land in — a capital letter because it is random until the step actually happens.
    Vπ(St+1) The value of that landing state. The same function appears on both sides of the equals sign, which is what makes this an equation rather than a definition.

    Check yourself

    A deterministic policy takes one action from a square. The step pays 0 and lands the agent in a square worth 0.60. With γ = 0.9, what is the starting square worth?

    • 0.60 — the discount only applies after two steps.
    • 0.54
    • 0.66 — you divide by γ rather than multiply.
    • 1.50
    Answer: B. 0 + 0.9 × 0.60 = 0.54. Note the direction: the earlier square is worth less than the one you are heading toward, because the good stuff is one step further away. If you got 0.66 you divided instead of multiplying — a common slip, and one that would make value grow as you moved away from the reward.

    Writing out the averages

    That word "average" is hiding two separate uncertainties, and the expanded form of the equation simply names them both.

    First, which action gets taken. That is up to the policy, so you average over actions with weights π(a | s). Second, where that action puts you. That is up to the environment, so you average over landing states with weights P(s′ | s, a) from Lesson 4. Two averages, one inside the other.

    The same equation, with both averages written out
    Vπ(s) = Σa π(a | s) Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) [ R(s, a, s′) + γ Vπ(s′) ]
    Σa π(a | s) "Take a weighted average over the actions, weighting each one by how often the policy picks it." In the room, four terms.
    Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) "Then, for that action, take a weighted average over the places you might land, weighting each by its probability." One term per possible landing state.
    s′ The state you land in; the prime notation is from Lesson 4.
    R(s, a, s′) The reward paid for that particular move — leaving s by doing a and arriving at s′.
    γ Vπ(s′) The value of the landing state, discounted once. Everything past the landing is already inside this number.
    [ … ] The bracket is what gets averaged. It is exactly the plain-English sentence: reward now, plus discounted value of where you land.

    There is a standard picture for this, and it is worth learning because it appears in every RL paper and textbook you will ever open. It is called a backup diagram, because it shows value flowing backward from the future into the state at the top.

    Figure 7.2 — How to read a backup diagram. Open circles are states, solid dots are actions, and you read downward from the state you are in. The first fork is the agent's choice, averaged with the policy's probabilities; the second fork is the environment's choice, averaged with P. Then the numbers travel back up to the root. Every algorithm in Parts II and III is some variation on this picture.

    The same construction works for Q. Start from a state and an action, take the step, and then — because you are back to following the policy — the thing waiting for you on the other side is the action-value of whatever the policy does next.

    The Bellman expectation equation for Q
    Qπ(s, a) = 𝔼π[ Rt+1 + γ Qπ(St+1, At+1) | St = s, At = a ]
    Qπ(s, a) The value of doing a in s, then following π — the action-value from Lesson 6.
    St = s, At = a Both are pinned: this state, this first action. Only what happens afterward gets averaged.
    Rt+1 The reward that action pays.
    At+1 The next action — chosen by the policy, not by you. This is the piece that makes the equation say "and then behave normally".
    γ Qπ(St+1, At+1) The discounted value of that next state-action pair. Same tail-eating move as before, one level down.

    Where people get lost

    Vπ appears on both sides of the equals sign, and that looks like cheating. It is not, because the two sides are talking about different states: the left is the state you are in, the right is the states you might land in.

    The equation is not a recipe that computes V out of nothing. It is a consistency condition — a rule that the true values must all satisfy at once, like the road signs that have to agree with each other. Lesson 8 shows the surprising part: if you just keep enforcing that condition over and over on made-up starting numbers, they converge to the true values.

    From "what π does" to "the best you can do"

    Everything so far describes a specific policy, warts and all. If the policy wastes 10% of its choices walking into stairs, the Bellman expectation equation faithfully averages that in.

    But we usually want something else: not "what is this policy worth" but "what is the best anyone could do here". So change one thing. Where the expectation equation averages over actions, the optimality equation takes the maximum.

    The Bellman optimality equation for V
    V*(s) = maxa Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) [ R(s, a, s′) + γ V*(s′) ]
    V*(s) The optimal value of s: what the state is worth under the best policy there is. The star in the superscript replaces π — the slot still says "measured under which policy", and the answer is now "the best one".
    maxa "Take the largest of the following, over the actions available in s." This is the only change from the expectation equation, and it is the whole difference between describing behavior and optimizing it.
    Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Still an average, and still over landing states. The environment does not get maximized — you choose your action, you do not choose your luck.
    R(s, a, s′) + γ V*(s′) The same bracket as before: reward now, plus the discounted value of landing — except the landing value is also the optimal one.
    π Notice it is gone. No policy is mentioned anywhere. The equation defines the best achievable values without anyone having to write down the policy that achieves them.

    Why does swapping average for max produce the best policy? Because the maximum is what an agent that never makes a mistake would get. Averaging says "sometimes I take the good action and sometimes I do not"; maximizing says "I take the good one, every time". And once you know V*, the best policy falls out for free — in each state, pick whichever action achieves the max.

    Figure 7.3 — The same state and the same two actions, backed up two different ways. On the left the policy still takes the weaker action 40% of the time, and the average pays for it. On the right the weaker action is simply discarded. That single swap — average to max — is the entire difference between "how good is this behavior" and "how good could behavior be".

    The same swap gives the Q version, and its shape matters more than any other formula in this class.

    The Bellman optimality equation for Q
    Q*(s, a) = Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) [ R(s, a, s′) + γ maxa′ Q*(s′, a′) ]
    Q*(s, a) The best achievable value of doing a in s: this action now, then perfect play forever after.
    Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Average over where the action puts you. There is no max out here — the first action was already chosen, and luck is not yours to choose.
    a′ Read "a prime": a candidate action in the next state.
    maxa′ Q*(s′, a′) The best action-value available after you land. Note where the max sits: inside the bracket, one step in the future.
    R(s, a, s′) + γ … The familiar bracket: reward now, plus the discounted value of the best thing you can do next.

    Read that last formula once more, because you will meet it again wearing a different hat. "Reward, plus γ times the best Q in the state you land in" is the exact target that Q-learning nudges its estimates toward in Lesson 10. That algorithm is this equation with the true probabilities replaced by whatever actually happened.

    Aside

    Richard Bellman worked this out in the 1950s and called the method dynamic programming. By his own account the name was chosen partly because it sounded impressive enough to survive a defense-department budget review. The underlying idea he called the principle of optimality: whatever the first decision is, the remaining decisions must themselves be optimal for the situation that first decision creates.

    Check yourself

    In the Bellman optimality equation, what exactly is the max taken over?

    • The states you might land in — you take the best outcome.
    • The actions available in the state — you take the best choice.
    • All possible policies, one at a time.
    • All future time steps.
    Answer: B. Option A is the misreading that causes the most damage. You choose your action; you do not choose where a slippery floor puts you. Landing states are always averaged with P, never maximized — otherwise the equation would describe an agent that gets lucky on purpose, and its values would be wildly optimistic. Option C describes what the equation achieves, but it does it without ever enumerating a policy: the max is over four actions, not over a universe of policies.

    One backup, by hand

    Here is a toy world small enough to compute completely — three states, not the room. Call them the start (S), the middle (M), and the goal (T), which pays +1 and ends the episode. Two actions in S: dash, which reaches the goal half the time and overshoots into the middle the other half; and creep, which usually gets nowhere. γ = 0.9.

    Current estimates, which are guesses and not yet correct: V(S) = 0.50, V(M) = 0.80, V(T) = 0 because the episode ends there and nothing follows.

    Check yourself

    Why did the optimality backup give 0.86 while the expectation backup gave 0.72?

    • The optimality equation uses a larger discount factor.
    • The expectation backup made an arithmetic error; both should agree.
    • The policy spends 40% of its time creeping, which is worth only 0.504, and averaging that in drags the number down.
    • The optimality backup counts the +1 reward twice.
    Answer: C. Both used the same γ, the same rewards and the same neighbor values — the only difference was what happened at the action fork. 0.72 is an honest description of a policy that creeps 40% of the time; 0.86 is what is available to a policy that never does. The gap between them is exactly the room for improvement, which is what Lesson 8 turns into an algorithm.

    The one thing to keep

    The value of where you are equals the reward you are about to get plus the discounted value of where you land. Averaging over the landings gives the Bellman expectation equation; maximizing over your own choices as well gives the Bellman optimality equation.

    Think it through

    Suppose you knew nothing about the room except that the charger pays +1, and you started every square's value at 0. If you applied the Bellman equation once to every square, then again, then again, how would numbers spread across the floor?

    Outward from the charger, one ring per pass. On the first pass almost every square backs up to 0, because all of its neighbors are still 0 — the only exception is a square adjacent to the charger, which now sees a real +1 on one of its steps and becomes 1.00. On the second pass the squares next to those pick up 0.9 × 1.00 = 0.90, and so on.

    The reason is built into the equation: it only ever looks one step ahead, so information can only travel one step per sweep. After enough sweeps every square has heard about the charger, discounted once per step of the journey — which is precisely the pattern of powers of 0.9 you saw in Figure 6.1.

    That process has a name, policy evaluation, and it is the first algorithm in Lesson 8.

    What to remember

    1. The sentence is the lesson. Value here equals reward now plus the discounted value of where you land.
    2. It follows from the return. Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1, averaged, with the tail recognized as the value of the next state.
    3. It is a consistency condition, not a definition. The same function appears on both sides, describing different states, and the true values are the ones that make every instance agree.
    4. Expectation averages, optimality maximizes. Average over actions to describe a policy; take the max over actions to describe the best one. Landing states are always averaged, never maximized.
    5. Backup diagrams: open circle is a state, solid dot is an action. Read downward, compute upward.
    6. The Q-optimality equation is Q-learning's ancestor. Reward plus γ times the best Q next door — remember the shape for Lesson 10.

    Lesson 8

    Planning with Dynamic Programming

    Suppose someone handed you a complete map of the room — every wall, and the exact consequence of every move. You would not need to explore at all. You could sit perfectly still and work out the best route in your head. This lesson is what "sit still and work it out" looks like when you write it down.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say what makes a problem planning rather than learning
    • Turn the Bellman expectation equation into a repeatable update
    • Run policy evaluation and policy improvement by hand on the room
    • Explain why value iteration reaches the same answer with less work
    • Name the two reasons this cannot be the whole story

    When you already have the map

    Lesson 7 gave you the Bellman equations. They are true statements about values, but a true statement is not yet a procedure. This lesson turns them into one.

    To do that we grant ourselves something generous: we assume the agent knows the model. It knows P(s′|s,a), the chance of landing in each next state, and it knows the reward for every move. When you have the model and are just computing, that is called planning. When you do not and have to find out by acting, that is learning — which is Part III, starting next lesson.

    Assuming the model is a big assumption and mostly an unrealistic one. It is worth it anyway, for two reasons. Some real problems genuinely do come with a model: board games, inventory systems, elevator scheduling. And more importantly, every algorithm in the rest of this class is one of the methods below with the known model swapped out for samples of actual experience. If you understand the planning version, the learning version is a small step.

    Figure 8.1 — The room, with the nine walkable cells named A through I so we can talk about them. Everything on the right is what "knowing the model" means: you can answer "if I do this, where do I end up and what do I score" without ever moving. Notice that C sits directly left of the charger and E sits directly below C — those two cells do most of the work in this lesson.

    One simplification

    Lesson 4 gave the room slippery wheels: commanded "up", the robot went up with probability 0.8 and sideways with 0.1 each. We switch the slip off for this lesson, and we set Lesson 3's −0.04 per-step cost to 0, purely so you can check every number on paper. Nothing about the method changes. The sum over next states below simply has one term in it instead of three.

    Policy evaluation: filling in the numbers

    Start with a fixed policy π and ask the narrow question: what is this policy worth from every cell? Not "what is the best I could do" — what is this behavior worth. That number is Vπ(s) from Lesson 6.

    Lesson 7 told you that Vπ satisfies the Bellman expectation equation. The trick of dynamic programming is to stop reading that equation as a description and start reading it as an instruction: take whatever numbers you currently have, plug them into the right-hand side, and let the result become your new numbers.

    Iterative policy evaluation — one update
    Vk+1(s) ← Σa π(a|s) Σs′ P(s′|s,a) [ R(s, a, s′) + γ Vk(s′) ]
    Vk+1(s) Your new estimate of the value of state s. The subscript k counts how many times you have done this, not time inside an episode.
    ← "Becomes". This is an assignment, not a claim that the two sides are already equal. That is the whole shift in the lesson.
    Σa Add up one term for each action available in s. In the room there are four: up, down, left, right.
    π(a|s) How often the policy picks action a in state s. For a deterministic policy this is 1 for the chosen action and 0 for the rest, so the sum collapses to a single term.
    Σs′ Add up one term for each state you could land in. In our slip-free room, exactly one landing spot has any weight.
    P(s′|s,a) The model: the probability of landing in s′ after doing a in s. This is the term you only have because we are planning.
    R(s, a, s′) The reward for that transition, exactly as in Lesson 7: 0 for an ordinary step, +1 for entering the charger, −1 for the stairs.
    γ The discount, 0.9 here. It is what makes a charger four steps away worth less than one two steps away.
    Vk(s′) Your old estimate for the state you land in. Old numbers go in on the right; new numbers come out on the left.

    Applying that update once to every state is called a sweep. You start from any guess at all — all zeros is the usual choice — and sweep until the numbers stop moving.

    Worked: evaluating a bad policy

    Let π0 be the dumbest policy that is still a policy: go right, everywhere. From C that walks into the charger. From E it walks into the stairs. From D it walks into the wall and stays put forever. Start every cell at 0.00 and sweep.

This is a truthful audit of a terrible policy, and every one of those numbers is about to earn its keep.

    Where people get lost

    Policy evaluation never improves anything. It is a measuring instrument, not an optimizer. If you catch yourself asking "why doesn't cell D go up, the charger is right there" — that is the next section's question. Evaluation answers only "what is π worth", and for π0 the honest answer at D is nothing at all.

    Check yourself

    After sweep 3, cell D still shows 0.00 even though the charger is only four steps away. What is that telling you?

    • There is a bug — every cell should have moved by now.
    • Under π₀ the robot in D walks into the wall forever, so following that policy from D really is worth nothing.
    • D cannot be reached from the start cell.
    • Three sweeps is not enough; D will fill in on the next sweep.
    Answer: B. Evaluation reports what the given policy earns, not what is achievable. π₀ sends D right, into the wall, forever, for a reward of 0 every step. Option D is the tempting one: it is true that information spreads one step per sweep, but D's chain of values leads nowhere, so no number of extra sweeps will change it. Option C is simply false — F is directly below D.

    Policy improvement: look one step further

    Now you have honest numbers for π0. Use them. In each cell, ask a purely local question: is there an action whose one-step lookahead beats what the policy currently does? If there is, switch to it.

    The greedy improvement step
    π′(s) = argmaxa Σs′ P(s′|s,a) [ R(s, a, s′) + γ Vπ(s′) ]
    π′(s) The new policy's action in state s. The prime just means "the improved one".
    argmaxa As in Lessons 5 and 6: the action that produces the largest value, not the value itself.
    Σs′ Average over where that action might land you. One term each in our slip-free room.
    P(s′|s,a) The model again. This is why you cannot do this step without one — you are asking "what if" about an action you did not take.
    R(s, a, s′) The reward on that transition.
    γ The discount, 0.9.
    Vπ(s′) The values you just measured for the old policy. You look one action ahead, then fall back on old numbers for everything after that.

    Worked: the cell that was walking down the stairs

    Take cell E, using Vπ0 from the table above. Four options, each worth reward plus 0.9 times the old value of where you land:

    Right, into the stairs: −1 + 0.9 × 0 = −1.00. Up, into C: 0 + 0.9 × 1.00 = 0.90. Down, into H: 0 + 0.9 × 0.00 = 0.00. Left, into the wall, so you stay in E: 0 + 0.9 × (−1.00) = −0.90.

    Up wins at 0.90, so π′ sends E upward. The same arithmetic at D makes it go up too. Every other cell keeps its old action.

    Figure 8.2 — One improvement step, driven entirely by the numbers printed in the left grid. Only two cells move, and both of them were walking into something: D into the wall, E down the stairs. Look at H, bottom row third from the left. It still points right, even though going up to E is on the fast route — because as far as H can see, E is still worth −1.00. H will not learn better until E has been re-measured. That is exactly why one pass is not enough.

    Why is switching safe? The policy improvement theorem says: if you take the greedy action in some state and then behave exactly as before, you do at least as well from every state, and strictly better from at least one unless you were already optimal. In words — you took the best available first step and then kept your old habits, so you cannot have lost ground. We will take that on trust; the proof is short but not illuminating.

    Check yourself

    Improvement moved D and E but left H pointing right, even though H is directly below E. Why?

    • H is already optimal, so it never needs to change.
    • The wall blocks H from moving up.
    • The greedy step uses V for π₀, in which E is still worth −1.00, so moving up scores 0.9 × (−1.00) = −0.90.
    • Improvement is only allowed to change one cell per pass.
    Answer: C. Greedy improvement looks exactly one action ahead and then trusts the old values for everything beyond. From H, "up" leads to a cell currently labeled −1.00, which looks like suicide. Once π1 is evaluated, E becomes 0.90 and H flips upward on the next pass. Option B is a real trap — the wall sits at the cell between B and G, not above H.

    Policy iteration: alternate until nothing moves

    You now have two operations that each do half a job. Evaluation produces honest numbers for a policy; improvement produces a better policy from honest numbers. Neither is enough alone. Chain them.

    Figure 8.3 — Policy iteration. Each trip round the loop produces a policy that is never worse than the one before, and there are only finitely many policies to go through, so the loop must stop. The exit on the right is the whole termination test: when improvement proposes nothing new, the policy is greedy with respect to its own values, which is precisely the Bellman optimality condition from Lesson 7.

    Written out as steps:

    1. Pick any starting policy. "Go right everywhere" is fine.
    2. Set every state's value to 0.
    3. Sweep the evaluation update over all states, repeatedly, until no value changes by more than a hair. You now have Vπ.
    4. Sweep the greedy improvement over all states, producing a new policy.
    5. If the new policy is identical to the old one, stop — it is optimal. Otherwise go back to step 3 with the new policy.

    On the room this takes four rounds. The first fixes D and E, as you just saw. The second fixes H and F, once E has been re-measured at 0.90. The third flips I from shoving against the east wall to heading left toward H. The fourth proposes nothing at all, so we stop. The final values run from 0.66 at the start cell F up to 1.00 at C.

    Value iteration: stop being so thorough

    Step 3 above is wasteful. You grind the evaluation all the way to convergence just so you can throw the policy away a moment later. Most of that precision is never used.

    Value iteration is the impatient version: do a single sweep, and fold the improvement into it by replacing "average over what the policy does" with "take the best action". No policy is stored at all while it runs.

    The value-iteration update
    Vk+1(s) ← maxa Σs′ P(s′|s,a) [ R(s, a, s′) + γ Vk(s′) ]
    Vk+1(s) The new number for state s after sweep k+1.
    ← "Becomes". Same assignment as before.
    maxa The one change from evaluation. Instead of averaging over what π would do, keep only the best action's score. This is the Bellman optimality equation used as an instruction.
    Σs′ Average over the possible landing spots for that action.
    P(s′|s,a) The model's probability of landing in s′.
    R(s, a, s′) The reward on that transition: 0, +1, or −1 in the room.
    γ The discount, 0.9.
    Vk(s′) Last sweep's number for the state you land in.

    Run it on the room from all zeros and watch what happens.

    Figure 8.4 — The wavefront. One cell knows something after sweep 1, three after sweep 2, five after sweep 3. Each new ring is exactly 0.9 times the ring before it: 1.00, then 0.90, then 0.81. A sweep carries information exactly one step, so after k sweeps every cell within k steps of the charger already holds its final number, and every cell further away has not moved at all. The shaded corner cells are terminal — the +1 and −1 there are what you collect on arriving, not values.

    Two more sweeps finish the job. Sweep 4 fills the three cells four steps out (D, G and I, all at 0.93 = 0.73), and sweep 5 fills the start cell F at 0.94 = 0.66. Five sweeps, against roughly twenty for policy iteration on the same room. The values are identical either way, and you read the policy off at the very end by taking the greedy action once in each cell.

    The pattern 0.9d−1, where d is the number of steps to the charger, is worth staring at. The value function is a distance measurement in disguise. Discounting is what converts "how far" into "how much".

    1. Set every state's value to 0.
    2. For each state, compute reward plus γ times the current value of the landing state, once per available action.
    3. Keep the largest of those numbers as that state's new value.
    4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until no value changes by more than a hair.
    5. Once, at the end, read out the policy: in each state pick the action whose number was largest.

    Check yourself

    Value iteration fills 1 cell after sweep 1, 3 after sweep 2, and 5 after sweep 3. Roughly how many sweeps before the start cell F — five steps from the charger — stops showing 0.00?

    • Four, since only four cells were still empty.
    • Five, because a sweep carries information exactly one step and F is five steps away.
    • Nine, one per walkable cell.
    • Never — F is too far for the discount to reach.
    Answer: B. The rule is "one sweep, one step". Option C confuses the number of cells with the number of rounds; sweeps update every cell at once, so the size of the grid does not set the count — the distance does. Option D would be true only if γ were 0, which kills the future entirely.

    Two reasons this cannot be the whole story

    Everything above works, converges, and is provably correct. It is also unusable on most real problems, for two separate reasons.

    You rarely know P. The greedy step asks "what would happen if I did the action I did not do?" — a question only a model can answer. A chess engine can answer it. A robot in somebody's actual kitchen cannot, and neither can a language model being asked whether a different sentence would have pleased the reader more.

    You cannot sweep every state. A sweep touches all of them. The room has nine. Backgammon has about 1020 positions; at a billion backups a second, a single sweep would take over three thousand years. Go and Atari screens are far worse. Even if the model were handed to you, you could not use it this way.

    The one thing to keep

    Dynamic programming is Bellman's equation used as an assignment instead of a description, applied over and over until the numbers stop moving. Everything that follows keeps the update and gives up the sweep.

    The fix for the first problem is to stop asking "what would happen" and start asking "what did happen" — replace the average over the model with an average over experience. That is Lesson 9, and it is where reinforcement learning proper begins. The fix for the second is to stop visiting states one at a time and let a function generalize across them, which is Lesson 11.

    Think it through

    Someone sets γ = 1 in the room instead of 0.9. What happens to the converged values, and what happens to the policy you read off them?

    Every cell from which the charger is reachable converges to exactly 1.00. With no discount, a reward you collect eventually is worth precisely as much as one you collect now, so distance stops appearing in the numbers. The whole grid goes flat.

    That wrecks the greedy step, because greedy needs differences to choose between. Nearly every cell ends up with a tie, and the tie-break — an implementation detail — decides the behavior. Worse: at cell A, the action "walk into the north wall and stay in A" also scores 1 × 1.00 = 1.00, tying with the action that actually makes progress. A greedy policy is allowed to pick it, and the robot stands there forever, holding a value estimate of 1.00 while earning nothing.

    So γ < 1 is not only about keeping infinite sums finite, which is how Lesson 3 introduced it. It is also what makes the value function encode distance, and distance is what makes a greedy agent hurry.

    What to remember

    1. Planning means you have the model. Dynamic programming computes the answer without ever taking a step, which is powerful and also why it does not apply to most real problems.
    2. Policy evaluation measures; it never improves. Sweep the Bellman expectation equation as an assignment until the numbers settle, and you have what that policy is worth.
    3. Policy improvement looks one action ahead. Go greedy in the values you just computed. It can never make things worse, and if it changes nothing you are optimal.
    4. Value iteration folds the two together. One sweep with a max in it, repeated. Same answer, far less work.
    5. Information travels one step per sweep. That single fact explains the whole 1.00, 0.90, 0.81 wavefront, and it comes back next lesson as the reason TD learning is slow to reach distant states.

    Lesson 9

    Learning from Experience: Monte Carlo and TD

    Take the map away. The robot no longer knows what is behind any door, what a move costs, or where a move leads. All it has is a memory of what actually happened. Everything from here to the end of this class is about squeezing value estimates out of that memory.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say exactly what changes when the model disappears
    • Run a Monte Carlo update on a finished episode
    • Run a TD(0) update after a single step
    • Compute a TD error and read what its sign means
    • Show that Monte Carlo and TD are one update with two different targets

    Throw away the map

    Lesson 8 leaned on P(s′|s,a) in every single line. Both the evaluation sweep and the greedy step ask "what would happen if", and only a model can answer that. Delete the model and both methods stop working mid-sentence.

    What survives is experience. The agent acts, and the world hands back a reward and a next state. String those together and you get exactly what Lesson 2 called a trajectory: s0, a0, R1, s1, a1, R2, s2 and so on.

    That is the entire raw material. No probabilities, no lookahead, no "what if". Just a list of things that happened. The question of this lesson is how you turn such a list into the numbers V(s).

    We stay in the room from Lesson 8 — nine walkable cells labeled A through I, the charger at the top right worth +1, the stairs just below it worth −1, and γ = 0.9. F is the bottom-left start cell and C sits directly left of the charger. The difference is that the agent is no longer told any of that. It has to find out by walking.

    Monte Carlo: wait for the ending

    Here is the most obvious idea anyone could have. You want to know the expected return from a state. So visit it, play the episode out to the end, and write down the return you actually got. Do that many times and average.

    That is Monte Carlo value estimation, and it is called that because it substitutes sampling for calculation, the way a casino substitutes outcomes for odds. It needs no model whatsoever. It only needs episodes that end.

    Rather than storing every return and averaging, we nudge the current estimate a fraction of the way toward each new one:

    The Monte Carlo update
    V(st) ← V(st) + α [ Gt − V(st) ]
    V(st) Your current estimate of the value of the state visited at time t. It appears on both sides because it is being revised.
    ← "Becomes". Same assignment arrow as Lesson 8.
    α The learning rate, a small positive number like 0.1. How far you move toward the new evidence rather than staying put.
    Gt The actual return observed from time t onward in this episode — the real discounted sum of everything that followed. Not an estimate. It is a measurement.
    [ … ] The error: how far the reality you just observed sits from what you believed. Positive means you were pessimistic.

    Set α to 1/N on the N-th visit and this is exactly a running average of the returns. Keeping α fixed instead makes it a weighted average that gently forgets old episodes — useful when the world is changing.

    Monte Carlo has two flaws, and they are both consequences of one design choice. It uses Gt, which means it cannot do anything until the episode ends. If the episode is long, you wait a long time. If the task never ends, you wait forever. And because Gt is the sum of everything that happened, one unlucky detour late in an episode changes your opinion of a state you visited at the very start.

    Check yourself

    You want the value of a state in a task with no ending — a server that runs indefinitely. Can Monte Carlo do it?

    • Yes, Monte Carlo works on any task.
    • No. Its target is the actual return, which does not exist until the episode finishes.
    • No, because Monte Carlo needs the transition probabilities.
    • Yes, provided you set γ = 1.
    Answer: B. No ending means no Gt, and with no target there is no update — ever. Option C names a requirement of dynamic programming, not of Monte Carlo, which is proudly model-free. Option D makes things worse rather than better: on a never-ending task, γ = 1 sends the return to infinity.

    Temporal difference: do not wait

    The fix is almost impudent. You wanted Gt, the true return from here. Lesson 3 told you that Gt = Rt+1 + γ Gt+1. You do not know Gt+1 either — but you have an estimate of it sitting right there in your table. It is called V(st+1).

    So take one step, collect the one real reward, and use your own current guess for everything after that. This is temporal-difference learning, and in its simplest one-step form it is called TD(0).

    The TD(0) update
    V(st) ← V(st) + α [ Rt+1 + γ V(st+1) − V(st) ]
    V(st) Your estimate for the state you are leaving. The thing being revised.
    ← "Becomes".
    α The learning rate. Same role as in Monte Carlo.
    Rt+1 The one reward you actually collected on this step. Remember the timing convention from Lesson 3: the reward for acting at time t arrives labeled t+1.
    γ The discount, 0.9 in the room.
    V(st+1) Your current estimate for the state you landed in. This is a guess, not a measurement — and it is the whole difference between TD and Monte Carlo.
    [ … ] The error again: one-step reality plus your onward guess, minus what you believed before you moved.

    Learning a guess from a guess has a name: bootstrapping. It sounds like it should not work, and the fact that it does is one of the genuinely surprising results in this subject.

    Think of it like

    A chess player who thinks she is winning, makes a move, and immediately sees her opponent's reply. She does not wait for checkmate to revise her opinion of the earlier position — she revises it the moment the new position looks worse than the old one did. She is comparing two evaluations, both of which could be wrong, and still getting steadily better at evaluating. That comparison is the temporal difference.

    Figure 9.1 — The same walk through the room, learned two ways. Monte Carlo draws one enormous arrow from the end of the episode back to the start; nothing at all happens until the charger is reached. Temporal difference draws five short ones, each reaching back exactly one step, and the first one fires before the robot has any idea the charger exists. Monte Carlo also updates every other state on the path — only the arrow for F is drawn, to keep the picture readable.

    The TD error

    The bracket in the TD update deserves its own name and its own symbol, because you will meet it again in almost every remaining lesson.

    The TD error, and the update written with it
    δt = Rt+1 + γ V(st+1) − V(st)
    V(st) ← V(st) + α δt
    δt The TD error at time t, spoken "delta". One number: how much better or worse this step turned out than you expected.
    Rt+1 The reward actually collected on this step. The only piece of hard evidence in the whole expression.
    γ The discount, 0.9 in the room.
    V(st+1) What you currently believe the state you landed in is worth.
    V(st) What you believed the state you left was worth — your prediction, being put to the test.
    α The learning rate: what fraction of the error you actually act on.
    ← "Becomes". The whole of TD learning is now one line: nudge your old belief by α times the surprise.

    Read the sign and you have read the message. δ > 0 means things went better than you predicted, so raise the prediction. δ < 0 means worse, so lower it. δ = 0 means the step held no news at all, and nothing changes. The TD error is surprise with a number attached.

    Aside: the same signal in a brain

    In the 1990s, recordings of dopamine neurons in the midbrain showed a strikingly familiar pattern: they fire above baseline for an unexpected reward, stay flat for one that was fully predicted, and dip below baseline at the moment a predicted reward fails to arrive. That is the sign pattern of δ, discovered independently in a completely different field. Reward-prediction-error accounts of dopamine and TD learning have been intertwined ever since.

    The one thing to keep

    If you remember one symbol from this class, make it δ. It becomes the update in Q-learning and SARSA (Lesson 10), the squared loss that trains a deep network (Lesson 11), and the one-step estimate of the advantage (Lesson 13) that PPO and GRPO are built on (Lessons 14 and 16). It is the same quantity every time.

    Figure 9.2 — One update, drawn to scale. The robot believed cell C was worth 0.80; stepping into the charger produced a target of 1.00, so the surprise was +0.20. With α = 0.1 the estimate moves one tenth of that distance, to 0.82 — not to the target. That restraint is the point: any single step could have been a fluke, and the small step lets many observations vote instead of the last one deciding.

    That figure also explains α. Set it to 1 and every estimate jumps straight to the latest target, discarding everything learned before — the values thrash and never settle. Set it to 0.001 and the estimates are beautifully stable and take a hundred thousand episodes to arrive. Values between 0.01 and 0.1 are the usual compromise.

    Check yourself

    You believe V(s) = 0.50. You take one step, collect R = 0, and land in a state you currently value at 0.90. With γ = 0.9, what is δ?

    • +0.40
    • +0.31
    • +0.81
    • −0.31
    Answer: B. 0 + 0.9 × 0.90 − 0.50 = 0.81 − 0.50 = +0.31. Option A comes from forgetting to discount the next state's value. Option C is the target, not the error — you still have to subtract what you already believed. The sign is positive because the step landed you somewhere better than your prediction had allowed for.

    One update, two targets

    Put the two rules next to each other and the family resemblance is total. Both have the shape new equals old plus a small step toward a target. They disagree about nothing except what goes in the target slot.

    The shape both methods share
    new ← old + α ( target − old )
    MC target = Gt
    TD target = Rt+1 + γ V(st+1)
    old Your current V(st) — the belief being tested.
    new The revised V(st) you store afterward.
    α The learning rate. Identical in both methods; it is not the difference.
    target What you are moving toward. The only slot the two methods fill differently.
    Gt Monte Carlo's choice: the full observed return, available only once the episode is finished. All measurement, no guess.
    Rt+1 TD's first ingredient: the single real reward from this step.
    γ V(st+1) TD's second ingredient: the discounted value you currently guess for the next state, standing in for the entire rest of the episode.

    Figure 9.3 — Both panels are computing an estimate of the same thing: the return from the start cell. Monte Carlo builds it out of five real rewards and reaches 0.66. TD builds it out of one real reward plus a stored guess and reaches 0.45. They then go into an identical machine. Whenever you meet a new algorithm in this class, the useful question is not "what is the update" — it is what did they put in the target.

    Why the caution pays off

    Suppose the robot had dithered — F, G, H, I, H, E, C, charger, seven steps instead of five. The return from F would then be 0.96 = 0.53, not 0.66, and Monte Carlo would revise F to 0.40 + 0.1(0.53 − 0.40) = 0.413 instead of 0.426.

    TD's update to F would be identical either way: 0.405. It only ever looked at the first step, which was F to G in both episodes. Everything that happened afterward was invisible to it. That is the variance difference in one line of arithmetic.

    Vocabulary

    bootstrapping
    Updating an estimate using another estimate rather than a completed measurement. TD bootstraps; Monte Carlo does not.
    unbiased
    Right on average, however wrong any single sample is. Monte Carlo's target is an unbiased sample of the return.
    variance
    How much the target jumps around from one sample to the next. Monte Carlo's is high, because a whole episode's worth of luck goes into it.
    bias
    Being systematically off, not merely noisy. TD's target inherits whatever is wrong with V(s′), and early on that is simply your initial guess.

    So the trade is: Monte Carlo is noisy but honest, and TD is steady but leans on its own possibly-wrong beliefs. In practice TD usually wins, and by a wide margin — it learns online, works on tasks that never end, and its bias shrinks as the estimates improve, while Monte Carlo's noise does not shrink at all. Most methods in the remaining lessons bootstrap — the exceptions are REINFORCE in Lesson 12, which uses the full return, and GRPO in Lesson 16, which scores whole responses.

    Check yourself

    Why is TD called biased while Monte Carlo is not?

    • TD uses a smaller learning rate.
    • TD's target contains V(s′), a current guess that may be systematically wrong; Monte Carlo's target is a return that actually happened.
    • TD throws away the reward and uses only the value estimate.
    • Monte Carlo averages over many episodes and TD does not.
    Answer: B. Bias here means "off in a consistent direction", which is what happens when your target is built from a table you initialized to zero. Option D is the tempting one: both methods average over experience — TD simply averages one-step targets instead of whole returns. Option A confuses a tuning choice with a structural property; both methods use the same α.

    Think it through

    At the last step of the episode, Monte Carlo and TD produced exactly the same new value for C: 0.820. Why? And what does that tell you about how the two methods are related?

    Because the charger is terminal, and a terminal state has value 0 — there is nothing after it. So TD's target at that step is 1 + 0.9 × 0 = 1.00, which is precisely the actual return from C. At the final transition there is no guessing left to do, so the bootstrap term vanishes and TD's target becomes a measurement.

    Generalize it: TD is Monte Carlo with the tail of the episode replaced by a stored guess. One step from the end there is no tail, so they agree exactly. Two steps out they disagree a little. At the start of a long episode almost the whole target is guesswork and they disagree a lot. There is a whole family of methods sitting between the two — replace three steps of guess with three steps of reality, or ten — and the knob that slides along that family is called λ. Lesson 13 uses it under the name GAE.

    What to remember

    1. No model means no lookahead. All you can do is act, watch, and average. That single constraint separates Part III from Part II.
    2. Monte Carlo waits for the ending. Its target is the return that really happened: unbiased, noisy, and unavailable until the episode is over.
    3. TD updates after one step by bootstrapping. Its target is one real reward plus its own stored guess about where it landed.
    4. δ = R + γV(s′) − V(s) is the quantity to memorize. Positive means better than expected, negative means worse, zero means no news. It reappears in every remaining lesson.
    5. Both are new ← old + α(target − old). When you meet a new algorithm, ask only what it puts in the target slot.

    Lesson 10

    Q-Learning, SARSA, and Exploration

    Two learners watch the same robot take the same clumsy steps through the same world. They see identical data. And they come away with different opinions about how close to the edge you should walk. This lesson is about why — and the answer is one symbol in one formula.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say why controlling behavior needs Q rather than V
    • Work out ε-greedy action probabilities for a given ε
    • Read the Q-learning and SARSA updates term by term
    • Explain, without being prompted, why SARSA takes the safer path

    From judging to choosing

    Lesson 9 taught you to estimate V(s) from experience. That tells you how good a square is. It does not tell you what to do. To turn V into a decision you would have to ask "where does each action land me?" — and that means knowing the environment's dynamics, which in Part III we have given up on.

    Q(s, a) dodges the problem entirely. Store a number for each action in each state, and choosing is just reading four numbers off a shelf and taking the biggest. No model needed.

    Acting greedily from Q
    π(s) = argmaxa Q(s, a)
    π(s) The action the policy picks in state s. Here it is a single action, not a spread of probabilities.
    argmaxa As in Lessons 5, 6 and 8: the action that produces the largest value, not the value itself. If the four numbers are 0.11, 0.05, 0.20 and 0.60, argmax is right.
    Q(s, a) Your current estimate of the return you get by doing a in s and behaving sensibly afterward. Just a number in a table.

    Estimating values is called prediction. Using them to improve behavior is called control. Everything from here on is control.

    The catch nobody mentions

    Reading the biggest number only works if the numbers are right. At the start they are all wrong — usually all zero. An agent that always takes the largest number will lock onto whichever action happened to pay off first and never discover the better one sitting next to it. Greedy is the goal, not the method.

    You cannot learn about a door you never open

    This is the exploration–exploitation dilemma, and it has no free solution. Every step you spend trying something new is a step you did not spend on the thing you already know works. Every step you spend on the known thing is a step that taught you nothing.

    Think of it like

    This is the restaurant from Lesson 1, with the numbers filled in. You have eaten at the same place on your street forty times. It is a solid 7 out of 10. The new place might be a 9, or it might be a 3. Trying it costs you one dinner; never trying it costs you every future dinner you could have upgraded. Notice that the right answer depends on how many dinners you have left.

    The standard fix is deliberately crude and works remarkably well. ε-greedy: flip a biased coin before every action. Most of the time, take the greedy action. Occasionally, ignore your estimates completely and pick uniformly at random.

    The exploration rule
    π(a|s) = 1 − ε + ε / |A|   for the greedy action
    π(a|s) = ε / |A|   for every other action
    π(a|s) The probability of picking action a in state s. This is the stochastic policy from Lesson 5, now built out of Q.
    ε Epsilon, the exploration rate — a number between 0 and 1. The fraction of steps on which you throw your estimates away and guess. Typical values are 0.1 down to 0.01.
    1 − ε The rest of the steps — the fraction on which you simply take the greedy action. With ε = 0.1 that is 0.9.
    |A| How many actions there are. The upright bars mean "size of the set". In the room it is 4: up, down, left, right.
    ε / |A| The share of the random draws that happen to land on any one particular action. Note that the greedy action gets this share too — the random draw does not exclude it.

    Run the numbers

    Take ε = 0.1 and the room's 4 actions. On 90% of steps you take the greedy action. On the other 10% you draw uniformly from four options, so each action picks up an extra 0.1 ÷ 4 = 0.025.

    The greedy action therefore has probability 0.9 + 0.025 = 0.925, and each of the other three has 0.025. Check: 0.925 + 3 × 0.025 = 1. Good.

    Read that second number again. Each individual wrong action gets taken about once every forty steps. Keep it in mind — the whole payoff of this lesson turns on it.

    Figure 10.1 — The same four Q values, two settings of ε. The green bar is the action the agent believes is best; the amber slivers are what exploration costs. Notice that ε does not change which action is favored, only how firmly. At ε = 0.5 the agent still prefers right, but it spends half its life wandering — fine on day one, ruinous on day ten hundred.

    That last point is why ε is usually decayed: start near 1.0, when your estimates are worthless and any information is welcome, and shrink toward 0.01 as the numbers settle. A common schedule multiplies ε by 0.999 after every episode, so it falls from 1.0 to about 0.05 over three thousand episodes.

    Check yourself

    An agent has 5 actions and uses ε-greedy with ε = 0.2. What is the probability it takes the greedy action?

    • 0.80 — one minus ε.
    • 0.84 — that is 0.8, plus a fifth of the remaining 0.2.
    • 0.20 — that is what ε means.
    • 0.96 — four fifths of the actions are non-greedy.
    Answer: B. Option A is the near-miss almost everyone picks. It forgets that the random draw is over all actions, including the greedy one, so the greedy action collects 0.2 ÷ 5 = 0.04 on top of its 0.8. Each of the other four gets exactly 0.04, and 0.84 + 4 × 0.04 = 1.

    Q-learning: learn about the policy you wish you had

    Take the TD update from Lesson 9, new ← old + α(target − old), and apply it to Q instead of V. The only real question is what to use as the target. Q-learning's answer: assume that from the next state onward you play perfectly.

    The Q-learning update
    target = R + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′)
    Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [ target − Q(s, a) ]
    target What we now believe this action is worth, worked out from what just happened. The first line builds it; the second line drags the old estimate part of the way toward it.
    Q(s, a) The one number in the whole table that this step changes: the value of the action you just took, in the state you just left.
    s′, a′ The square you landed in, and an action available there. Same prime notation as Lessons 4 and 7.
    ← "Becomes". Overwrite the old number with the new one.
    R The reward that actually arrived after this action — what Lesson 3 wrote as Rt+1. Real and observed, not estimated.
    γ The discount factor. How much the future is worth relative to now.
    maxa′ Q(s′, a′) The whole lesson lives here. Look at every action available in the square you landed in, and take the largest value — whether or not that is the action you are going to take next.
    α The learning rate from Lesson 9. What fraction of the gap you close in one step. With α = 0.5 you move halfway to the target; with α = 0.1, a tenth of the way.
    [ … ] The TD error from Lesson 9: target minus old estimate. Positive means "better than I thought", negative means "worse".

    Now walk one update through an actual table. The room's squares are written as (column, row), with (1,1) bottom-left and the charger at (4,3). Set α = 0.5 and γ = 0.9.

    Figure 10.2 — The robot was in (2,3), moved right, collected nothing, and landed in (3,3). Only the blue cell changes. The green cell is where the target comes from: the biggest number in the row for the state it landed in. The shaded cells are the ones Q-learning scans past on its way to that maximum — it looks at all four and keeps only the best.

    The arithmetic: the target is 0 + 0.9 × 0.60 = 0.54. The TD error is 0.54 − 0.20 = 0.34. Half of that is 0.17, so 0.20 becomes 0.37. Value has leaked one square backward from the charger. Run this a few thousand times and it reaches the whole room.

    Q-learning, as steps

    1. Set every entry of the table to 0.
    2. Put the robot at the start of an episode.
    3. Look at the current state's row. Pick an action with ε-greedy.
    4. Take it. Observe the reward and the square you land in.
    5. Find the largest value in the landing square's row.
    6. Update the cell you came from, using that largest value in the target.
    7. Move to the landing square and go back to step 3, until the episode ends.
    8. Shrink ε slightly and start a new episode.

    Vocabulary

    behavior policy
    The rule the agent actually uses to pick its actions while collecting experience. Here, ε-greedy.
    target policy
    The rule the agent is learning about — the one its numbers will end up describing.
    off-policy
    The two are different. Q-learning behaves with ε-greedy but its max describes a purely greedy agent, so it learns about a policy it never actually runs.
    on-policy
    The two are the same. The agent learns about the clumsy, exploring thing it really is.

    SARSA: learn about the agent you actually are

    SARSA changes exactly one thing. Instead of the best available next value, it uses the value of the action it is going to take next — the one ε-greedy has already drawn.

    The SARSA update
    target = R + γ Q(s′, a′)
    Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [ target − Q(s, a) ]
    a′ The one change. Now it means the specific action ε-greedy has actually chosen for the next step. Not the best one — the real one, exploratory blunders included.
    Q(s′, a′) The value of that real next action. Compare with Q-learning's max, which quietly assumed the next action would be the best one.
    SARSA The name is just the five things the update touches, in order: s, a, R, s′, a′. That is the entire etymology.
    α, γ, R Unchanged from Q-learning: learning rate, discount, and the reward that actually arrived.
    target Same job as before — the number we drag the old estimate toward. Only its recipe, on the first line, has changed.
    Q(s, a) ← … [ … ] The entire second line is identical to Q-learning's. Every difference between the two methods lives on the first line, in one symbol.

    The same transition, two answers

    Take the exact step from Figure 10.2 — into (3,3), where the four values are 0.11, 0.05, 0.20, 0.60. Q-learning uses 0.60 and turns 0.20 into 0.37.

    Now suppose the ε-greedy coin comes up "explore" and picks down, worth 0.05. SARSA's target is 0 + 0.9 × 0.05 = 0.045. The TD error is 0.045 − 0.20 = −0.155, so the new value is 0.20 + 0.5 × (−0.155) = 0.1225.

    Identical step, identical reward. One learner raised the number, the other lowered it. Over thousands of episodes those small differences add up to different behavior.

    When exploration is harmless, the two methods agree. When exploration is expensive, they diverge — and the next section is what that looks like.

    A robot moves right and lands in a square where the four values are 0.9, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1. Its ε-greedy draw for the next step comes up left, worth 0.1. With γ = 1 and reward 0, what target does each method use?

    The cliff, and why the cautious learner wins

    Here is the standard test, and it is worth memorizing. A corridor four rows tall and twelve columns wide. Start bottom-left, goal bottom-right, and the ten squares between them along the bottom are a cliff. Every step costs −1. Step into the cliff and you take −100 and are teleported back to the start.

    Figure 10.3 — Both agents explore with the same ε = 0.1. Q-learning's numbers say the blue route is optimal, and they are right — for an agent that never slips. SARSA's numbers price in the slipping, so its greedy path sits one row further from the drop and pays two extra steps for the privilege.

    Now the part worth going slowly over. Stand on the row directly above the cliff. Q-learning's target for that square asks a hypothetical question: if I acted perfectly from here, what would I collect? The answer never mentions falling, because a perfect agent does not step down. So the squares along the edge keep high values, and the greedy path they imply runs right along the drop.

    SARSA's target asks a different question: given what I am actually about to do, what will I collect? And what it is actually about to do is ε-greedy. One step in forty, ε-greedy picks down. When it does, SARSA's next action value is the value of walking off the cliff, and a −100 flows into the update for the square you were standing on.

    Pricing your own clumsiness

    On the edge row, ε = 0.1 over 4 actions makes down come up with probability 0.025 — one step in forty. Each of those costs an extra −100, so the expected surcharge for standing next to the cliff is 0.025 × 100 = 2.5 per step, on top of the −1 you pay anyway.

    The edge route spends about eleven steps in that danger zone: roughly 11 × 2.5 ≈ −27 of self-inflicted damage per episode. The detour costs two extra steps, so −2. Twenty-seven versus two. For an agent that really does explore, the safe route is not timid — it is correct.

    Where people get lost

    It is tempting to conclude that Q-learning is wrong here. It is not. Q-learning correctly finds the optimal policy, which really is the edge route — for an agent that has stopped exploring. What it does not do is optimize the score you actually collect while still exploring. SARSA optimizes that instead. Two different questions, two correct answers.

    The tell: decay ε toward 0 and SARSA drifts over to the edge route too, because the thing it is being honest about stops happening.

    Check yourself

    Why does SARSA's estimate for a square beside the cliff end up lower than Q-learning's?

    • SARSA uses a smaller learning rate, so it moves more slowly.
    • SARSA sees the −100 more often, because it explores more than Q-learning does.
    • SARSA's target sometimes contains the value of the exploratory step that goes over the edge, so the −100 gets averaged into that square.
    • SARSA discounts the future more heavily.
    Answer: C. Option B is the popular near-miss: both agents run the same ε-greedy behavior with the same ε, so neither explores more than the other — if anything Q-learning falls more often, because its greedy path hugs the edge. The difference in the estimates is entirely in what gets written down. Options A and D change hyperparameters that are identical between the two.

    Think it through

    You are training a delivery drone in the real world, where a crash costs a drone. Would you rather it learned with Q-learning or SARSA? What changes once training is finished and you are just flying it?

    During training, SARSA. You are still exploring, and SARSA is the method that takes your own random actions seriously. It will learn routes with margin built in, because it has priced the possibility that its next move is a twitch rather than a plan. Q-learning would keep insisting that the tight route is fine, because its target assumes flawless flying it is not currently doing.

    Once training stops, the argument evaporates. With ε set to 0 there are no random twitches to account for, the two targets become identical, and the optimal policy is the optimal policy. This is the real lesson: on-policy versus off-policy is a statement about the learning phase, not about which method is better in the abstract. Choose based on whether your mistakes during learning are cheap or expensive.

    What to remember

    1. Q instead of V lets you act without a model. Read the row, take the biggest number. That is why control is built on Q.
    2. ε-greedy is the cheap fix for exploration. With ε = 0.1 and 4 actions the greedy choice gets 0.925 and each rival gets 0.025. Decay ε as the estimates improve.
    3. Q-learning uses max over the next row. It learns the optimal policy while behaving badly on purpose — off-policy.
    4. SARSA uses the action actually taken next. Same shape, one symbol different, and it learns about the exploring agent it really is — on-policy.
    5. On the cliff, SARSA walks the safe row. Its target sometimes contains a step off the edge, so the −100 sinks the edge squares' values. Q-learning's max never sees that fall.

    Lesson 11

    Deep RL: When the Table Is Too Big

    Everything so far assumed you could write one number per state on a shelf and go find it later. For the room that is 44 numbers. For a video game screen it is a number with seventy thousand digits. This lesson is about what you do when the shelf runs out — and about the two strange bugs that show up the moment you stop using a shelf.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say why a lookup table fails on real problems, with numbers
    • Explain what a function with parameters θ buys you that a table cannot
    • Read the DQN loss and say what each half of it is doing
    • Explain the moving-target problem and how a frozen copy fixes it

    The shelf runs out

    The room has 11 states and 4 actions, so its Q-table is 44 numbers. You could write it on a napkin. Now count the same way for something real.

    Lesson 5 counted these: about 10172 Go positions, on the order of 1070,000 distinct Atari screens, against roughly 1080 atoms in the observable universe. The table is not merely large. It cannot exist.

    The deeper problem

    Suppose someone handed you infinite storage. You still could not learn. A table has no notion that two entries are related, so knowing the value of one screen tells you nothing about the screen where a single pixel moved. You would have to visit every state, many times each, and there are more of them than there are seconds in the age of the universe. Storage is the obvious problem; generalization is the real one.

    One function instead of a table

    So stop storing answers and store a recipe for producing them. Feed the state in one end, get values out the other, and keep only the recipe's adjustable settings. Those settings are θ, the parameters we first named in Lesson 5.

    Approximating the action-value function
    Q(s, a; θ) ≈ Q*(s, a)
    Q(s, a; θ) Our estimated value of doing a in s — now computed, not looked up.
    ; θ The semicolon separates inputs from settings. s and a change every step; θ is the same for all of them and changes only when we learn. Read it as "given the current settings".
    θ Theta, the parameters: one long list of adjustable numbers. A few million of them, typically. This is the entire memory of the agent.
    Q*(s, a) The true optimal action-value from Lesson 7 — the thing we wish we knew. The star means "the best possible", not "our estimate".
    ≈ "Approximately equals", and it is doing real work. We will never match Q* exactly with a few million numbers — only closely enough to act well.

    The payoff is generalization. Two screens that differ by one pixel arrive at the function as almost the same input, so they come out with almost the same value — for free, without either one being visited twice. Experience in one state now improves the estimate for a whole neighborhood of states you have never seen.

    What a neural network is

    A neural network is a box containing a few million numbers you are allowed to change, and that collection of numbers is what we call θ. It takes an input, runs it through a fixed pattern of multiplications and additions using those numbers, and produces an output. Whenever the output is wrong, you measure how wrong, and nudge every number in θ a tiny step in the direction that would have made it less wrong. Do that a few million times and the box starts producing useful outputs.

    That is the whole of it for our purposes. From here on the network is a black box with a dial marked θ, and the only question we care about is what error we tell it to reduce.

    Figure 11.1 — Left, the table: mostly empty, and it never fills up because almost every row is visited zero times. Right, the same job done by a function whose entire contents are a fixed-size list of numbers. The compression is not the point — the sharing is. Every update to θ moves the estimate for millions of states at once.

    One practical detail. It would be wasteful to feed in a state and an action and get back one number, because choosing greedily would mean running the network once per action. Instead the network takes the state alone and produces the whole row at once, one output per action.

    Figure 11.2 — The shape of a deep Q-network. The state goes in whole; four numbers come out, one per action. This is exactly the row of a Q-table from Lesson 10, except that it is computed on demand instead of retrieved. Everything you learned about ε-greedy still applies, unchanged, to these four numbers.

    Turning an update into an error

    A table update was an instruction: overwrite this cell. A network has no cells to overwrite — you can only tell it what it got wrong. So restate Q-learning as an error to be reduced. The target is the same one from Lesson 10; the error is how far the network's output sits from it; and we square that error so that being wrong in either direction counts as being wrong.

    The DQN loss
    target = R + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ−)
    L(θ) = 𝔼 [ ( target − Q(s, a; θ) )2 ]
    L(θ) The loss: one number saying how badly the current settings are doing. Lower is better. Training means pushing this down.
    target The reward that actually arrived plus the discounted best value at the next state — exactly Q-learning's target from Lesson 10, with the table lookup replaced by a network.
    R The observed reward, the one Lesson 3 wrote as Rt+1. Nothing about the timing convention has changed.
    γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ−) The discount γ times the largest of the four numbers the frozen network produces for the next state s′. Exactly Lesson 10's max, with a network doing the lookup.
    θ− Theta-minus: a frozen copy of θ from several thousand steps ago. Why it has to be frozen is the second half of this lesson; for now, read it as a label, not a minus sign.
    𝔼[ … ] "Average over". In practice, the average across a batch of stored transitions, not over any infinity.
    Q(s, a; θ) The prediction: what the live network currently says about the action we actually took. This is the only part we are allowed to move.
    ( … )2 Squaring makes over- and under-shooting count the same, and makes big errors matter much more than small ones.

    With an error defined, learning is just walking downhill on it.

    One gradient descent step
    θ ← θ − α ∇θ L(θ)
    ∇θ L(θ) The gradient. Read it as: "the direction you would nudge every number in θ to make the loss go up fastest." You are given this direction; you do not have to know how it is computed.
    − The minus sign is the whole idea. The gradient points uphill, so step the other way.
    α The same learning rate as in Lesson 9, doing the same job: how big a step to take. Too big and you overshoot; too small and nothing moves.
    θ ← Every number in θ is updated at once, by its own small amount. This happens millions of times over training.

    Check yourself

    In the DQN loss, why does the target use θ− while the prediction uses θ?

    • Because the target must be computed on a different, smaller network.
    • Because the target is meant to stand still while we move the prediction toward it.
    • Because the minus sign means the target is subtracted.
    • Because the target uses the previous state rather than the next one.
    Answer: B. Both networks have the identical shape — θ− is a snapshot of θ, not a different design, which rules out A. Option C reads the superscript as arithmetic; it is a name, not an operation. The point of freezing it is coming up next: if the target moves every time the prediction moves, there is nothing stable to aim at.

    Two bugs, two repairs

    Bolt a network onto Q-learning exactly as described so far and it usually falls apart. Two things go wrong, and each has a fix that is almost embarrassingly simple.

    Bug one: your data arrives in a straight line

    Consecutive steps are nearly identical. Frame 900 looks like frame 901 looks like frame 902. Train on them in order and the network spends a whole minute learning only about one corridor, forgets what it knew about the rest, then spends the next minute unlearning that. Worse, every experience is used once and thrown away.

    The fix is experience replay. Every transition — state, action, reward, next state — goes into a large buffer, typically the last million of them. Training draws a random handful, say 32, from anywhere in that history. Old and new, from twenty different situations, all mixed together.

    Figure 11.3 — Look at the step numbers on the right. The four transitions being learned from happened at wildly different moments — 812, 40,109, 3, 27,750 — so the network sees a shuffled cross-section of its whole life rather than one corridor in order. The buffer also means each experience gets reused dozens of times before it is pushed out, which matters when every sample costs a real game frame.

    Bug two: you are aiming at something you are pushing

    This is the subtle one, and it is why there is a θ− in the target above rather than a plain θ. Write the loss the obvious way — the same θ in the target and in the prediction — and every time you nudge θ to bring the prediction closer to the target, you also move the target.

    Think of it like

    A dog chasing its own tail. Every lunge forward moves the thing it is lunging at by exactly the same amount, so it can spin all afternoon and never close the gap. Worse than futile: because the two are linked, a small overshoot raises the target, which raises the prediction, which raises the target again. Values in a broken DQN do not wander — they blow up.

    The fix is a frozen copy, called a target network. Keep a second set of parameters, θ−, which is simply θ as it was some thousands of steps ago. Compute every target with the target network. Train only the live one. Every ten thousand steps, copy the live parameters over the frozen ones and carry on.

    Figure 11.4 — Time runs left to right in both panels. On the left the red target runs away exactly as fast as the blue estimate chases it, so the gap never closes and both drift upward together. On the right the target is a flat dashed line: held still for ten thousand steps, so the estimate can actually reach it, and only then bumped to a new level. Slower per step, and it is the difference between learning and diverging.

    Where people get lost

    "But the frozen copy is out of date, so the target is wrong." Yes — and that is fine. A stale target that holds still is far more useful than a fresh one that squirms, because gradient descent can only make progress toward something that stays put long enough to be approached. You trade a little accuracy for enough stability to have any accuracy at all.

    The whole loop, as steps

    1. Feed the current state through the live network and read off one value per action.
    2. Pick an action with ε-greedy on those values.
    3. Take it. Record the state, the action, the reward, and the state you landed in. Put that record in the replay buffer.
    4. Draw 32 records at random from the buffer, from anywhere in your history.
    5. For each one, compute the target using the frozen copy: the reward, plus γ times the frozen copy's best value at the next state.
    6. Compute each record's squared gap between the live network's prediction and that target, and average the 32 numbers. That is the loss.
    7. Take one gradient descent step on θ to lower it.
    8. Every ten thousand steps, copy θ into the frozen copy.
    9. Go back to step 1.

    Check yourself

    What goes wrong if you train on transitions in the order they happened, with no replay buffer?

    • Nothing — the order of the data does not affect learning.
    • The agent stops exploring, because ε can no longer decay.
    • Consecutive samples are nearly identical, so the network overfits whatever it is doing right now and forgets the rest.
    • The rewards arrive one step late and the timing convention breaks.
    Answer: C. This is the one place where "the data is shuffled" stops being a technicality. In supervised learning your dataset arrives already mixed; in RL it arrives as a movie, one nearly-identical frame after another. Option B confuses exploration with data ordering — they are independent. Replay also buys reuse: a frame costs real time to collect, and the buffer lets you learn from it many times.

    DQN and the Atari moment

    All of this landed at once. In 2013, and more fully in a 2015 paper, a group at DeepMind put these pieces together — a convolutional network reading raw pixels, the squared TD error as the loss, experience replay, and a target network — and called it DQN, for deep Q-network. They pointed it at 49 Atari games.

    The detail that made it famous was not the scores. It was that the same network shape and the same settings were used for all 49 games, with no per-game tuning and no hand-written features. The input was the screen and the score; the output was joystick directions. On roughly half the games it reached or beat a professional human tester, and on a few it found strategies the testers had not.

    The one thing to keep

    Deep RL is not a new theory. It is Lesson 10's update with the lookup replaced by a function — plus two repairs, replay and a frozen target, that exist only because the lookup is gone.

    Check yourself

    Which of these is the strongest reason a network beats a table for Atari, rather than merely a convenient one?

    • A network uses less memory than a table would.
    • A network can represent negative Q values, which a table cannot.
    • A network gives similar states similar values, so one visit teaches it about states it has never seen.
    • A network removes the need for exploration, so ε-greedy is no longer required.
    Answer: C. Option A is true and beside the point: infinite memory would not save the table, because you would still have to visit every screen at least once and there are more screens than seconds in the age of the universe. B is simply false — a table stores whatever numbers you put in it. D is false too; ε-greedy sits on top of the four outputs exactly as before. Generalization is the whole argument.

    Think it through

    The frozen copy is only ever a slightly out-of-date version of the same network. Why does that help at all — and what would go wrong if you refreshed it every single step, or never refreshed it?

    It helps because the problem was never accuracy, it was motion. Gradient descent works by taking many small steps toward a fixed point. If the point moves in response to every step, the errors feed back on themselves and the values inflate. Freezing breaks the feedback loop: for ten thousand steps the target is a constant as far as the optimizer is concerned, which turns an unstable chase into an ordinary regression problem.

    Refresh every step and θ− equals θ, so you are back to the dog and its tail — the fix disappears. Never refresh and the target stays anchored to whatever the network believed at initialization, which is nothing; the estimate converges neatly to a wrong answer and stops. The refresh interval is a dial between stability and speed, and ten thousand steps is just a value that worked.

    What to remember

    1. Tables die twice. They cannot fit real state spaces, and even if they could, they never share what they learn between similar states.
    2. Replace the table with Q(s, a; θ). A fixed list of parameters computes values on demand, and generalization comes free.
    3. The loss is the squared TD error. Same target as Q-learning; the only new idea is measuring the gap instead of overwriting a cell.
    4. Experience replay breaks the correlation. Store transitions, sample randomly, and reuse each one many times.
    5. A target network stops the goalpost moving. Compute targets from a frozen copy θ−, refreshed every few thousand steps.

    Lesson 12

    Policy Gradients: Learning the Policy Directly

    For eleven lessons you have learned how good things are, and let good behavior fall out as a consequence. This lesson cuts out the middleman. It adjusts the behavior itself: make the actions that went well more likely, make the actions that went badly less likely, in proportion to how they went.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Name three situations where learning values first is the wrong route
    • Say what the policy gradient claims, in one plain sentence
    • Read ∇θ log π without knowing any calculus
    • Run REINFORCE as a seven-step procedure
    • Show with numbers why subtracting a baseline is nearly free and nearly always worth it

    Skipping the middleman

    Every method in Parts II and III took the same route: learn how good things are, then behave well as a side effect. Q-learning never stores a policy at all — the policy is a by-product, "look up the four numbers for this state and take the biggest".

    That route works, and for the room it works well. It breaks in three specific places.

    Continuous actions. "Take the biggest" means checking every action. A robot elbow's torque is a real number, so there are not four options, there are infinitely many. You cannot loop over them.

    Deliberate randomness. A greedy policy is deterministic. In rock-paper-scissors, from Lesson 5, a deterministic player loses to anyone who watches for two minutes. Value-based methods bolt randomness on from outside with ε, uniformly and blindly. A policy can carry its own randomness and learn how much of it to keep, and where.

    Jumpy updates. Suppose two actions score 4.90 and 4.91. One small update flips which is larger, and the behavior in that state swings from "always left" to "always right". The values moved by 0.02; the policy moved all the way. Change a policy's parameters slightly and its probabilities move slightly instead.

    Figure 12.1 — The same experience, two routes to the same destination. The upper route goes through a value function and has to stop and compare numbers before it can act. The lower route has no stop: experience adjusts the behavior directly. Notice that the lower route has one box where the upper has two — that missing box is the middleman this lesson removes.

    The hill we are climbing

    From here the policy is a parameterized one, as in Lesson 5: a bundle of adjustable numbers called θ that takes a state in and produces a probability for each action. In a small problem θ is a dozen numbers; in a language model it is billions. The machinery is identical.

    What we are trying to maximize
    J(θ) = 𝔼[ G0 | πθ ]
    θ The parameters. The full list of adjustable numbers inside the policy — every knob you are allowed to turn. This is the only thing in the whole equation you get to change.
    πθ The policy that those knobs produce. Change θ and you have a different policy, so writing the subscript is a way of saying "the behavior is a function of the knobs".
    J(θ) The objective: one number saying how good this setting of the knobs is. Same J as Lesson 5, now written as a function of θ instead of a function of π.
    𝔼[ … ] "Average over many runs." One episode is luck; this is what you would score on average across thousands of them.
    G0 The discounted return of a whole episode, counted from step 0 — the total score, as defined in Lesson 3.
    | Read as "given that". The average is taken over episodes generated by this policy and no other.

    Now the plan. J is a landscape: every possible setting of the knobs is a location, and the height at that location is the average score. We want the top. So take a small step uphill, look again, step again. That is gradient ascent — the same hill-climbing Lesson 11 used to train a network, run in the other direction: downhill to shrink an error there, uphill to grow a score here.

    The obstacle

    You cannot see the hill. J(θ) is an average over episodes, and episodes run through the environment — which you have no formula for. You cannot ask "if I nudged knob 400, how would the room respond?" Finding the uphill direction anyway is the entire problem this lesson solves.

    The policy gradient, in one sentence

    Here is the whole result, before any notation.

    The claim

    Push up the probability of actions that were followed by good returns. Push down the probability of actions that were followed by bad returns. Push in proportion to how good or how bad. Do that with the actions you actually sampled, and on average you are walking uphill on J.

    This is the policy gradient theorem. The remarkable part is that it needs no knowledge of the environment at all — the dynamics drop out. You get an uphill direction from nothing but actions you took and returns you observed.

    The policy gradient theorem
    ∇θJ(θ) = 𝔼[ Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · Gt ]
    θ The parameters again — the knobs inside the policy.
    ∇θ Read as "the direction in θ-space that increases". It is not a number, it is an arrow with one component per knob, saying which way and how hard to turn each one. You will never compute one by hand; software does it.
    ∇θJ(θ) The direction that increases the average score. This arrow is the answer we want. Step along it and the policy gets better.
    𝔼[ … ] Average over episodes played by the current policy. In practice you replace this average with one episode, or a batch of them.
    Σt Add up over every time step in the episode. Every decision the agent made gets its own term.
    ∇θ log πθ(at|st) Treat this whole cluster as one word. It means: the direction in parameter space that makes action at more likely in state st. Nothing more.
    Gt The return from step t onward — how the rest of the episode actually turned out after that decision. A plain number.
    · Ordinary multiplication. It scales the arrow: a big return means a big step, a negative return flips the arrow around.

    The one thing to accept on faith

    You are being asked to take exactly one piece of notation without justification, and this is it:

    Read it this way and move on

    ∇θ log πθ(a|s) is the direction in parameter space that makes action a more likely in state s.

    That is the complete meaning. It is an arrow, one number per knob, pointing whichever way you would turn the knobs to raise the chance that the policy picks a when it sees s. It knows nothing about reward and has no opinion about whether a was a good idea. It only knows how to make a more likely.

    The reward enters separately, as the multiplier Gt. That split is the elegance of the whole method:

    • Gt large and positive — a long step along the arrow; much more likely.
    • Gt small and positive — a short step; slightly more likely.
    • Gt negative — a step backwards along the arrow; less likely.
    • Gt zero — no movement at all.

    Figure 12.2 — One state in the room, four actions. The agent happened to sample "up" and the episode went well, so "up" gets pushed to 0.34. The three actions it did not take are never mentioned by the update, yet all three shrink — probabilities must add to 1, so raising one lowers the rest automatically. That is worth sitting with: policy gradients only ever talk about the action you actually tried.

    Where people get lost

    Nothing here computes "the right action". The update never compares at against a better alternative, because no better alternative was observed. It only re-weights the actions the agent happened to sample. If a genuinely great action is never tried, policy gradients will never find it, which is why the policy has to stay random enough to keep trying things.

    Check yourself

    In one episode the agent took "left" from the start cell and the episode's return was −3. What does the update do to the probability of "left" in that state?

    • Nothing, because the update only handles positive returns.
    • Lowers it — the direction is multiplied by a negative number, so the step runs backwards along it.
    • Raises it, because the agent still needs to explore that action.
    • Sets it to zero, because the action was shown to be bad.
    Answer: B. The ∇θ log π cluster always points toward "make this action more likely". Multiplying by −3 reverses it and stretches it, so you step three units in the "make this less likely" direction. Option D is the tempting one: the step is small and proportional, never a wipeout — one bad episode is evidence, not a verdict.

    REINFORCE

    Turning the theorem into a procedure gives the oldest policy gradient algorithm, from 1992:

    1. Set the parameters θ to anything. Random is fine — a random policy just picks actions roughly uniformly.
    2. Play one complete episode. At each step, feed the state into the policy, read off the action probabilities, and roll the dice on them.
    3. When the episode ends, walk backwards through it computing Gt, the return from each step onward, exactly as in Lesson 3.
    4. For each step, take the direction that would make the action you actually took more likely from the state you were actually in.
    5. Scale each of those directions by its own Gt and add them all together.
    6. Take one small step of size α along that sum. The parameters have now changed.
    7. Throw the episode away and go back to step 2.
    The REINFORCE update
    θ ← θ + α Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · Gt
    θ The parameters of the policy — all the knobs, treated as one long list of numbers.
    ← "Becomes". Read the whole line as a recipe: compute the right-hand side, then store it back into θ.
    α The learning rate from Lesson 9. How far to walk along the direction. Too big and you overshoot into a policy worse than the one you started with; too small and nothing happens.
    Σt Sum over every step of the episode, so one episode produces one combined update rather than many small ones.
    ∇θ log πθ(at|st) As before: the direction that makes the sampled action more likely in the state it was sampled from.
    Gt The actual observed return after that step. Note "actual" — this is a measured number from one real episode, not an estimate.

    Why step 7 throws the data away

    The returns in that episode describe the policy that generated them. The moment θ changes, the episode is a report about an agent that no longer exists. This makes REINFORCE strictly on-policy and strictly wasteful: every episode is used once. Lesson 14 shows the trick that buys back several updates per batch.

    Check yourself

    Why can REINFORCE not update after every single step, the way TD learning in Lesson 9 could?

    • Because the policy has parameters, and parameters can only change once per episode.
    • Because it needs Gt, the real return, and the real return is not known until the episode is over.
    • Because the environment does not report rewards until the end.
    • Because summing over t requires all steps to have equal returns.
    Answer: B. This is the Monte Carlo restriction from Lesson 9 showing up again in a new place: if your target is the actual return, you have to wait for the actual return. Option C is a different situation — many environments pay out every step, and REINFORCE still has to wait, because Gt sums all of those rewards to the end. Lesson 13 removes the wait.

    The problem with raw returns

    Consider a balancing task: keep a pole upright, score +1 for every step you stay up, and episodes run about a hundred steps. From one particular state, three actions are available. You try each one, and the episodes score 100, 102, and 104.

    Which action is best? Obviously "right", by two points. Now watch what REINFORCE does with that.

    Action takenReturnMultiplier used by REINFORCEEffect on that action
    left100100pushed up, hard
    hold102102pushed up, harder
    right104104pushed up, hardest

    All three get pushed up, and by almost the same amount. The multiplier for the worst action is 100 and for the best is 104 — a difference of four percent. The signal you care about is the 4-point spread between the actions. The 100 points they share tell you nothing about which to prefer, and they are doing 96% of the shouting.

    Worse, you only ever sample one action per visit. If "left" happens to come up three times and "right" once, "left" receives three shoves of size 100 and "right" one of size 104. The policy moves toward the worse action, purely because of sampling luck. The learning becomes a story about which action got sampled, not which action was better.

    The fix: compare against average

    Subtract a number that does not depend on which action you took. Call it the baseline, written b(s). The obvious choice is the average return from that state — here, 102.

    Action takenReturnMinus baseline 102Effect on that action
    left100−2pushed down
    hold1020left alone
    right104+2pushed up

    Read the two tables side by side. Nothing about the world changed — the same three episodes, the same three returns — but the second table gets the answer right and the first does not.

    And notice what the sign is now doing. In the first table every multiplier was positive, so the sign carried no information at all. In the second, a single sampled episode already tells you which way to move: take "left" once, see 100, and that alone pushes "left" down. Sampling luck no longer sets the direction; comparison against average does.

    Figure 12.3 — The same three numbers, measured from two different places. On the left the bars are so nearly equal that the picture carries no usable information, and every one of them points upward. On the right the reference line has moved to 102 and the differences become the entire figure. The right panel is drawn at roughly forty times the scale of the left, which is exactly the complaint: without a baseline, the part you care about is a rounding error on the part you do not.

    REINFORCE with a baseline
    θ ← θ + α Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · ( Gt − b(st) )
    θ The policy's parameters, as before.
    ← "Becomes" — compute the right-hand side and store it back.
    α The learning rate: how far to step along the combined direction.
    Σt Sum over every step of the episode.
    ∇θ log πθ(at|st) The direction that makes the sampled action more likely. Unchanged from the previous formula — the baseline touches only the multiplier.
    b(st) The baseline: any number you like, as long as it does not depend on which action was taken. Writing st inside the brackets says it may depend on the state, and usually should.
    Gt − b(st) "How much better than usual did this turn out?" The number whose sign now decides whether the action gets more likely or less.

    Two things make this safe. First, subtracting a baseline does not change what the update converges to: because b(s) is the same for every action in that state, its contribution averages out to exactly zero, so the expected step is unchanged. It removes spread, not signal. Second, the best baseline is the one you spent Parts II and III learning — the value function V(s), which is by definition the average return from s. That is where the next lesson starts.

    Check yourself

    Someone edits the environment so that finishing an episode pays a one-time bonus of 1000. The three returns become 1100, 1102, 1104. What happens?

    • Both plain REINFORCE and the baseline version are unaffected — the ordering did not change.
    • The baseline version is unchanged; plain REINFORCE gets updates about eleven times larger that say the same thing even less clearly.
    • Both break, because negative returns are now impossible.
    • Only the baseline version breaks, because 102 is no longer the right baseline.
    Answer: B. With the baseline now at 1102, the multipliers are still −2, 0, +2 — bit for bit the same update. Plain REINFORCE multiplies every direction by roughly 1100 instead of roughly 100, so it takes eleven times bigger steps while the useful 4-point spread stays 4. Option A is the trap: the ordering is indeed unchanged, but plain REINFORCE never used the ordering, only the raw magnitudes. Option D misreads the baseline — it is whatever the average currently is, so it moves with the returns.

    Think it through

    A friend objects: "If you subtract the average, the multipliers average out to zero, so on average the update does nothing and the policy never learns." What is wrong with the objection?

    The multipliers do sum to zero. But each one multiplies a different direction. The +2 pushes up the probability of "right"; the −2 pushes down the probability of "left". Those are two different knob-turnings, so they do not cancel — together they re-rank the actions, which is exactly what learning a policy means.

    What does cancel is the part of the update that was identical across all three actions: the shared 102. That part was pushing every action up equally, which changes no probabilities at all once they are renormalized, while still making every individual step enormous and noisy. Removing it is pure gain.

    Said precisely: the baseline leaves the average update unchanged and shrinks its spread. Same destination, far less staggering on the way.

    What to remember

    1. Policy gradients adjust behavior directly. No value function in the middle, so continuous actions, genuinely random policies, and smooth updates all become available.
    2. The claim is one sentence. Push up actions that were followed by good returns, push down actions followed by bad ones, in proportion.
    3. Read ∇θ log π as one word. It is the direction that makes this action more likely — nothing else. The return is a separate multiplier that decides how far and which way you go.
    4. REINFORCE waits for the end. It needs the real return, so it updates once per episode and then discards the episode.
    5. Always subtract a baseline. Comparing against average instead of against zero costs nothing, biases nothing, and turns a 4% difference in magnitude into a difference in sign.

    Lesson 13

    Actor–Critic and the Advantage

    Lesson 12 ended with a loose end: the best baseline to subtract is the average return from a state, and the average return from a state is exactly what a value function is. So learn one. That single move fixes both of REINFORCE's problems at once, and hands you a quantity that every method in the rest of this class is built on.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say what the actor and the critic each do, and why neither is enough alone
    • Define the advantage and read what its sign is telling you
    • Explain how a good action can sit inside a bad state
    • Recognize the one-step advantage as an old friend from Lesson 9
    • Walk through the actor–critic loop step by step

    Two learners, one stream of experience

    REINFORCE, from the last lesson, pays two prices. It has to play a whole episode before it can learn anything, because it needs the real return. And that real return is a single sample of one possible future: play the same episode again from the same state and you might get a wildly different number. The updates are honest but jumpy.

    A value function fixes both. It gives you an estimate of the future immediately, without waiting, and an estimate is smoother than a sample because it has already averaged over many episodes. So put two learners side by side and feed them the same experience.

    Vocabulary

    Actor
    The policy. It looks at the state and chooses what to do. This is the thing you actually deploy — everything else is scaffolding.
    Critic
    A value function. It never chooses anything. Its only job is to say what score it expected from this state, so the actor can tell whether things went better or worse than expected.
    Advantage
    The gap between what happened and what the critic expected. The number that drives the actor's update.

    The names are literal. The actor performs; the critic sits in the audience and grades. The critic cannot act and the actor cannot judge itself.

    Figure 13.1 — The same state goes into both heads. The actor turns it into an action; the critic turns it into a predicted score. When the environment answers, the gap between prediction and reality becomes one small number, and that number travels back up the middle to drive the actor's update. Follow the arrow that goes upward — that is the only channel through which the critic influences behavior.

    The advantage

    Here is the quantity the whole rest of this class runs on.

    The advantage function
    Aπ(s, a) = Qπ(s, a) − Vπ(s)
    s, a A state and one particular action taken from it.
    Qπ(s, a) From Lesson 6: the expected return if you take action a in state s and follow the policy afterward. "How this action turns out, on average."
    Vπ(s) Also Lesson 6: the expected return from state s when the policy chooses the action too. Since the policy is what picks, this is the average over the actions the policy would take — the state's typical outcome.
    π The little π on top is the reminder from Lesson 6 that all three numbers are measured under one particular policy. Change the policy and all three change.
    Aπ(s, a) The advantage: how much better than the state's own average this particular action is. Positive means above average here, negative means below average here. (A is the conventional letter for the advantage; it is unrelated to the action set A from Lesson 4.)

    Subtracting V(s) is precisely the baseline from Lesson 12, and it is the natural one: the baseline should be the average return from that state, and that is the definition of V(s). The advantage is what "compared with average" looks like when you write it down.

    Take a real state in the room. The robot is in the cell directly to the left of the stairs — one wrong step from a −1. Under a policy that still picks uniformly at random, suppose the critic currently holds these four numbers for that state. They are estimates part-way through training, not the true values — which is exactly the situation the actor has to work with.

    Figure 13.2 — One state, four actions. The horizontal line is not zero; it is V(s), the average outcome of this state. Each bar is that action's advantage. Two facts are worth checking by hand: the four Q values average to exactly 0.18, which is why the line sits there, and the four advantages add up to exactly zero, because this policy is uniform. What is always true is the weighted version: averaged over how often the policy takes each action, the advantages come to exactly zero. That is what stops the actor from pushing every action up at once.

    Reading the signs takes five seconds and tells you the whole update. "Up" is strongly above average, so make it much more likely. "Down" and "left" are barely above average, so nudge them up a little. "Right" walks into the stairs and is far below average, so push it down hard.

    Where people get lost

    A negative advantage does not mean the action was bad in absolute terms, and a positive one does not mean it was good. The advantage is measured against the state's own average, and nothing else. Put an agent in a burning building and every action has a miserable Q, yet exactly the same arithmetic applies: the least terrible exit still has a positive advantage, because it beats the average of that particular bad situation.

    Check yourself

    The agent is in a state so bad that every available action has a negative Q. What do the advantages look like?

    • Some positive and some negative, because they are measured against that state's own average.
    • All zero, because there is nothing good to compare against.
    • Undefined, because the advantage requires a positive value function.
    Answer: B. Option A is the natural guess and it is wrong for an important reason. V(s) is the average of those same negative Q values, so it is negative too, and subtracting a negative number lifts everything. Roughly half the actions come out above the line. The advantage cannot tell you whether you are in trouble — only what to do now that you are.

    A good action in a bad state

    The cleanest way to feel the difference between Q and A is to find one number that gets two opposite verdicts. Here is one.

    Figure 13.3 — Two cells of the room, the blue one marking where the robot is. In both, the action "down" is worth 0.32. On the left that is a step away from danger and comfortably better than the state's average of 0.18, so the actor should do it more. On the right it is walking away from the charger, far below that state's average of 0.70, so the actor should do it less. The raw Q value cannot tell those two situations apart; the advantage can.

    This is why the advantage is the right currency for a policy update. The actor does not need to know whether it is in a good part of the world — it can do nothing about that. It only needs to know which of the options in front of it beat the local average. Subtracting V(s) deletes exactly the part of the score that the action had no influence over.

    The advantage you can actually compute

    There is an obvious objection. The formula asks for Q(s, a) and V(s), and you do not have either. Learning both would mean maintaining two value functions, which rather defeats the point of a lightweight critic.

    So estimate Q from the single step you just took. Q(st, at) is the reward you got plus the discounted value of where you landed — that is the Bellman relation from Lesson 7, with the average replaced by one sample. Substitute it in, and something happens.

    The one-step advantage estimate
    A(st, at) ≈ Rt+1 + γ V(st+1) − V(st) = δt
    A(st, at) The advantage of the action actually taken at step t — the thing we wanted and could not compute.
    ≈ "Approximately equals". This is an estimate from one sample, not an identity. It is right on average and wrong on any given step.
    Rt+1 The reward that came back after the action, following the +1 timing convention from Lesson 3. The only part of this line that is a measurement rather than a guess.
    γ The discount factor from Lesson 3, between 0 and 1. It shrinks the value of the next state because that value lies one step further away.
    V(st+1) The critic's score for where you landed. If st+1 is terminal this is 0 — nothing follows the end of an episode.
    V(st) The critic's score for where you started. What it expected before the action was taken.
    δt The TD error at step t, defined in Lesson 9 as exactly this expression. Not something similar to it. The same three terms in the same order.

    The payoff

    Look at the right-hand side again. Reward, plus discounted value of the next state, minus value of this one. That is δt, the TD error you met in Lesson 9 as the engine of temporal-difference learning.

    Nobody arranged this. We wanted "how much better than average was this action", chased it through Q − V, replaced Q with a single sampled step, and landed on a formula that was already sitting there for a completely different purpose. The prediction error a critic makes about a state is the advantage of the action that produced it. One quantity, computed once per step, tells the critic how wrong it was and tells the actor what to do about it.

    Worked numbers

    The robot is in the cell left of the stairs, where the critic says V = 0.18. It takes "up", the move works, no reward arrives, and it lands in the cell left of the charger, where the critic says V = 0.70. With γ = 0.9:

    δ = 0 + 0.9 × 0.70 − 0.18 = 0.63 − 0.18 = +0.45

    Compare that with Figure 13.2, where the true advantage of "up" in that state is +0.45. Same number, obtained without playing the rest of the episode.

    Now the honest version. Suppose instead the robot slips sideways into the stairs. Then R = −1, the episode ends so V(s′) = 0, and δ = −1 − 0.18 = −1.18. The same action, the same state, a wildly different number. One step is one sample. It is right on average and noisy in particular, which is the trade you are making.

Option D forgets to discount the 0.18. The sign is what matters most here — it is negative, so "down" from that cell becomes less likely.

    The two updates and the loop

    Both learners now run off the same number. The critic uses δ to correct its own prediction, exactly as in Lesson 9.

    The critic's update
    V(st) ← V(st) + αV δt
    V(st) The critic's stored score for the state it just left. In a small problem this is one entry in a table; with a network it is one output, and the parameters move instead. The idea does not change.
    ← "Becomes". Compute the right-hand side, store it back.
    αV The critic's learning rate. The subscript only names which learner it belongs to; the actor gets its own, and they are usually different.
    δt The TD error. Positive means the state turned out better than the critic expected, so raise its score; negative means lower it.

    The actor uses the same δ as the multiplier in the policy gradient update from Lesson 12, in place of the baselined return.

    The actor's update
    θ ← θ + αθ ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · δt
    θ The actor's parameters — every adjustable knob inside the policy.
    ← "Becomes". Compute and store back.
    αθ The actor's learning rate. Usually smaller than the critic's, because a bad policy step is far more expensive than a bad prediction.
    ∇θ log πθ(at|st) The one piece of notation from Lesson 12, unchanged: the direction in parameter space that makes this action more likely in this state.
    δt The advantage estimate, standing in for Gt − b(st). Its sign decides whether the action gets more likely or less; its size decides by how much.

    Put together, the algorithm is short:

    1. Start with two things: a policy πθ (the actor) and a value estimate V (the critic). Both begin roughly random.
    2. Look at the current state st.
    3. Sample an action from the actor's probabilities and take it.
    4. The environment returns a reward Rt+1 and a next state st+1.
    5. Compute δt — one line of arithmetic, three numbers.
    6. Update the critic: add αVδt to its score for st.
    7. Update the actor: step θ by αθδt along the direction that makes the action just taken more likely. If δt is negative that direction reverses and the action becomes less likely.
    8. Move to st+1 and go back to step 2. Nothing waits for the episode to end.

    Compare that with REINFORCE's seven steps from last lesson. The whole "play an episode, then walk backwards computing returns" phase is gone. The agent learns while it is still moving.

    One knob you will see named later

    The one-step estimate leans hard on the critic being right, while the full return from Lesson 12 leans on nothing but is very noisy. There is a whole family of estimates in between, built from two-step, three-step and longer lookaheads. Generalized Advantage Estimation blends them with a single knob λ between 0 and 1: λ = 0 gives you exactly the TD error above, λ = 1 gives you the Monte Carlo version, and around 0.95 buys most of the variance reduction for a little bias. You will see it as a hyperparameter in Lesson 14; you do not need to derive it.

    Check yourself

    Compared with REINFORCE, what does actor–critic give up?

    • Nothing — it is strictly better in every respect.
    • Honesty. The return was a real measurement; V(st+1) is a guess, so a wrong critic points the actor slightly wrong.
    • The ability to handle continuous actions.
    • The ability to use a baseline.
    Answer: B. This is the bias–variance trade from Lesson 9 reappearing one level up. REINFORCE's multiplier is noisy but unbiased; actor–critic's is steady but only as correct as the critic. Option C is backwards — the actor is still a policy, so continuous actions are still fine. Option D is backwards too: actor–critic is the baseline idea, with the baseline learned instead of guessed.

    Think it through

    At the start of training the critic's values are nonsense. Why does the actor not get permanently ruined by following a nonsense critic?

    Three reasons, and one honest caveat.

    First, every δ contains Rt+1, a real reward from the real world. That term is not a guess. Each update injects a little reality, so the critic is dragged toward correctness by the environment itself, one step at a time — this is exactly the bootstrapping-still-converges point from Lesson 9.

    Second, the critic has an easier job than the actor. It only has to predict one number per state, and it sees every transition. It usually sharpens faster than the actor moves.

    Third, the steps are small. With a modest αθ, early nonsense mostly makes the actor wander, and wandering is exploration.

    The caveat: this is a real failure mode, not a solved one. A confidently wrong critic can push the actor somewhere bad; the worse actor then generates worse data; the pair spirals. Keeping policy updates small enough that this cannot run away is precisely the problem Lesson 14 is built to solve.

    What to remember

    1. Two learners, one experience stream. The actor chooses, the critic predicts. The critic never acts; it only supplies the number the actor multiplies by.
    2. The advantage is Q − V. How much better than this state's own average a particular action is. Positive means do more of it; negative means do less.
    3. The sign is local. A good action can sit in a terrible state and a bad action in a wonderful one — the same Q of 0.32 was worth +0.14 in one cell and −0.38 in another.
    4. The one-step advantage is the TD error. R + γV(s′) − V(s) from Lesson 9, arriving here from a completely different direction. One number per step trains both halves.
    5. You trade noise for a little bias. Updates arrive every step instead of every episode, and they are far steadier — as long as the critic is roughly right.

    Lesson 14

    PPO: Small, Safe Steps

    Every method so far has assumed that a bad step is just a setback. In reinforcement learning it can be worse than that: a bad step does not only make the agent wrong, it makes the agent's next batch of data useless. Proximal Policy Optimization is an entire algorithm built to stop that from happening.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Say why an oversized policy update is more dangerous in RL than in supervised learning
    • Read the probability ratio and say what a value like 1.5 or 0.8 means
    • Walk through all four clipping cases and say what the minimum does in each
    • Explain why PPO reuses one batch of data, and why that reuse forces the ratio to exist

    A step you cannot take back

    Think about what happens when a supervised model takes a step that is too big. The loss jumps. The model's predictions get worse. And then the next batch arrives — drawn from the same fixed pile of labeled examples it was always going to be drawn from — and the gradient points back the way it came. The training data is a stationary judge. It does not care how badly you just embarrassed yourself. Overshoot, and it will patiently correct you.

    Now the same story in reinforcement learning. The policy is not only the thing being trained; it is also the thing that goes out and collects tomorrow's data. Break it, and you have broken your own measuring instrument.

    Make that concrete in the room. The robot is standing one cell to the left of the stairs. Its current policy there is up 0.55, left 0.20, right 0.15, down 0.10 — sensible, since "right" walks straight into the −1. Now suppose one batch of episodes happens, by noise, to make "right" look good: the advantage estimate for that action comes back at +2 instead of its true negative value. That is normal. Advantage estimates are noisy; Lesson 13 was largely about making them less noisy, not perfect.

    Take one oversized gradient step on that estimate and the probability of "right" goes from 0.15 to 0.82. Watch what follows. Most episodes now end on the stairs within a step or two. Because the episodes are short and all identical, the next batch contains almost no information about the route to the charger. The critic's values for the good cells go stale, because nothing visits them any more. And the policy has become nearly deterministic, so it barely explores — the 0.55 that used to go to "up" is now 0.10, and "up" is sampled once every ten steps instead of once every two.

    Figure 14.1 — The same mistake, two different fates. The vertical axis is how well the agent is doing, higher being better, and both panels take the identical oversized step. On the left, the dataset sits below the model and never moves, so the next gradient drags the model back up. On the right the arrow goes in a circle: the policy produces the data that produces the next policy. Once that circle is poisoned there is nothing outside it to pull things straight.

    That circle is the death spiral: a bad update narrows the policy, the narrowed policy collects narrow and uninformative data, the estimates computed from that data get worse, and the next update is worse still. Nothing in the loop is checking the loop.

    Where people get lost

    The problem is not that the gradient pointed the wrong way. Most of the time it points roughly the right way. The problem is distance. A policy gradient is computed from data collected under the current policy, so it only tells you which way to lean from where you are standing. Walk a long way in that direction and the estimate that justified the walk stopped being true somewhere along it. This is the single hardest thing to internalize about policy optimization: a correct direction plus an oversized step equals a wrong update.

    Think of it like

    A scientist whose next experiment is designed by their current theory. If the theory is roughly right, the experiments probe the right places and the theory sharpens. If one bad result swings the theory hard enough, the scientist stops running the experiments that would have refuted it — and from then on every result confirms the wrong theory. The data was never independent of the belief.

    Check yourself

    Why does a supervised model usually survive one oversized update while an RL agent may not?

    • Supervised gradients are more accurate than policy gradients.
    • The supervised model's training data is unaffected by the update, so the next batch still contains the information needed to correct it.
    • Supervised models use smaller learning rates by convention.
    • RL agents have more parameters, so errors compound faster.
    Answer: B. The asymmetry is about where the data comes from, not about gradient quality or model size. Option A is tempting — policy gradients really are noisier — but even a perfectly accurate gradient taken too far would cause the same collapse, because the damage is done by the policy's new behavior, not by the estimate. Option D is irrelevant: the spiral happens just as readily with a small policy.

    The ratio: measuring how far you actually moved

    If oversized steps are the disease, we need a way to measure step size. Not in parameters — nobody can interpret "θ moved by 0.003" — but in behavior. The right measurement is per action: for an action the old policy actually took, how much more or less likely has the new policy made it?

    The probability ratio
    r(θ) = πθ(a | s) πold(a | s)
    r(θ) The probability ratio. One number per stored step. It is 1 when the new policy is unchanged for this action, above 1 when the action became more likely, below 1 when it became less likely.
    θ The parameters being trained right now — the knobs inside the policy network, the same θ from Lesson 5.
    πθ(a|s) The probability the current policy assigns to action a in state s.
    πold(a|s) The probability the frozen policy assigned to the same action — the policy that was actually running when this step was collected. It is a stored number, not recomputed.
    a, s The action that was actually taken, in the state it was taken from. The ratio is only ever computed for actions that appear in the collected batch — never for actions nobody tried.

    Back to the robot standing left of the stairs, where the old policy gave "right" a probability of 0.15. Suppose an update nudges it to 0.18. Then r = 0.18 / 0.15 = 1.2: the new policy is 20 percent more likely to take that action. Nudge it to 0.225 instead and r = 1.5 — half again as likely. Push it down to 0.075 and r = 0.5 — half as likely.

Third, the last row is the disaster, and it is a factor of 5.5. Any sane cap would have stopped it long before.

    Why a ratio and not a difference

    There is a second reason, and it connects to Lesson 12. If you multiply the ratio by the advantage, r(θ)A is the policy-gradient objective rewritten so that it can be evaluated on data collected by a different policy. At the moment training starts, θ equals θold, every ratio is exactly 1, and the gradient of r(θ)A is precisely the ordinary policy gradient you already know. The ratio is not a new idea bolted on. It is the old idea, written so old data stays usable.

    Check yourself

    A batch is collected. Before any gradient step has been taken, what is the value of r(θ) for every stored action?

    • 0, because no learning has happened yet.
    • Exactly 1, because the current policy and the frozen policy are the same policy.
    • It depends on the advantage of each action.
    • Somewhere between 0.8 and 1.2, depending on ε.
    Answer: B. The frozen copy is made from the current parameters, so numerator and denominator are identical numbers and the ratio is 1 everywhere. Option A confuses "no change" with "zero" — a ratio of 0 would mean the new policy will never take that action again. Option C is a common slip: the advantage multiplies the ratio later, but it never enters the ratio itself. Option D describes where clipping will try to keep the ratio, not where it starts.

    Clipping: a ceiling, but only on one side

    Now we can state the fix. PPO takes the quantity it wants to maximize, r(θ)A, and builds a second version of it in which the ratio has been forced back inside a narrow band around 1. "Clipping" a number to the band [0.8, 1.2] just means: leave it alone if it is already inside, otherwise replace it with whichever edge it fell past. Then PPO takes the smaller of the two versions.

    The clipped surrogate objective
    LCLIP(θ) = 𝔼t[ min( rt(θ)At , clip(rt(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε)At ) ]
    LCLIP(θ) The clipped objective. A score we push upward by adjusting θ. Bigger is better.
    𝔼t[ ] Average over every step t stored in the batch. In practice: compute the inside for each step and take the mean.
    min Take the smaller of the two quantities. This is the piece that does the real work, and the next few paragraphs are entirely about it.
    rt(θ) The probability ratio at step t, from the formula above.
    At The advantage at step t — from Lesson 13, how much better than average that action was. Positive means "do more of this", negative means "do less".
    clip(·) Force the ratio into the band. Below 1−ε it returns 1−ε; above 1+ε it returns 1+ε; in between it returns the ratio untouched.
    ε The clip range, a small positive number. Almost everyone uses 0.2, giving the band [0.8, 1.2]. This is a different ε from Lesson 10's exploration rate — the letter is reused by convention, and the two never appear in the same formula.

    Here is the question that decides whether you understand PPO: why the minimum? If all we wanted was to stop the ratio running away, we could clip and be done. The trouble is that a clipped-only objective is flat on both sides of the band, so a policy that has already drifted too far would feel no pull at all — no gradient, no way home. The minimum fixes exactly that. It keeps the flat ceiling in the direction that would make things worse, and keeps the live, sloped objective in the direction that brings the ratio back toward 1.

    The one thing to keep

    The minimum makes PPO deliberately pessimistic: since min(x, y) ≤ x, the clipped objective is never larger than the raw one. PPO refuses to believe that moving an action's probability far beyond the band bought it anything.

    One honest caveat. Clipping does not forbid a large move; it only removes the reward for making one. With ε = 0.2 the optimizer gains nothing once a probability has moved about 20 percent, so in practice it stops there — though a stack of minibatch steps can still carry a ratio past the edge before the flat region bites.

    Where this came from

    PPO's ancestor is TRPO, which enforced a genuine hard limit on how far the policy could move by solving a constrained optimization problem at every step — correct, but heavy, and awkward to implement. PPO throws away the constraint machinery and gets most of the same behavior with a minimum and a clip, which is why it is sometimes described as a trust region without the hard math.

    Check yourself

    An action has advantage A = −3 and its ratio has drifted to r = 1.7, with ε = 0.2. Which term does the minimum select, and what happens?

    • The clipped term, −3.6, and the objective goes flat.
    • The unclipped term, −5.1, and the objective keeps sloping, pushing the ratio back down toward 1.
    • The clipped term, −3.6, and the ratio is forced to exactly 1.2.

Options A and C pick the wrong one: with a negative advantage the clipped term is the larger number, so the minimum passes it over. That is the whole point — a bad action that has become more likely is precisely the case where you want a live gradient, not a flat one. Option D describes a situation that arises constantly, because the ratio moves for many reasons during an epoch, not only because of this one action's advantage.

    The loop, and why the ratio has to be there

    Here is PPO end to end. Nothing in it is new except step 4.

    1. Freeze a copy of the current policy. Call it the old policy; its probabilities will be stored, not retrained.
    2. Run the old policy in the environment for a fixed number of steps — a few thousand is typical — recording for each step the state, the action taken, the reward, and the probability the old policy gave that action.
    3. Use the critic from Lesson 13 to compute an advantage for every step in the batch — in practice a GAE advantage, with Lesson 13's λ knob set around 0.95.
    4. Shuffle the batch into small minibatches and take several gradient steps over it — usually four to ten passes over the same data. On each step, recompute the ratio for every stored action under the current parameters and push the clipped objective upward.
    5. At the same time, fit the critic to the observed returns and add a small bonus for keeping the policy uncertain, so it does not become deterministic before it has finished exploring.
    6. Throw the batch away. Set the old policy equal to the current one. Go back to step 1.

    Step 4 is where PPO earns its reputation for sample efficiency. REINFORCE and plain actor–critic use each batch exactly once, because both are on-policy: the theory that justifies the gradient assumes the data came from the policy being updated. PPO wants to squeeze more out of every expensive batch. But the instant you take the first gradient step, the current policy is no longer the policy that collected the data, and the on-policy justification is gone.

    The ratio is what buys it back. It reweights each stored step by how much more or less likely the current policy is to have produced it, which keeps the data honest as long as the two policies are close. And "as long as they are close" is exactly the condition the clip enforces. Reuse creates the need for the ratio; the ratio's unreliability at a distance creates the need for the clip. The two halves of PPO are one idea.

    The full objective, all three terms
    L(θ) = LCLIP(θ) − c1LVF(θ) + c2H[πθ]
    L(θ) The single score the optimizer maximizes each gradient step.
    LCLIP(θ) The clipped objective from the previous formula. This is the actor's term — it changes behavior.
    LVF(θ) The value loss: the squared error between the critic's prediction and the return actually observed. It is subtracted because we want it small while we want everything else large.
    c1 How much the critic's accuracy matters relative to the policy. Commonly around 0.5.
    H[πθ] The entropy of the policy — how spread out its action probabilities are. High when the policy is still undecided, near zero when it always picks the same action.
    c2 The size of the entropy bonus, often 0.01. Adding it pays the agent a little for staying uncertain, which slows the collapse of exploration this lesson opened with.

    Check yourself

    Suppose you deleted the ratio and trained directly on the advantage for several epochs over one batch. What breaks?

    • Nothing — the advantages were already computed, so the extra epochs are free.
    • After the first gradient step the data no longer comes from the policy being updated, and with no ratio there is nothing correcting for that mismatch — or capping it.
    • The critic would stop converging, but the policy would be fine.
    • Entropy would rise without bound.
    Answer: B. Option A is the tempting one, and it is precisely the mistake: the advantages are stale the moment the policy moves, and repeatedly pushing on stale advantages is how you get the runaway of the first section. The ratio is the bookkeeping that makes reuse legitimate, and the clip is the brake on how far that bookkeeping can be trusted. Option D has the sign backwards — an uncorrected policy collapses toward certainty, so entropy falls.

    Why this is the one everybody uses

    PPO is not the most elegant algorithm in reinforcement learning, and it is not provably the best. What it is, is dependable. It needs only first-order gradient descent, it works on continuous and discrete action spaces alike, it tolerates a wide range of hyperparameters, and it has essentially one safety knob — ε — whose meaning a person can hold in their head. That is why it became the default, and why Part V builds directly on it.

    The stakes there are higher, not lower. When the environment is a learned reward model rather than a real world, an over-eager policy does not merely collapse — it finds responses that score well and mean nothing, with no true reward to contradict it. Everything in this lesson is about to be applied to a language model, where one batch costs real money.

    Think it through

    Why not just use a much smaller learning rate instead of all this clipping machinery?

    Because a learning rate limits movement in the wrong units. It caps how far θ travels, but the same size step in parameters can change one action's probability by one percent and another's by three hundred percent, depending on where in the policy you happen to be sitting. What you actually want to limit is the change in behavior, and the ratio measures that directly, per action.

    There is a second reason. A small learning rate slows down every update equally, including the great majority that were perfectly safe, so you pay for the rare dangerous step with slowness everywhere and waste the batch you were trying to reuse. Clipping brakes only the specific actions that have already moved too far and leaves the rest at full speed. It is a targeted brake rather than a global one.

    What to remember

    1. In RL a bad update poisons the data. The policy collects its own training set, so an oversized step narrows behavior, the narrowed behavior returns useless data, and the next step is worse. Supervised learning has no such loop.
    2. The ratio measures the step in behavior, not parameters. r = 1 means unchanged, 1.5 means half again as likely, 0.5 means half as likely. It is computed only for actions the old policy actually took.
    3. Clipping caps the objective, and the minimum picks which side. Flat in the direction the update was already pushing; still sloped in the direction that leads back to r = 1.
    4. Only two of the four out-of-band cases are clipped. Good action made much likelier, and bad action made much rarer. The other two keep their gradient so the policy can be pulled home.
    5. Reuse is why the ratio exists. PPO takes several gradient epochs on one batch; the ratio makes that reuse valid, and the clip stops it from being pushed past the point where it stays valid.

    Lesson 15

    RLHF: Teaching a Language Model What "Good" Means

    Fourteen lessons of robots, rooms and cliffs, and it turns out they were about text all along. Nothing new arrives here. The loop from Lesson 2, the policy from Lesson 5, the advantage from Lesson 13, the clipped update from Lesson 14 — the machinery is already right. Only the labels change.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Map every RL word you have learned onto language generation
    • Say precisely why supervised fine-tuning cannot finish the job
    • Read the reward model's preference loss symbol by symbol
    • Explain what the KL penalty prevents, and why it is Lesson 3's failure again

    A language model is already an agent

    A language model produces text one token at a time. A token is roughly a word or a piece of one; the model reads everything written so far, produces a probability for every token in its vocabulary, and one gets picked. Then it does it again, with the new token now part of "everything written so far".

    Read that description again and it is Lesson 2 word for word. Here is the whole mapping.

    In the RL language of Lessons 1–14In language generation

    Action at The next token — one pick from the whole vocabulary
    Policy πθ(a|s) The language model itself, and its next-token probabilities
    Environment A clerk that glues your token onto the end of the text

    Reward R One number, at the very end, for how good the finished response is

    Figure 15.1 — This is the loop from Lesson 2, drawn exactly as it was in Figure 2.1, with new labels on it. That is the entire point: two boxes, two arrows, no new parts. The one thing worth staring at is the right-hand box. In the room, the environment could surprise you; here it only ever appends the token you just chose, which makes it the most boring environment in this whole class.

    Now walk the table one row at a time. Each row hides something.

    State. The state is the prompt plus everything generated so far, growing by exactly one token per step. It satisfies the Markov property from Lesson 4 for free: there is no hidden history, because the history is the state. How the text got written does not matter — only what is on the page.

    Action. One token. Not a sentence, not an idea — a token. Vocabularies run from about 50,000 to 130,000 entries, so at every step the agent chooses among roughly a hundred thousand discrete actions. Lesson 10's Q-table had four. Lesson 11's argument becomes unavoidable here: no table survives, so the policy has to be a network.

    Policy. The model does not have a policy; the model is the policy. When you read πθ(a|s) in an RLHF paper, that is literally the probability the network assigns to the next token. Lesson 5's stochastic policies turn out to be the normal case, not the exotic one: sampling a token rather than always taking the likeliest is exactly a stochastic policy.

    Environment. Nearly trivial. It receives a token, sticks it on the end, and hands the longer text back. Deterministic (Lesson 4), with no dynamics to learn. All the difficulty sits in the reward, which is the opposite of the room.

    Episode. One response. A 400-token answer is a 400-step episode. A conversation is a sequence of episodes.

    Reward. Zero, zero, zero, three hundred more zeros, then a single number when the response ends. This is the sparsest reward in the class, and Lesson 1's credit-assignment problem in its purest form: one score spread backward over four hundred token choices, none of them marked good or bad at the time. Everything Part IV built — baselines, the advantage, the critic — exists to make that spreading survivable.

    Check yourself

    The model produces a 200-token answer. How many actions did the agent take, and how many rewards came back?

    • 200 actions and 200 rewards, one grading each token.
    • 200 actions and one reward, delivered after the last token.
    • One action — the answer — and one reward.
    • 200 actions and no reward, since nothing is scored during generation.
    Answer: B. The action is a token, so 200 tokens is 200 decisions. The reward model only scores finished text, so exactly one number comes back. Option C is the tempting one, and it is how most people first picture this — but if the whole answer were a single action there would be no credit-assignment problem at all, and no reason for anything in Part IV. Option A describes a world that would be much easier and does not exist.

    Why imitation runs out

    The obvious way to make a model helpful is to show it helpful answers. Collect prompts, pay people to write good responses, train the model to reproduce them. That is supervised fine-tuning, and it works — it turns a raw text predictor into something that answers questions instead of continuing them.

    But it has a ceiling, and the ceiling is not about compute. It is about what humans are actually good at.

    Think of it like

    You can tell which of two songs you prefer in about eight seconds, and be consistent about it. You could not write either song. Every person you might hire to teach a model has this shape: their judgment is far sharper than their production.

    This is the honest reason preference learning exists. Humans are much better at comparing two answers than at writing an ideal one. Ask ten skilled people to write the best possible explanation of a mortgage for a nervous first-time buyer and you get ten uneven essays that disagree with each other, written under time pressure by someone not trying their hardest at 2pm on a Tuesday. Ask those same ten which of two explanations is better and they agree, in seconds, most of the time.

    So supervised fine-tuning is limited twice over. It can only copy the demonstrations it was given, which caps the model at the quality of a tired annotator. And it cannot use the signal humans produce most reliably, because a comparison is not a demonstration — there is no text to imitate in "the second one is better".

    A comparison is, however, exactly a score. And "I can grade it but I cannot produce it" is the condition Lesson 1 named as the reason RL exists at all — the last row of that lesson's table was this lesson all along.

    Check yourself

    Why do the labelers compare two answers instead of rating each answer out of ten?

    • Because a rating out of ten cannot be turned into a gradient.
    • Because people are far more consistent judging two answers side by side than assigning an absolute number.
    • Because the reward model can only accept two inputs at once.
    • Because comparisons are quicker to collect, and speed is the only concern.
    Answer: B. Hand the same answer to ten people and ask for a rating and you get 4s and 8s on identical text, plus each person's private scale drifting over the afternoon. Ask which of two is better and agreement jumps. Option D is half true — comparisons are faster — but reliability is the reason, not speed. Option A is simply false: an absolute rating trains perfectly well, it is just noisier data.

    The three stages

    RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — is a pipeline, not an algorithm. Three stages, run in order.

    1. Supervised fine-tuning. Train on written demonstrations until the model answers instructions. Call the result the reference model; it is about to matter twice.
    2. Reward modeling. Sample two responses to the same prompt, have a human pick the better one, and train a separate network to predict that choice as a single number.
    3. Policy optimization. Run PPO from Lesson 14. The policy generates responses, the reward model scores them, the advantage is computed, and small clipped updates nudge the policy.

    Figure 15.2 — Only the third box is reinforcement learning. Stages 1 and 2 are ordinary supervised learning: fixed data in, fixed targets, no agent, no exploration. Follow the dashed line — the Stage 1 model is used twice, once as the starting point for the policy and once as a frozen yardstick the policy is not allowed to wander far from. That second use is the subject of the last section.

    The reward model is the one piece with no counterpart earlier in this class. It takes a prompt and a response and returns one number. Structurally it is a language model with its final layer swapped for a single output, trained on preference pairs with the loss below.

    The Bradley–Terry preference loss
    loss = − log σ( rφ(x, yw) − rφ(x, yl) )
    loss The number being minimized, for one preference pair. Small means the reward model agreed with the human on this pair.
    rφ(x, y) The reward model. Feed it a prompt and a response, get back one number. Bigger means "a human would like this more".
    φ The reward model's own tunable parameters. A different letter from the policy's θ on purpose — these are two separate networks.
    x The prompt.
    yw The winning response — the one the human picked. The w is for won.
    yl The losing response. The l is for lost.
    − The subtraction inside the parentheses. The gap between the two scores is called the margin; positive means the reward model already agrees with the human.
    σ The sigmoid, or logistic function. It squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, so the margin becomes a probability: how sure the model is that the human picked the winner. σ(0) = 0.5, σ(1.5) ≈ 0.82, σ(−1.5) ≈ 0.18.
    −log The negative natural logarithm, which turns that probability into a loss. A probability near 1 gives nearly 0; a probability near 0 gives a very large number, so being confidently wrong is punished hard.

    Worked example

    Suppose the reward model scores the winner 2.4 and the loser 0.9. The margin is 2.4 − 0.9 = 1.5, σ(1.5) ≈ 0.82, so the loss is −log(0.82) ≈ 0.20. Now flip them: winner 0.9, loser 2.4. The margin is −1.5, σ(−1.5) ≈ 0.18, and the loss is −log(0.18) ≈ 1.70 — eight and a half times larger. Notice what the loss ignores: the absolute scores. Scoring the pair 102.4 and 100.9 gives exactly the same 0.20, because only the difference enters. The reward model learns an ordering, not a scale.

    Figure 15.3 — One pass of Stage 2. Two responses to the same prompt, one human judgment, and the loss shoves the two scores apart along a single axis. Watch the small arrows: the loss never says "A should be 2.4". It only ever says "A above B". Repeat that a hundred thousand times across many prompts and the reward model ends up with a coherent notion of better, assembled entirely out of pairwise nudges.

    Where people get lost

    The phrase "human feedback" makes everyone picture a person watching training and grading answers. No human sees any of the millions of responses generated in Stage 3. The humans did their work weeks earlier, on a few tens of thousands of pairs, and their judgment is now frozen inside a network. From the policy's side the environment contains no people at all — only a fixed function to score highly against. The next section is entirely about what goes wrong because of that.

    Check yourself

    Which of the three stages is actually reinforcement learning?

    • Stage 1, supervised fine-tuning.
    • Stage 2, training the reward model.
    • Stage 3, optimizing the policy against the reward model.
    • All three — the whole pipeline is one RL algorithm.
    Answer: C. Stage 1 is imitation from fixed targets. Stage 2 is a classification problem on a fixed dataset of pairs — it has a loss and labels and nothing else. Neither has an agent whose choices change what data it sees next, which was Lesson 1's second defining feature. Only in Stage 3 does the model generate its own samples, get scored, and update — which is why PPO from Lesson 14 appears here and nowhere else in the pipeline.

    The leash: KL and reward hacking

    Stage 3 hands the policy a function to maximize and lets it loose. You already know how this ends, because Lesson 3 told you: an agent given a stated reward maximizes the stated reward, not your intent. The boat spinning in a loop for bonus pickups instead of finishing the race. The water-pouring robot from Lesson 1 that tips the glass back into the bottle and pours again forever.

    The reward model is a far richer target than either of those, but it is still a stated reward — a network trained on a finite pile of comparisons, with soft spots where it returns a high number for reasons unrelated to any human being pleased. A policy allowed to search freely will find them. Left unchecked, models drift toward text that is repetitive, oddly flattering, stuffed with hedges, or in bad cases barely English, and the score climbs the whole way. This is reward hacking: the same failure as the robot with the glass, on a much larger machine.

    The fix is to refuse to let the policy travel far. Keep a frozen copy of the Stage 1 model — the reference model — and subtract a penalty that grows as the policy's token probabilities diverge from it.

    The KL-regularized RLHF objective
    J(θ) = 𝔼x,y[ rφ(x, y) ] − β · KL( πθ ‖ πref )
    J(θ) The quantity being maximized — the same J from Lesson 5's objective and Lesson 12's policy gradient, with two terms now instead of one.
    θ The language model's parameters. The only thing in this equation that changes during training.
    𝔼x,y[ … ] Average over prompts x drawn from a prompt set, and responses y sampled from the current policy. Sampled, not fixed — this is what makes Stage 3 online RL.
    rφ(x, y) The reward model's score for the finished response. It is frozen here: Stage 2 trained it, Stage 3 only reads it.
    β · Beta, how hard the leash pulls; the dot after it is ordinary multiplication. Typically 0.01 to 0.05. Set β to 0 and there is no leash at all; set it very large and the policy refuses to move.
    KL(‖) The KL divergence: a number that is 0 when two probability distributions are identical and grows as they disagree. It is never negative. The double bar just separates the two things being compared — it is not division. Here it measures how differently the two models spread probability over the next token.
    πθ The policy being trained — the model you are trying to improve.
    πref The reference model: the Stage 1 model, frozen forever. It never learns anything. It exists only to be compared against.
    − A minus, because this is a penalty. Drifting is charged against whatever reward the drifting earned.

    Some arithmetic to make β concrete. Say the reference gives the token "sure" probability 0.30 somewhere and the tuned policy has pushed it to 0.90. That one token contributes log(0.90 / 0.30) = log 3 ≈ 1.1 to the KL. Reward scores span only a few units end to end, so with β = 0.02 a divergence of 10 costs 0.2 of reward — cheap, if it buys real improvement. A divergence of 200 costs 4, more than the reward model can ever pay back. The policy is free to change its mind; it is not free to become a different model.

    Figure 15.4 — Two different measures of the same training run. Early on they agree and both rise, which is the shaded band where you want to live. Past the dashed line they come apart: the amber curve, the only one you can actually see during training, keeps going up, while the green curve — what a person would say if you asked them — turns over. Nothing announces the turn. That is what makes it dangerous, and it is why β is not an optional refinement.

    The one thing to keep

    RLHF is not a new algorithm. It is PPO from Lesson 14, on an environment where the action is a token, the reward comes from a network trained to imitate human comparisons, and a KL penalty stops the policy exploiting that network's blind spots.

    Check yourself

    You set β = 0 and train for a long time. The reward model's average score climbs from 0.3 to 4.8. What is the most likely explanation?

    • The policy has genuinely become that much more helpful.
    • The policy has found text the reward model scores highly and humans would not, with nothing holding it back.
    • The policy has overwritten the reward model's parameters.
    • With no KL term the score is pure noise and means nothing.
    Answer: B. Option A is the real trap, because a rising number is exactly what you would see in both the good case and the bad one — which is precisely why the number alone cannot tell you which you are in. That is Lesson 3's lesson: the agent optimizes what you wrote down. Option C is wrong because the reward model is frozen in Stage 3; φ never updates. Option D is wrong too — the score is real, it is just no longer measuring what you wanted it to.

    What this does not fix

    Three honest limitations, none of them a detail. First, whose preferences. A reward model compresses the judgments of a few hundred to a few thousand paid annotators working from a written rubric. "Helpful" ends up meaning "what that group, following those instructions, preferred". The model is not aligned to humanity; it is aligned to a labeling process, and the choices inside that process are mostly undocumented. Second, overoptimization. The reward model is a proxy, accurate near the responses it was trained on and increasingly guesswork away from them, so the harder you optimize it the less it tracks what it stands for. The KL penalty buys time inside the region where the proxy holds; it does not make the proxy true. Third, cost. Stage 3 keeps four models in play at once — policy, frozen reference, reward model, and PPO's critic — and the comparison data underneath goes stale as soon as the policy improves enough that the old pairs stop resembling what it writes. That last point is the entire motivation for Lesson 16.

    Vocabulary

    supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
    Training on written example answers so the model responds to instructions rather than just continuing text.
    reward model
    Reads a prompt and a response, returns one number standing in for how much a person would like it.
    reference model
    A frozen copy of the fine-tuned model, kept so the policy can be penalized for drifting away from it.
    KL divergence
    How different two probability distributions are. Zero when identical, never negative, larger the more they disagree.
    reward hacking
    Scoring highly on the stated reward while failing at what the reward was supposed to stand for.

    Think it through

    Stage 3 trains against a reward model rather than against real people. Name two things that go wrong because of that substitution, and one thing you could do about each.

    One. The reward model is only reliable near the responses it was trained on, and the policy's job is literally to move away from those. As it moves, the scores it collects mean less and less. Two partial fixes: the KL penalty, which keeps the policy near the reference and therefore near the region the comparisons came from; and running the whole pipeline in rounds — collect fresh comparisons on the new policy's outputs, retrain the reward model, optimize again. Real systems do the second one repeatedly.

    Two. The reward model encodes one specific group's taste, including their idea of what a good answer sounds like. If those annotators reward confident-sounding answers, the policy learns to sound confident whether or not it should. There is no technical fix. You can broaden and document who labels, write sharper rubrics, and measure separately for the things you care most about — but no single number represents everyone, and pretending otherwise is how the problem gets hidden rather than solved.

    What to remember

    1. A language model is an RL agent with new labels. State is the prompt plus the tokens so far, action is the next token, the policy is the model, an episode is one response, and the reward lands only at the end.
    2. Preferences exist because comparison is easier than composition. People judge two answers reliably and write ideal ones badly, so the training signal is built out of the thing they are good at.
    3. Three stages, one of them RL. Fine-tune on demonstrations, train a reward model on comparisons, then optimize the policy with PPO. Only the third stage is reinforcement learning.
    4. The reward model is a proxy, and proxies break under pressure. Optimize it hard enough and its score rises while quality falls — Lesson 3's reward hacking, at scale.
    5. The KL penalty is what makes it work at all. It anchors the policy to a frozen reference so the policy stays in the region where the reward model still means something.

    Lesson 16

    Beyond PPO: DPO and GRPO

    Lesson 15 gave you a working recipe for training a language model from human feedback. It also gave you four neural networks running at once and a drawer full of knobs. This lesson is about two ways the field cut that down, and they cut in opposite directions.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • Name the four models PPO-based RLHF keeps resident, and what each is for
    • Read the DPO loss and say in plain words what it does to a preference pair
    • Compute GRPO advantages by hand for a group of four responses
    • Say why GRPO suits math and code while a learned reward model suits taste

    Four models, and a lot of knobs

    Count the networks Lesson 15's pipeline holds in memory during the RL stage.

    The policy is the model you are training. The reference is a frozen copy from before RL began, so the KL penalty has something to measure drift against. The reward model scores finished responses. And the critic — the value function from Lesson 13 — estimates how good a half-finished response is, so a single number arriving at the end becomes a per-step advantage.

    Four networks. Three are usually the size of the model you intend to ship, and two are being trained. Then the knobs: the KL coefficient, the clip range ε, two learning rates, reward normalization, epochs per batch. Set the KL coefficient too high and the policy barely moves; too low and it drifts into text that scores well and reads badly.

    Both methods in this lesson begin from that complaint, and they take opposite routes out of it. DPO deletes the RL loop. No sampling, no reward model, no critic — just a loss computed on a fixed dataset of preference pairs. GRPO keeps the RL loop and deletes the critic. It still samples, still scores, still takes clipped policy-gradient steps; it simply gets its baseline from the samples instead of from a learned value function.

    Figure 16.1 — Read this as a subtraction. Dashed boxes marked with a cross are the ones the method removes. Notice what nobody deletes: the reference model survives all three, because both methods still need something fixed to measure the policy against. DPO and GRPO are not variants of each other — they cut from opposite ends of the same pipeline.

    Check yourself

    Which network does GRPO drop, and which does DPO drop?

    • GRPO drops the reward model; DPO drops the critic.
    • GRPO drops the critic; DPO drops both the reward model and the critic.
    • Both drop the reference model, since neither needs a KL penalty.
    • GRPO drops the reference model; DPO drops the reward model.
    Answer: B. GRPO is still a reinforcement learning algorithm — it samples, scores, and takes policy-gradient steps — so it needs something to produce rewards. What it drops is the learned value function, because the group's mean does the baseline's job. DPO is not a loop at all, so it needs neither a scorer nor a value function during training. Option C is the common wrong guess: the reference model is the one piece nobody throws away.

    DPO: the loop turns out to be optional

    Lesson 15 wrote down an objective: maximize reward, minus β times the KL divergence from the reference model. That objective has a known solution — not an approximation you reach by gradient steps, but an exact, closed-form one. The policy that maximizes it is the reference model with every response reweighted by the exponential of its reward, divided by a normalizing constant.

    Now read that backwards. If the optimal policy can be written in terms of the reward, the reward can be written in terms of the policy. Rearranging gives an expression for the reward that mentions no reward model at all — only the policy, the reference, and β. Take it as a stated result; the algebra is short but beside the point.

    The reward, rewritten using the policy
    rθ(x, y) = β log πθ(y | x)πref(y | x)
    rθ(x, y) The implicit reward the policy assigns to response y for prompt x. It is not produced by a reward model — it is read off the policy itself.
    x The prompt.
    y One complete response to that prompt. Not a token — the whole thing, which is one episode in the Lesson 15 mapping.
    πθ(y | x) The probability the model being trained gives to producing that entire response; θ is its parameters, the thing gradient descent adjusts, and in practice you compute its logarithm, which is the sum of the per-token log-probabilities.
    πref(y | x) The same quantity under the frozen reference model, the supervised-fine-tuned checkpoint you started from.
    log Natural logarithm. It turns the ratio into something that can be positive or negative: positive when the policy likes the response more than the reference does, negative when it likes it less.
    β The same KL strength knob as in Lesson 15. Large β keeps the policy tethered to the reference; small β lets it roam.

    That formula describes an optimum, not a training procedure. DPO's move is to treat it as a definition and substitute it into the preference loss from Lesson 15. The Bradley–Terry loss wanted the chosen response's score to exceed the rejected one's; put the expression above in for both scores and the reward model cancels out of the picture. What is left is a loss on the policy, computed straight from preference pairs.

    The DPO loss
    ℒDPO = − 𝔼pairs [ log σ ( rθ(x, yw) − rθ(x, yl) ) ]
    ℒDPO The number training drives down. Lower means the model orders the preference pairs more often the way the humans did.
    𝔼pairs Average over every preference pair in the dataset. Each pair is a prompt plus two responses with a human verdict attached.
    yw The chosen response — the one the human preferred. The w is for "winning"; it is a label, not a variable.
    yl The rejected response — the one passed over. The l is for "losing".
    rθ( … ) The implicit reward from the previous formula, evaluated on the chosen response and on the rejected one.
    σ The logistic function, which squashes any number into the range 0 to 1. Feed it the gap between the two implicit rewards and it returns the model's implied probability that the chosen response really is better.
    − log The minus sign in front turns "make that probability large" into "make this loss small". A probability of 1 gives a loss of 0; a probability near 0 gives a very large loss.

    Mechanically this is a two-way classifier. Gradient descent responds to it by raising the log-probability of the chosen response and lowering the log-probability of the rejected one, hardest on the pairs the model currently gets backwards. The reference terms are constants and never move. Their job is to make every push relative: a response the reference already found likely needs less of a shove, and β sets how far the policy may travel before the loss stops paying for it.

    Figure 16.2 — Bar height is the log-probability the model gives a whole response. The dashed line through each pair of bars is the frozen reference's level, and it is the only thing the loss measures against: DPO does not care whether a response is likely in absolute terms, only whether the policy has moved it up or down relative to where it started. Watch the two grey bars — they are identical, because the reference never trains.

    No sampling happens here. The model never generates a token during DPO training; everything it learns from was written before training started, by people or by some other model. That is the entire trade. DPO is offline, and cannot discover anything its dataset does not already contain.

    Where people get lost

    Watch the log-probability of the chosen responses during DPO training and you will often see it fall. That looks like a broken run. It usually is not. The loss only cares about the gap between chosen and rejected, and pushing the rejected one down faster widens the gap just as well as pushing the chosen one up. Both can decline while the loss steadily improves.

    The other thing to keep straight: DPO is not reinforcement learning with the middleman removed. There is no loop, no exploration, no environment, no advantage. It is supervised learning on pairs. The reinforcement learning lives entirely in where the loss came from.

    Check yourself

    Halfway through a DPO run, the average log-probability of the chosen responses is going down. What should you conclude?

    • The run is broken — the model is learning the preferences backwards.
    • Possibly nothing. The loss only measures the gap, and the gap can widen while both sides fall.
    • The reference model has drifted and needs to be refreshed.
    • β must be set too small, so the policy cannot move at all.
    Answer: B. Check the rejected responses before you panic: if they are falling faster, the model is doing exactly what the loss asked. Option A confuses the sign of one term with the sign of the difference. Option C is impossible — the reference is frozen by construction, which is the whole reason it can serve as a fixed yardstick. Option D has it backwards: it is a large β that restrains the policy, and restraint would hold the log-probabilities near the reference's rather than making them slide.

    GRPO: the critic was the expensive part

    Now the other route. Keep the loop, and ask what the critic was ever for.

    Lesson 12 showed that raw returns make poor learning signals because they push every action the same way; an action has to be compared against average. Lesson 13 named that comparison the advantage and computed it with a learned value function. For a language model that value function is another network the size of the policy, trained from a reward arriving exactly once, at the end of a response a thousand tokens long. It is the least reliable piece of the pipeline, and it doubles your training memory.

    GRPO's move is almost rude in its simplicity. You want to know whether a response is better than average, so generate several responses to the same prompt, score all of them, and let their mean be "average". That is your baseline. No critic required.

    The group-relative advantage
    Ai = ri − μσ ,    μ = 1G Σj=1G rj
    Ai The advantage given to response i. Positive means "do more of this", negative means "do less" — the same reading of the sign as in Lesson 13.
    i The group index: which of the sampled responses this is. It runs from 1 to G. Every response in the group answers the same prompt.
    j A second group index, used only as the counter inside the sum so it does not collide with i. It also runs from 1 to G.
    G The group size — how many responses you sample for one prompt. Four in the example below; real runs use something like 8 to 64.
    ri The reward for response i: one number for the finished response, from a reward model or from an automatic checker.
    μ The group mean reward — the baseline. This is the quantity a critic used to predict, now simply measured.
    σ The standard deviation of the G rewards. Careful: this is the ordinary spread of a set of numbers, not the logistic function that wore the same letter in the DPO loss above.
    Σ Add up the term to its right for j = 1, 2, … G. Combined with the 1/G in front, that is just an average.

    Dividing by the group's standard deviation turns the advantages into z-scores, so a group scored out of 10 and a group scored out of 1 push with comparable force. It has one sharp edge: if every response in the group scores the same, σ is zero and the division is undefined. Implementations add a small constant to the denominator, and people drop prompts where the whole group agrees, since such a group carries no information anyway.

    Worked example

    One prompt, four sampled responses. The scorer gives them 8, 6, 2, and 4.

    1. Mean: (8 + 6 + 2 + 4) ÷ 4 = 20 ÷ 4 = 5.
    2. Subtract the mean from each reward: +3, +1, −3, −1. Those are the raw advantages, and they sum to zero, which is always a good check.
    3. Square them: 9, 1, 9, 1. Add: 20. Divide by the group size 4: 5. Square root: σ = √5 ≈ 2.236.
    4. Divide each raw advantage by σ: +1.34, +0.45, −1.34, −0.45.

    Response 1 gets pushed up hardest, response 3 gets pushed down hardest, and responses 2 and 4 barely move. Nothing in those four steps needed a value function, a reward model's calibration, or a second training loop. It needed four samples and a mean.

    The GRPO objective, simplest form
    JGRPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ 1G Σi=1G min( ρiAi , clip(ρi, 1−ε, 1+ε) Ai ) ] − β DKL(πθ ‖ πref)
    JGRPO(θ) The quantity you climb by adjusting the parameters θ. Bigger is better, so this is gradient ascent.
    𝔼 Average over prompts, each of which contributes one group.
    G, i Group size and group index again. The 1/G with the sum averages over the G responses to one prompt.
    ρi The ratio from Lesson 14: how much more likely the current policy makes response i than the policy that generated it did. Lesson 14 wrote it r(θ); here it gets a new letter because r is already busy being the reward.
    Ai The group-relative advantage from the previous formula — the only place a critic used to be needed.
    min, clip PPO's safety catch, unchanged. clip pins the ratio inside the allowed band, and taking the smaller of the two products means an update is never rewarded for overshooting.
    ε The clip range, typically 0.2, so the ratio is held between 0.8 and 1.2.
    β How hard to penalize drift from the reference. Same letter and same job as in the DPO loss, though here it multiplies an explicit KL term rather than hiding inside a reward.
    DKL The KL divergence from Lesson 15: a measure of how far the trained policy's output distribution has moved from the frozen reference's. The double bar simply separates the two distributions being compared.
    πθ, πref The policy being trained and the frozen reference.

    Check yourself

    You sample a group of four responses and the checker scores all four as correct. What does GRPO learn from that prompt?

    • It pushes all four up, since all four were correct.
    • Nothing — every advantage is zero, because every reward equals the group mean.
    • It pushes all four down, since nothing distinguishes them.
    • It learns from the first sampled response and discards the rest.
    Answer: B. The advantage is a reward minus the group's mean. If all four rewards are equal the mean equals every one of them, all four raw advantages are 0, and the whole term vanishes — and the standard deviation is 0 as well, so the division needs guarding. This is a property, not a bug: GRPO learns from disagreement inside a group. A prompt every sample gets right, or every sample gets wrong, teaches nothing, which is exactly why training sets get filtered for prompts of the right difficulty. Option A is the tempting one — being correct feels like it deserves reward, but there is no baseline here to be better than.

    Why math and code are the natural home

    Look again at ri in the advantage formula. Nothing about it requires a learned model. It can come from a checker: run the generated program against unit tests, or compare a math solution's final number against the known answer. The reward is 1 or 0, or the fraction of tests passed, and no network produced it.

    That substitution is larger than it looks. Lesson 3's boat spun in a loop collecting pickups instead of finishing the race; Lesson 15's policy found the reward model's blind spots and drifted into text that scored beautifully and read like nothing. Both failures have one cause: the number being maximized is a proxy, and pushing hard on a proxy finds exactly where it and the goal disagree. A checker is not a proxy. "The tests pass" is not a model's estimate of whether the tests pass; it is whether the tests pass. Swap the reward model for a checker and that entire reward-hacking surface is gone, because no learned approximation is left between the policy and the goal. The KL penalty stays, but its job narrows to keeping the prose from degrading while the model chases correctness.

    Two honest limits. The checker has to be airtight — weak tests can be satisfied without solving the problem, and then you are hacking a hand-written proxy instead of a learned one. And it only works where correctness is mechanically checkable: nothing will unit-test whether a reply was kind. For that, a learned reward model is still the only instrument.

    This is why GRPO is the method most associated with training reasoning models. A long chain of thought is painful to judge step by step and trivial to grade at the end: nobody wants to score the intermediate work, but anybody can check the final answer. Sample a group of attempts, mark them, push up whatever beat the mean, repeat. The reasoning appears as a side effect of being rewarded for arriving.

    Check yourself

    Why does replacing a learned reward model with an automatic checker reduce reward hacking?

    • Checkers are much faster, so training finishes before hacking has time to appear.
    • The checker measures the thing you care about directly rather than approximating it, so there is no gap between proxy and goal to exploit.
    • Checkers give a reward on every token instead of only at the end.
    • With a checker you no longer need a KL penalty at all.
    Answer: B. Reward hacking happens when a policy optimizes a proxy hard enough to find where the proxy and the goal come apart. A learned reward model is a proxy by construction — a network's guess at human judgment, blind spots included. Option C has it backwards: a checker's reward is if anything sparser, arriving only once the response is complete. Option D is wrong too — you still want the KL penalty, just for a different reason, to stop the model's language degrading while it optimizes for correct answers.

    The one thing to keep

    DPO and GRPO are both subtractions from Lesson 15's pipeline, made at opposite ends. DPO removes the loop and keeps the preference signal. GRPO keeps the loop and replaces the learned baseline with a measured one.

    Think it through

    You have 50,000 preference pairs on customer-support replies, a modest GPU budget, and no way to grade a reply automatically. Which of the three methods would you reach for, and what are you giving up?

    DPO. You already hold exactly the input it wants, it needs two models rather than four, and there is nothing for GRPO to work with — with no automatic grader you would have to train a reward model first, at which point you have paid most of PPO's cost without collecting its benefit.

    What you give up is everything that comes from the model generating its own data. DPO can only learn the orderings inside the 50,000 pairs you have. If those replies were written by a weaker model, DPO will teach yours to prefer the better of two mediocre answers rather than to write a good one. It cannot find a reply nobody in the dataset thought to write, because it never writes anything during training.

    The honest follow-up: this is often fine. If your preference data sits close in quality to what you want the model to produce, offline is enough. It stops being enough when you want the model to become better than its data — which is precisely the situation reasoning models are in, and precisely why they use a method that samples.

    Sixteen lessons, one question

    Lesson 1 said reinforcement learning is what you reach for when you can score an outcome but cannot demonstrate the right answer. Lesson 2 drew the loop. Lesson 3 said the thing worth chasing is not the next reward but the discounted return, and warned that you get exactly what you measure. Part II summarized the future as a value that obeys a recursion. Part III showed how to learn it from experience alone, one step at a time, from a TD error. Lesson 11 swapped the table for a network so the idea could scale.

    Then Part IV turned the problem around: learn the behavior directly, and subtract a baseline so an action is judged against average rather than against zero. That comparison got its name in Lesson 13 — the advantage — and PPO wrapped it in a clip so no one update could wreck the policy. Part V pointed the whole apparatus at a language model.

    And here at the end, the advantage is still the advantage. GRPO computes it from four samples instead of a critic. DPO drops the loop but keeps the preference signal that motivated it. Nothing new was invented in this lesson. Two pieces were removed.

    What to remember

    1. PPO-based RLHF holds four models. Policy, frozen reference, reward model, critic — and each one is another thing to fund and tune.
    2. DPO deletes the RL loop. The closed-form link between the optimal KL-regularized policy and the reward lets you rewrite the reward using the policy itself, collapsing the pipeline into one classification-style loss on preference pairs. Cheap and stable, but offline.
    3. GRPO deletes the critic. Sample a group of responses to one prompt and use the group's mean as the baseline; the advantage is each response's z-score within its group. Rewards 8, 6, 2, 4 give a mean of 5, raw advantages +3, +1, −3, −1, and scaled advantages +1.34, +0.45, −1.34, −0.45.
    4. A checker removes the proxy. When correctness can be graded automatically there is no learned reward model left to hack, which is why GRPO suits math, code, and reasoning.
    5. The advantage never went away. Everything after Lesson 12 is a different way of asking how much better than average an action was, and a different way of limiting how far you move in response.
